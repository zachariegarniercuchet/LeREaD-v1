The Court:

 

Overview

 

[1]               This appeal arises out of legislative changes in Nunavut regarding the government’s obligation to provide instruction in the Inuit language in the Nunavut public school system.

[2]               With the creation of Nunavut, education came under the jurisdiction of the Nunavut legislature: Nunavut Act, SC 1993, c 28, s 23. Nunavut’s Education Act, S.Nu. 2008, c 15, s 23 provides that:

Every student shall be given a bilingual education and the languages of instruction shall be Inuit Language and either English or French as determined by a district education authority with respect to the schools under its jurisdiction.

[3]                Through its Education Act and the Inuit Language Protection Act, S.Nu. 2008, c 17, the legislature established a framework that contemplated children would receive Inuit language instruction from kindergarten to grade 3 commencing July 1, 2009. By July 1, 2019, all schools were to provide fully bilingual instruction from kindergarten to grade 12. However, by 2019 Inuit language instruction was still only available from kindergarten to grade 3.

[4]               In 2019, the legislation was amended[1] to provide for a phased-in delivery over 20 years of Inuit Language Arts, defined as “a class or course that is focused on teaching the Inuit Language”. The requirement to provide bilingual education for all students from kindergarten to Grade 12 was postponed by 20 years, from July 1, 2019 to July 1, 2039 (the “2019 Amendments”).

[5]               Two Inuit mothers with school-aged children and Nunavut Tunngavik Incorporated, a non-profit that represents the interests of Inuit in Nunavut, (collectively referred to as NTI), commenced an action against the Government of Nunavut (GN). They allege that the 2019 Amendments infringe their right to equal benefit of the law without discrimination under s 15(1) of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms (Charter). They claim the 2019 Amendments effectively remove the requirement for bilingual Inuit education, and that this results in multiple negative impacts on Inuit, including the loss of Indigenous language and culture. The claim pleads adverse effects discrimination because of the disproportionate impact of the 2019 Amendments on Inuit and that the 2019 Amendments reinforce and perpetuate historical disadvantage suffered by Inuit. It is grounded in the interconnectedness between racial and linguistic identity.

[6]               The GN applied to strike the claim on the grounds that it disclosed no reasonable cause of action. The application was dismissed by a chambers judge: Nunavut Tunngavik Incorporated et al v The Commissioner of Nunavut et al, 2023 NUCJ 5. The GN appeals that decision.

[7]               The appeal is dismissed for the reasons that follow.

Chambers Decision

[8]               The GN’s application to strike the claim as disclosing no reasonable cause of action was brought pursuant to section 129(1) of the Rules of Court[2]. The test to be applied in considering such applications, as noted by the chambers judge, was explained in Atlantic Lottery Corp Inc v Babstock, 2020 SCC 19 at para 14:

... whether it is plain and obvious, assuming the facts pleaded to be true, that each of the plaintiffs’ pleaded claims disclose no reasonable cause of action. Simply stated, if a claim has no reasonable prospect of success it should not be allowed to proceed to trial.

[9]               The chambers judge stated that whether the application to strike succeeds depends on the ambit of section 15 of the Charter, and referred to the two-part test to determine whether an impugned law infringes section 15(1) set out in Ontario (Attorney General) v G, 2020 SCC 38, [2020] 3 SCR 629 [Ontario] at para 40:

The Court asks two questions in determining whether a law infringes s 15(1). First, does the challenged law, on its face or in its impact, draw a distinction based on an enumerated or analogous ground? If a law is facially neutral, it may draw a distinction indirectly where it has an adverse impact upon members of a protected group. Second, if it does draw a distinction, does it impose “burdens or [deny] a benefit in a manner that has the effect of reinforcing, perpetuating or exacerbating . . . disadvantage”, including “historical” disadvantage?

[10]           The chambers judge noted that “the Respondents claim that the 2019 Amendments create adverse effects discrimination on Inuit based on race and ethnicity.” NTI pleads that by removing the requirement for public education to be delivered in the Inuit language after grade 3, multiple adverse impacts are experienced by the Inuit as a people, including cultural, economic and social political disadvantage, and reduced educational attainment for Inuit children. The Amended Statement of Claim expressly asserts that the 2019 Amendment violates the norms of substantive equality, denies all Inuit in Nunavut equal protection and benefit of the law, and discriminates based on the enumerated grounds of race and ethnic origin.

[11]           After reviewing Supreme Court jurisprudence on the identification of “analogous grounds” for the purpose of section 15(1), the chambers judge recognized that the possibility of language being viewed as an analogous ground was acknowledged in Gosselin (Tutor of) v Quebec (Attorney General), 2005 SCC 15 at para 12:

Section 15 (1) of the Canadian Charter does not expressly enumerate language as a prohibited ground of discrimination. However, we agree with the observations of the Saskatchewan Court of Appeal in Reference re Use of French in Criminal Proceedings in Saskatchewan (1987), 1987 CanLII 204 (SK CA), 36 C.C.C. (3d) 353, at p. 373, that:

 

Nor, in our view, does the presence in the Charter of the language provisions of ss. 16 to 20, or the deletion from an earlier draft of s. 15(1) of the word “language”, have the effect necessarily of excluding from the reach of s. 15 the form of distinction at issue in this case.

[12]           The chambers judge concluded that the Supreme Court “has left the door open, arguably, for the Respondents to assert that the provision of Inuit language education from kindergarten to grade 12 is an analogous ground protected against discrimination by Charter section 15(1).”: para 27.

[13]           The chambers judge then turned to consider the 2019 Amendments within the context of the Ontario test. First, he found that the 2019 Amendments create a distinction, holding that, “taken at face value, the 2019 Amendments [apply] explicitly to, and will have a direct impact upon, Inuit children and their ability to study in their mother tongue or the language of their immediate ancestors” (para 29).

[14]           With respect to the second part of the Ontario test, the chambers judge reviewed the factual assertions in NTI’s claim, and concluded that the 2019 Amendments arguably impose a burden upon, or deny benefits to, Inuit by contributing to Inuit youth “losing their language and their connection to Inuit culture”, and that the effect of the 2019 Amendments may be to “perpetuate the undeniably historical disadvantages experienced by Inuit from colonialism” (para 31).

[15]           The chambers judge confirmed that NTI invoked section 15(1) alleging that the 2019 Amendments “discriminate against Inuit” and that “race and ethnic origin [are] two of the explicitly prohibited forms of discrimination.” He then observed: “The Applicants, on the other hand, assert that the claim pertains to language rights.” After recognizing the competing characterizations of the claim, the chambers judge concluded, “the law has yet to be settled whether language in some circumstances may be an analogous ground under Charter section 15(1). It is not plain and obvious that the Respondents’ claim would fail, however it is characterised”: (para 32). He concluded the action must proceed to trial and dismissed the application to strike.

Issues on Appeal

[16]           The GN advances the following grounds of appeal:

1.      The chambers judge misapplied the test on an application to strike by refusing to dismiss the action simply because it advanced a novel claim; and

2.      The chambers judge failed to answer the threshold legal question: whether a right to language instruction can be protected by section 15(1) of the Charter.

Standard of Review

[17]           Whether a pleading discloses a cause of action raises a question of law, reviewable for correctness. “However, the decision to strike a statement of claim is discretionary and is reviewable on a standard of reasonableness: Nammo v Canada (Justice and Attorney General), 2015 ABCA 389 at para 7, 609 AR 189; Ernst v Alberta (Energy Resources Conservation Board), 2014 ABCA 285 at para 11, 580 AR 341...”: Jane Doe v Government of Nunavut, 2021 NUCA 15 at para 15.

Analysis

Did the chambers judge misapply the test on an application to strike?

[18]           The GN acknowledges that the chambers judge articulated the correct legal test for an application to strike, but submits he misapplied that test because he exercised too much judicial reticence and refused to dismiss the action simply because a novel claim is advanced.

[19]           In its written submissions, the GN argued that the chambers judge applied what the GN described as “the now-defunct approach of assessing whether the plaintiffs’ claim is ‘arguable’ or ‘not doomed to fail’”. In oral submissions, the GN argued that the chambers judge failed to apply the robust approach from Atlantic Lottery when evaluating a pure question of law, where the threshold legal question has been thoroughly canvassed by the Supreme Court of Canada and other courts, the necessary legal matrix is present, and the issue is ripe for determination.

[20]           In our view, Atlantic Lottery did not change the test to be applied on applications to strike. Rather, it encouraged courts to be bold in applying the test, and in particular, provided guidance on the approach to be adopted for novel claims. The Court said, at para 19:

Of course, it is not determinative on a motion to strike that the law has not yet recognized the particular claim. The law is not static, and novel claims that might represent an incremental development in the law should be allowed to proceed to trial (Imperial Tobacco, at para. 21; Das v. George Weston Ltd., 2018 ONCA 1053, 43 E.T.R. (4th) 173, at para. 73; see also R. v. Salituro, 1991 CanLII 17 (SCC), [1991] 3 S.C.R. 654, at p. 670). That said, a claim will not survive an application to strike simply because it is novel. It is beneficial, and indeed critical to the viability of civil justice and public access thereto that claims, including novel claims, which are doomed to fail be disposed of at an early stage in the proceedings. This is because such claims present “no legal justification for a protracted and expensive trial” (Syl Apps Secure Treatment Centre v. B.D., 2007 SCC 38, [2007] 3 S.C.R. 83, at para. 19). If a court would not recognize a novel claim when the facts as pleaded are taken to be true, the claim is plainly doomed to fail and should be struck. In making this determination, it is not uncommon for courts to resolve complex questions of law and policy (see e.g. Imperial Tobacco; Cooper v. Hobart, 2001 SCC 79, [2001] 3 S.C.R. 537; Syl Apps; Alberta v. Elder Advocates of Alberta Society, 2011 SCC 24, [2011] 2 S.C.R. 261).   

 (emphasis added)

[21]           The suggestion that the phrase “doomed to fail” following Atlantic Lottery is now defunct is not sustainable. The phrase “doomed to fail” was used by both the majority in that case (on two occasions at para 19, highlighted above, and at para 59) and by the minority (at para 84).

[22]           While it is possible on an application to strike to determine a novel claim is doomed to fail based on the facts in the pleadings, “novel claims that might represent an incremental development in the law should be allowed to proceed to trial” (Atlantic Lottery, para 19). This is particularly the case when a claim involves Charter or other constitutional challenges, where the importance of a proper factual foundation is well recognized: see Manitoba (A.G.) v Metropolitan Stores Ltd, [1987] 1 SCR 110 at paras 50-51; MacKay v Manitoba, [1989] 2 SCR 357; Danson v Ontario (Attorney General), [1990] 2 SCR 1086. Moreover, caution should be exercised in striking claims that explore the scope of a Charter right: Leroux v Ontario, 2023 ONCA 314 at para 86.

[23]           The chambers judge was sensitive to the nuances within the test, and the need to balance competing interests, noting at paragraph 11 of his reasons:

Public resources are not infinite, and limited court resources should not be squandered on actions which are frivolous or destined to failure. At the same time, however, courts must be careful not to dismiss an action merely because the claim is a novel one. As we shall see in a moment, the reach of Charter section 15(1) itself since its inception has been extended by the Supreme Court of Canada. Thus, in cases where the law is unsettled, and it is not plain and obvious that the claim will fail, the claim must be permitted to go to trial.

[24]           We find no error in the chambers judge’s application of the test. He correctly examined whether it is plain and obvious that the pleaded claims have no reasonable prospect of success and therefore disclose no reasonable cause of action. He considered whether finite resources would be wasted, whether the claim as pleaded satisfied the elements of a section 15(1) action, the scope of the Charter right to substantive equality as described in the existing jurisprudence, and the caution to be exercised in striking a Charter claim. As explained below, he also correctly recognized the unsettled state of the law about the scope of the section 15(1) Charter right.

Did the chambers judge fail to answer the threshold question under s 15(1)?

[25]           The GN submits that the chambers judge failed to answer what the GN characterizes as the threshold legal question: whether language instruction rights can be protected by section 15(1) of the Charter. In particular, the GN submits that Gosselin and Lalonde v Ontario (Commission de restructuration des services de santé) (2001), 56 OR (3d) 505, 208 DLR (4th) 577 (Ont CA) do not permit section 15(1) of the Charter to be used to advance a language right because sections 16 to 23 of the Charter provide a complete code with respect to language rights under the Charter. As the GN notes, those provisions relate to the status and protection of English and French in public institutions, the availability of public services in those languages, and a right to English or French minority language schooling for certain defined classes of individuals and under certain defined circumstances. While the claim is expressly framed under the enumerated grounds of race and ethnicity, the Court should look at the “real essence” or “essential character” of the claim – language of educational instruction – and conclude that no such analogous ground is available under section 15(1) and therefore no cause of action is disclosed.

[26]           NTI rejects the threshold legal question proposed by the GN and the proposition that the Court should look beyond the plain wording of the pleading to recharacterize the claim on an analogous ground. The Amended Statement of Claim expressly alleges that the 2019 Amendments discriminate against all Inuit in Nunavut “on the bases of their race and ethnic origin and deny them the equal protection and benefit of the law” (para 63). The inequality “reflects and reinforces historic discriminatory and assimilative practices” and “perpetrates negative stereotypes and messages about the Inuit and their language” (para 67). Unlike other students in Canada, Inuit students in Nunavut are “deprived of the ability to receive education in the locally dominant official language, which is also the first language of most Inuit in Nunavut” (para 64). This inequality is “imposed on Inuit of Nunavut by reason of their race and ethnic origin, which are personal characteristics listed as enumerated grounds of discrimination under subsection 15(1)” (para 68). The Amended Statement of Claim therefore frames a cause of action under section 15(1) based on enumerated grounds and the pleaded facts support the essential elements of that claim.

[27]           The GN’s arguments on this ground of appeal are not persuasive for two reasons.

[28]           First, we reject the GN’s submission that NTI’s claim must be assessed for its “real essence” or “essential character”, and characterized as an affirmative language rights claim in disguise. In making that argument, the GN relies on JP Morgan Asset Management (Canada) Inc v Canada (National Revenue), 2013 FCA 250 at paras 49-50; Canada (Attorney General) v Scow, 2022 BCCA 275 at para 32; Leroux v Canada Revenue Agency, 2012 BCCA 63 at para 20; Grand River Enterprises Six Nations Ltd v Attorney General (Canada), 2017 ONCA 526 at para 46.

[29]           The “essential character” inquiry is not a component of the test for determining whether a reasonable cause of cause of action is disclosed. The cited cases involved circumstances where a court’s subject matter jurisdiction was challenged, and the essential nature of the claim had to be ascertained to establish whether the court had jurisdiction. On an application to strike for no reasonable cause of action, the Court focuses on the facts as pleaded, not a substantive inquiry into the merits. Moreover, in assessing alternative approaches to the cause of action, the Court considers whether “the pleadings are bound to fail on all reasonable theories of the case”: Nevsun Resources Ltd v Araya, 2020 SCC 5 at para 147 (per Brown and Rowe JJ, dissenting in part but not on this point). A violation of the section 15(1) substantive equality right is expressly pleaded on enumerated grounds with sufficient material facts to support the essential elements of an arguable cause of action.

[30]           Second, the law addressing the scope of the section 15(1) substantive equality right and the interaction with sections 16-23 of the Charter is not sufficiently settled to preclude a reasonable claim grounded on racial and ethnic discrimination merely because those enumerated grounds are interconnected with the language of educational instruction.

[31]           As NTI contends, the claim arguably does not relate to English or French, but to the Inuit language, and does not involve minority language rights, as the Inuit language is spoken by the majority in Nunavut. Moreover, there is no hierarchy among constitutional rights - one cannot be used to expand or circumscribe another.

[32]           We recognize that some claims under section 15(1) of the Charter can be appropriately addressed on a preliminary basis, such as determining that section 15 rights are not available to corporations: see Edmonton Journal v Alberta (Attorney General), [1989] 2 SCR 1326 at 1382. However, we agree with NTI that the law is not as clear as suggested by the GN and that this claim should proceed to trial.

[33]            Neither Gosselin nor LaLonde clearly precludes the claim advanced by NTI. Gosselin confirmed that equality guarantees in section 15 cannot be used to modify the categories of rights holder defined under section 23 (para 22), but it did not determine whether section 23 can be used to constrain the interpretation and application of section 15 equality rights to exclude other linguistic groups. LaLonde recognized that “where the Constitution accords special rights to special groups, those specific guarantees must be respected and other Charter rights cannot be used to expand or diminish the rights so granted” (para 101). While section 23 accords special rights to special groups of English and French minorities, Lalonde does not resolve whether section 23 precludes other linguistic groups, including Indigenous majorities, from seeking substantive equality under section 15.

[34]            In our view, whether NTI’s claim is limited to protecting language rights (as characterized by the GN) or is broader in nature (as alleged in the Amended Statement of Claim), how that claim should be assessed in relation to section 15(1) of the Charter, and how section 15 interacts with sections 16 – 23 of the Charter are complex questions of law that should be addressed in the context of a proper evidentiary record and concrete factual findings. Further, NTI’s expressly pleaded claim of racial and ethnic discrimination causing substantive inequality requires a fulsome evidentiary record to examine how the 2019 Amendments impose a burden or deny a benefit in a way that reinforces, perpetuates or exacerbates disadvantage including historical disadvantage. We decline to undertake that analysis in the abstract.

Conclusion

[35]           The appeal is dismissed.
