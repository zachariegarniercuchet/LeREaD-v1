                                       REASONS FOR JUDGMENT

 

Hall, J:

 

Background

 

[1]              The parties to this application are parties to a Collective Agreement (the “Collective Agreement”).  The present application arrises out of a grievance filed by the Respondent Union grieving that a non-union contractor was harvesting wood on the timber limits of the Applicant Company in violation of Article 3 and other Articles of the Collective Agreement.  The grievance was ultimately submitted to arbitration pursuant to the Collective Agreement.  Under the Collective Agreement grievances referred to arbitration are to heard by a three-person Board of Arbitration with the Employer and the Union each selecting one nominee and those two nominees selecting a third person who will act as chairman.

 

[2]              Clause 13:07 of the Collective Agreement sets out the jurisdiction of the Board of Arbitration as follows:

 

“It is understood that the function of the Arbitration Board shall be to interpret and apply this Agreement, and that it shall deal only with the specific questions as submitted and shall have no power to alter, add to, or amend this Agreement, except arbitrations under Article IV.”

 

[3]              In April 2003 the Union appointed its nominee to the Arbitration Board and in June 2003 the Employer selected as its nominee Mr. Ronald D. McKelvie.  The Union made no objection to the Employer selection of its nominee and these two nominees agreed upon the appointment of the Chairperson of the Arbitration Board.  The Chairperson then entered into correspondence with the parties and a date for the hearing of the arbitration was set as December 5, 2003.  On November 28, 2003 the Union’s legal counsel wrote to the Employer’s legal counsel to advise that the Union would be raising a preliminary objection to the composition of the Board of Arbitration on the basis of a reasonable apprehension of bias with respect to the Employer’s nominee on the Board.

 

[4]              The Arbitration Board convened on the agreed date, at which time the Union raised before the Arbitration Board its preliminary objection to the Employer’s nominee.  The Employer responded that the Board of Arbitration lacked jurisdiction to consider the preliminary objection as the Board was already constituted and that, in any event, there was no reasonable apprehension of bias.  The Board of Arbitration heard evidence and argument on the Union’s preliminary object and reserved its decision which it published on July 29, 2004.  In the decision the Employer nominee dissented.  The majority upheld the preliminary objection, concluding as follows:

 

“The Board has jurisdiction to decide a question of bias.  The Union’s objection to the Employer nominee, Ron McKelvie, on the grounds of reasonable apprehension of bias, is allowed.  The Employer nominee is disqualified from continuing as a member of the Arbitration Board.  The Board is no longer properly constituted and lacks jurisdiction to hear the merits of the grievance.  To process the grievance to arbitration, the parties are required to appoint a new Arbitration Board.”

 

[5]              The Employer brought this present application to have the Award of the Arbitration Board set aside on two bases as follows:

 

(1)     The Board of Arbitration lacked jurisdiction to rule on its own composition; and

 

(2)     In the alternative, there was no reasonable apprehension of bias on the part of Ronald McKelvie, the Employer’s nominee.

 

[6]              In its reply memorandum, the Respondent Union took the following positions.

 

(1)     That there was jurisdiction in the Arbitration Board as constituted to decide whether there was a reasonable apprehension of bias on the part of the Employee nominee , Mr. Ronald McKelvie; and

 

(2)     That the majority of the Arbitration Board was correct in its decision that there was a reasonable apprehension of bias on the part of Mr. McKelvie in the circumstances of the case.

Arbitration Board’s Hearing of Preliminary Issue.

 

[7]              At the hearing of the preliminary issue the Union called as witnesses Mr. Ron Smith, the National Representative for the Union, and Mr. Lindy Vincent, the Secretary/Treasurer of the Local.  The Employer called Kevin Dawe who was the Director of Industrial Relations for Newfoundland woodlands and William Furey, the present woodlands’ Manager.  At p. 21 of the Arbitration Award the majority of the Arbitration Board sets out its findings of fact with respect to the alleged apprehension of bias on the part of Mr. McKelvie.  It states:

 

“The Board has considered the facts in the present case as determined from the evidence presented.  Ron McKelvie is the former manager of the Woodlands Division for the Employer.  The employees in the bargaining unit work for the Woodlands Division.  Most of the current employees were also employees when Mr. McKelvie was the manager.  Mr McKelvie retired in about 1991 and has not been an employee of the Employer for about 13 years.  Mr. McKelvie was active in the negotiation of several successive collective agreements.  He signed seven successive collective agreements on behalf of the Employer.  The last collective agreement signed by Mr. McKelvie was the 1989 to 1992 collective agreement.  In that collective agreement the language that is the subject of the current dispute was first introduced.  The 1989 to 1992 collective agreement was also the first collective agreement entered into between the Employer and the current bargaining agent.  Mr. McKelvie participated in those negotiations and he signed the 1989 to 1992 collective agreement.  Recently, Mr. McKelvie was appointed as one of two persons on a fact finding board.  Mr. McKelvie was appointed by Abitibi Consolidated Inc. and Corner Brook Pulp & Paper Limited appointed one of its retired employees.  The board was asked to report on a dispute between the two companies concerning exchanges of timber cutting rights.  The work of the fact finding board was completed in November and December, 2003.  The work is now completed and Mr. McKelvie is no longer working int hat capacity.  The report was not accepted by Abitibi Consolidated Inc.”

 

 

[8]              Against this factual background the majority of the Arbitration Board at p. 22 concluded as follows:

 

“The issue of bias concerns the perception of independence from the Employer.  The Board has considered whether a reasonably informed bystander would be satisfied with the independence of Mr. McKelvie to hear the case in these circumstances.  The Board notes that a former employee, or a current employee, does not necessarily lack independence.  However, lack of independence arises on the facts of this case by reason of the fact that Mr. McKelvie was one of the negotiators of the language in Article 3 of the Collective Agreement that is the subject matter of the current dispute.  The lack of independence in these circumstances arises from the fact that an objective bystander would reasonably conclude that Mr. McKelvie would have personal knowledge as to the intention of the Employer when the language was negotiated.  Therefore, his decision might reasonably be influenced by his personal knowledge of the factual background of the case.  Further, Mr. McKelvie has recently served as a fact finding board member for the Employer and in that respect his situation is analogous to a solicitor who has provided services to an employer and is subsequently appointed as an arbitrator.  The fact that Mr. McKelvie served in the position of manager of the Woodlands Division does not by itself raise a reasonable apprehension of bias.  However, when that fact is considered together with his involvement with the negotiation of prior collective agreements, in particular the negotiation of the language that is the subject matter of the current dispute, and with his recent position as a fact finding board member for the Employer, there is a reasonable apprehension of bias.  There is a reasonable perception of lack of independence, on the basis of the perception of a reasonably informed person in the circumstances.”

 

 

[9]              As can be seen from the portion of the Award set out in para. [8] hereof, the majority of the Arbitration Board essentially concluded that there was a reasonable apprehension of bias on the part of Mr. McKelvie not because of his having previously served in the position of Manager of the Woodlands Division of the Applicant but rather because of the following:

 

(1)     His direct involvement with the negotiation of the prior collective agreements and in particular the negotiation of the language that is the subject matter of the present dispute; and

 

(2)     His recent position as a fact-finding board member for the Employer.

 

Arbitration Board’s Consideration of Its Jurisdiction.

 

[10]         Commencing at p. 13 of the Award, the majority of the Arbitration Board expressed the following positions with respect to its jurisdiction to rule on the issue of a reasonable apprehension of bias on the part of Mr. McKelvie.  It stated:

 

“...  Arbitration Boards in the Province of Newfoundland and Labrador have jurisdiction to consider and apply principles of law, such as estoppel (see U.F.C.W., Local 1252 v. Lewisporte Wholesalers (1989) 79 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 237 (Nfld. S.C.T.D.)).  An issue of bias raises the application of a principle of law, which is distinct from a question of the interpretation or application of the Collective Agreement.  There is no authority in this Province that directly addresses the issue of the Board’s jurisdiction to decide an issue of bias.  The issue does not appear to have been addressed in several Supreme Court of Newfoundland and Labrador decisions that have reviewed the merits of decisions by an Arbitration Board or the Labour Relations Board on a question of bias.  The Court has either upheld or quashed the decision without addressing the issue of jurisdiction (see NAPE v. Newfoundland Medical Care Commission (1991) 90 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 17 (Nfld S.C.T.D.) (the ‘MCP’ case), Newfoundland (Treasury Board) v. NAPE (1999) 184 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 231 (Nfld. S.C.T.D.) (the ‘Treasury Board’ case) and Transport and Allied Workers Union, Local 855 v. Newfoundland Construction Labour Relations Association (2000) 187 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 118 (Nfld. S.C.T.D.) (the ‘NCLR’ case). 

 

The requirement of arbitration board impartiality is addressed in Brown & Beatty, Canadian Labour Arbitration, 3rd edition, at paragraph 1:5210.  The authors suggest the procedure to follow is that an objection on the grounds of bias be raised at the outset of the hearing.  The authors state ‘and when such an objection is taken, the usual procedure is for the arbitrator or board to give a ruling, and if the challenge is upheld, to withdraw from the arbitration.’

 

In Palmer, Collective Agreement Arbitration in Canada, the authors discuss a decision being made by an arbitration board on a question of its own bias, and state at paragraph 1.20 that ‘it would seem that this is an improper procedure as it would appear inappropriate to rule on an accusation against oneself.’  One of the authorities quoted for this statement was discussed by the parties in their submissions, namely, City of Saint John and Canadian Union of Public Employees, Local 18 (1986) 24 L.A.C. (3d) 152, (Collier) the (‘Saint John’ case).  The Saint John case was a New Brunswick case which decided that a board of arbitration does not have jurisdiction to determine a challenge made to it on the grounds of reasonable apprehension of bias or pursuant to Section 74(1) of the Industrial Relations Act, R.S.N.B. 1973, c. I-4.  The arbitration board suggested the appropriate procedure was to have the matter challenged in the Courts.  The arbitration board in the Saint John case relied on the principle that an arbitrator who has been removed by the Courts may be replaced by the Courts.  The arbitration board concluded that in New Brunswick there was no procedure available to replace an arbitration board other than by Court appointment following removal by the Court.  However, in this Province, in the event the Board finds there is a reasonable apprehension of bias, the Board is no longer validly constituted but there is a procedure available to replace the Board.  The parties may process the grievance to arbitration by following the procedure to appoint an arbitration board under the Collective Agreement.  The arbitration board in the Saint John case also relied upon the reason that the nominee was prohibited from determining an issue where the nominee had an interest in the outcome, and a nominee would have an interest in a decision on his or her own bias.  However, the decision in the Saint John case was based upon a specific legislative provision under the Industrial Relations Act of New Brunswick, a provision that is not applicable in this Province.  Therefore, the Saint John case is distinguishable from the present case.

 

The Board has considered the finding in the MCP

case that where there is reasonable apprehension of bias any decision made by the tribunal is void.  The Employer submitted that a decision by an arbitration board that a board member has a reasonable apprehension of bias, would itself be a void decision.  However, an arbitration board does have jurisdiction to decide a question of its own jurisdiction.  The Board finds that the reference in the MCP case to a void decision means a decision on the merits of the grievance.  The MCP case does not have the effect of removing the Board’s jurisdiction to decide an issue of bias.

 

In Jones and de Villars, Principles of Administrative Law, the authors state at page 389 that ‘the practice of having an allegation of bias heard and decided by the member or panel in respect of whom the allegation is alleged is problematic’.  Various suggestions were made in the text regarding the most practical procedure to follow to decide a question of bias.  However, the text does not state that an arbitration board lacks jurisdiction to decide the issue.

 

The Arbitration Board has considered the practical effect of its ruling on the issue of jurisdiction.  By having the issue of bias raised before the Arbitration Board, the factual basis for the allegation may be presented in the first instance to the Board.  Witnesses may be called to testify, as they were in the present case, with respect to the facts related to the allegation of bias. It is more convenient for the parties to present the factual background with respect to an allegation of bias at an arbitration hearing, rather than to defer presentation of evidence until such time as the matter is heard on judicial review before a Court.  It is also in the interest of fairness to the parties and to the Arbitration Board to have disclosure of the factual background related to the allegation at the earliest opportunity.  In addition, by presenting the evidence relevant to the allegation of bias and making submissions to the Arbitration Board, the Board members affected by the allegation of bias may give consideration to withdrawing from the case without the necessity for making a ruling on the allegation of bias.  Further, in the event that the allegation of bias was not raised in the first instance before the Arbitration Board and the bias objection was subsequently raised and upheld by the Court, then any award would likely be quashed and the matter remitted to a new arbitration board.  The parties would incur the unnecessary time and expense of an arbitration hearing which could possibly be avoided if the issue of reasonable apprehension of bias was raised before the Board and made the subject of a ruling by the Board.  For these reasons, it is appropriate that an allegation of bias be raised before the Arbitration Board, and that the Board have an opportunity to make a ruling on the objection.

 

Dissent of Employer Nominee.

 

[11]         It is worthy of note that the majority concluded that the Employer nominee, Mr. McKelvie, would have a personal knowledge as to the intention of the Employer when the language in the Collective Agreement which was the subject matter of the dispute was originally negotiated.  The majority concluded therefore that his decision might be influenced by his personal knowledge of the factual background of the case.  However, quite naturally, the Board did not have before it any evidence from Mr. McKelvie himself offering any explanation or view with respect to his background and involvement, if any, in the negotiation of the disputed language.  It was not until he filed his dissent to the majority award that Mr. McKelvie’s view or understanding of the background of the negotiations of the disputed provisions of the Collective Agreement was made known. 

 

[12]         Apparently at one time there was only a single union representing the Company’s workers in its Woodlands’ operations.  When the Company acquired the second mill in Newfoundland situated at Stephenville, workers doing similar work but for the two different mills were represented by different unions.  Apparently it was against this background that the disputed language of the Collective Agreement was negotiated.

 

[13]         In his dissent Mr. McKelvie stated:

 

   “Quite a bit of emphasis was placed on the fact the writer negotiated language that directly impacted on the grievance on hand.  In fact the language relative to the article in question was initiated by the Union (CEPU) because the new contract at the time had to cover their members only, those that were registered with seniority in the Grand Falls area of the woodlands operations.  Previously all woodlands employees were in the same union, and that union’s jurisdiction had been island wide, there now being two unions involved delivering wood to both mills each had to have their own area of jurisdiction.  This was necessary to provide for the separate listing for seniority for employees in the two different areas working for the same employer.

 

...

 

   Given the above I feel that the objection by the union directed at the writer and the recognition clause was not reviewed sufficiently before the board.  Undo weight was given to the writer’s involvement as a signatory as part of his responsibilities as Manager of the woodlands at the time.”

 

Standard of Review.

 

[14]         Although expressed differently, the parties are in agreement with respect to the standard of review.  The Applicant states it as follows:

 

“The case raises the issue of the jurisdiction of the Board of Arbitration to consider an objection to its composition as well as the issue of breach of the rules of natural justice.  If jurisdiction is found, the case raises the issue of the application of a general principle of law, namely, reasonable apprehension of bias.”

 

 

[15]         The Respondent Union states the issue as follows:

 

“The matter for determination by the arbitration board on the preliminary objection was an issue of natural justice, as to whether there was a reasonable apprehension of bias.  As such it raises a question of jurisdiction.  It is submitted that the proper standard of review of an arbitration board on a question of whether the facts give rise to a reasonable apprehension of bias is correctness. ...

 

As to the determination of the facts on which the determination is based it is submitted that the appropriate standard of review is patent unreasonableness.”

 

 

[16]         Thus, both the Applicant and the Union seem to agree that where there is a question of jurisdiction the decision of the Arbitration Board is reviewable on a standard of correctness.  However, the Union’s submission appears to add a nuance, namely, that in determining the facts upon which the Arbitration Board then concludes that there has been a breach of natural justice and thus a loss of jurisdiction, the determination of the facts upon which the ultimate ruling is based is done on a standard of patent unreasonableness.  The Union makes no statement as to the application of the facts to the general principles of law as to what constitutes a reasonable apprehension of bias.  Presumably its position is subsumed in the general statement that this is an issue of jurisdiction and thus where it comes to applying the principles of law as to what constitutes a reasonable apprehension of bias, the standard of review is correctness.  The Applicant Employer asserts a correctness standard throughout.

 

[17]         I am satisfied that the standard of review applicable with respect to this matter is a standard of patent unreasonableness insofar as it applies to the determination of the facts to which the principles of law are to be applied.  Once the facts are determined however, it is necessary that the Board achieve a standard of correctness when applying the principles of law with respect to reasonable apprehension of bias to those facts.

 

Jurisdiction of Arbitration Board to Hear Allegation of Bias.

 

[18]         Obviously at the commencement of an Arbitration Board hearing it is convenient and practical for allegations of a reasonable apprehension of bias on the part of one or more of the Board members to be raised before the Arbitration Board.  A discussion between counsel and the Board as to the basis for the objection may well  result in the obvious benefit of either the challenged Board member recusing himself or herself, or the allegation of bias possibly being withdrawn.  In this regard, counsel for the Union stated that such a procedure is no different than the manner in which all labour Arbitration Boards in this Province deal with preliminary questions, including jurisdiction issues.  In that regard he points to the first two paragraphs of the Arbitration Award where there was discussion concerning:

 

(1)     whether the grievance procedure was properly followed, or any requirements waived;

 

(2)     whether the Board would remain seized of the matter following publication of the Award in the event there was a question of interpretation or compensation arising from the Award, and

 

(3)     any applicable time limits for the filing of the Award.

 

These issues however were all agreed to.  It was not necessary for the Board to make any decision with respect thereto.  Essentially these were procedural agreements or agreements on facts.

 

[19]         The question of the acceptability of the Board was raised by the Chair in accordance with the Board’s standard procedure and objection was taken in accordance with the notice previously given to the other party through its solicitor.  The Employer submitted to the Arbitration Board that the issue of bias was not an arbitrable issue and the Board had no jurisdiction to hear the issue.  Nonetheless the Board proceeded to hear evidence and argument and rendered its decision finding that there was a reasonable apprehension of bias on the part of the Employer nominee, Mr. McKelvie.  In Brown and Beatty, Canadian Labour Arbitration, 3rd edition, section 1:5210, the authors state:

 

“A party seeking to challenge an arbitrator for bias or reasonable apprehension of bias ought to do so at the outset of the hearing as a preliminary matter; otherwise, failure to object with knowledge of the facts will likely be taken as acquiescence.  And when such an objection is taken, the usual procedure is for the arbitrator or board to give a ruling, and if the challenge is upheld, to withdraw from the arbitration.”                                                                                                    [Footnotes omitted.]

 

[20]         It is sometimes suggested that it would be an improper procedure for an Arbitration Board to rule on reasonable apprehension of bias on the part of one or more of its members because it would be inappropriate to rule on an accusation against oneself.  In that regard Palmer, Collective Agreement Arbitration in Canada, 3rd edition, at para. 1.20 states:

 

“A party seeking to disqualify an arbitrator on the grounds of bias must abduce sufficient evidence to meet the common law burden of proof.  One assumes that decisions as to bias are made by the courts or the individuals involved.  In recent cases, however, there has been a trend to have the decision made by the board itself.  It would seem that this is an improper procedure as it would appear inappropriate to rule on an accusation against one’s self.” [Footnotes omitted.]

 

And at para. 122:

 

“Finally, it is clear that the issue of bias cannot be determined by a board of arbitration with respect to one of its members; a point sometimes not recognized in awards.”                                                                                                   [Footnotes omitted.]

 

[21]         As the Arbitration Board itself noted (see para. [10]), numerous Newfoundland cases have dealt with reasonable apprehension of bias on the part of Board members in arbitration settings but have not commented on the jurisdiction of the Arbitration Board to decide such an issue at first instance.  This lack of commentary however hardly qualifies as a considered approval by the Courts of this Province of such assumption of jurisdiction to determine issues of bias by Arbitration Boards themselves.

 

[22]         There are practical considerations militating against the suggestion that it is improper for a Board to rule on reasonable apprehension of bias on the part of one or more of its own members.  In Flamborough (Township) v. Canada (National Energy Board) [1984] F.C.J. No. 526 at pp. 16 – 17, Mahoney, J.A. in the Federal Court of Appeal, stated with respect to such a proposition:

 

“I should have added that the proposition that a member of a tribunal against whom an allegation of an apprehension of bias has been made cannot, himself, dispose of or participate in disposing of that allegation is utterly fatuous.  The practical effect, if that were the law, would be the paralysis of tribunals, and trial courts, at the whim of anyone willing to allege bias.  The availability of judicial review and appeal ensures that such charges will, ultimately, be dealt with by a disinterested judiciary.”

 

Other Applicable Case Law and Commentary.

 

[23]         Support for the proposition that an Arbitration Board is without jurisdiction to decide the question of bias may be found in Re City of Saint John v. The Canadian Union of Public Employees, Local 18 (1986), 24 L.A.C. (3d) 152 (Collier).  This case involved consideration of s. 74(1) of the New Brunswick Industrial Relations Act which provided as follows:

 

“74(1)   Any person is eligible to be appointed an arbitrator, or chairman or member of an arbitration board, in any arbitration proceeding under the provisions of s. 55, but no person shall serve who is directly affected by the matter in an arbitration or who has been involved in an attempt to negotiate or settle the matter.”

 

[24]         In Re City of Saint John the award at pp. 154 – 155 states:

 

 “In my opinion, a board of arbitration does not have jurisdiction to determine a challenge made to it or to one of its members pursuant to s. 74(1) of the Industrial Relations Act or a challenge made on the grounds of bias or the reasonable apprehension of bias.  In addition it is my opinion that a board of arbitration cannot assume jurisdiction over these issues even with the concurrence of the parties who are responsible for the creation of the board.

 

   The moment a challenge such as the one in this case is raised to the appointment of a member of an arbitration board the appropriate resolution of that dispute lies to the courts. ...

 

  It could be argued very strenuously that a member of this board, and in particular Mr. Levine could raise a reasonable apprehension of bias on the part of the employer by his participation in the board’s decision on this issue.  But, it also goes one step further.  It could also be strenuously argued that, in participating in the board’s decision as he must, Mr. Levine is a person ‘who is directly affected by the matter in arbitration’ and therefore is in violation of s. 74(1) of the Industrial Relations Act.

 

   It is because of this latter difficulty I do not feel a board of arbitration can assume jurisdiction over an issue such as this even with the concurrence of the parties who appointed it.  The Act places a clear prohibition upon the appointment of an arbitrator who is directly affected by the matter in arbitration.  This board was appointed for the purpose of determining an issue which arrises by way of a grievance.  This arbitration board, as it is currently composed, could not have been appointed for the purpose of determining this preliminary issue because the presence of Mr. Levine would constitute a violation of s. 74(1) of the Industrial Relations Act. ...”

 

 

[25]         Both counsel cite this case as support for their vastly divergent positions with respect to whether or not an arbitration board does have jurisdiction to rule on issues of its own bias.  Counsel for the Union also points out that pursuant to s. 88(1)(a) of the Labour Relations Act, RSNL 1990 c. L-1, an arbitration board has the power to determine its own procedure and in furtherance of this power can determine whether a matter is arbitrable pursuant to s. 88(1)(c).  Counsel for the Union points out that the decision of the Arbitration Board in Re City of Saint John that Mr. Levine could not participate in the decision relating to reasonable apprehension of bias on his part stems solely from the statutory section 74(1) of the Industrial Relations Act.  I am not satisfied he is correct in that interpretation of the decision.  However, nothing in this decision turns on that point as shall be seen later in these reasons. 

 

[26]         In MacDonalds Consolidated Ltd. v. Saskatchewan Joint Board, Retail, Wholesale and Department Store Union and Dickinson (1988), 65 Sask. R. 224 (Sask. Q.B.), the employer contested the appointment of a union nominee to an Arbitration Board on the ground of bias because the union nominee had acted as the union’s solicitor.  Apparently the record of the arbitration clearly indicated that all parties agreed that the Arbitration Board should make the decision whether or not the union’s nominee should be disqualified.  That is, the Board was recognized as having the jurisdiction to decide whether, on the facts before it, there was a reasonable apprehension of bias.  Scheibel, J. on an application to set aside or quash the award of the Board stated at paras. 8 – 9 as follows:

 

  “The initial reaction is to quickly conclude that it was improper for (the union nominee) to sit as a member of the board which was to decide whether he has any bias or whether there was any reasonable apprehension of bias.

 

  Generally speaking, the issue of bias of one of the board members cannot be determined by that board.  However, where the parties agree that the board is to make the determination, the general rule does not apply.”

 

[27]         Scheibel, J. then continued at paras. 12 – 13 with respect to the employer consenting to the arbitration Board making the decision on bias:

 

  “The employer did not have to agree with this procedure.  It was open for it to apply to the court for prohibition, however, it chose to have the matter decided by the board. 

 

   In agreeing to have the matter determined in that fashion the employer cannot now complain that this decision of the board is void or contravenes the principles of natural justice simply because the board dealt with the matter.  The employer, by consenting to the procedure, agreed to have the board make the decision.  In so doing, the board is not restricted to handing down an award which is favourable to the employer.  It is not open for the employer to say that it will accept an award which is in its favour while all others are void or contrary to natural justice.  The effect of that would be to take away the right of the board to make a decision.”

 

 

[28]         Jones and deVillars in their work Principles of Administrative Law (3d edition) Carswell 1999, at p. 389, state:

 

  “Normally, the allegation of bias is heard and decided by the very member or panel in respect of whom a bias or reasonable apprehension of bias is alleged.  This practice, though hallowed by time, is problematic.” [Footnote omitted.]

 

 

[29]         The authors then proceed to discuss various methods for resolving the question of “who should decide” and ultimately conclude at pages 389  – 390:

 

“Perhaps the best choice is to leave all of the options open, including the possibility of having the allegation heard and decided by the member in respect of whom the allegation is made. The choice between them should be left in the discretion of the tribunal.  The decision might be based on a weighing of the availability, cost and delays inherent in each option, together with any statutory requirements about quorum and representation.  This calculation will change from tribunal to tribunal, and from time to time within the same tribunal.  This open-ended solution leaves the tribunal with the ability to respond to the justice of the situation, while retaining control over its process and discouraging frivolous allegations of bias.”

 

Conclusion.

 

[30]         The issue of reasonable apprehension of bias is succinctly set out in the following principle enunciated by Rand, J. in Szilard v. Szasz, [1955] S.C.R. 3, where he stated:

 

“Each party to an arbitration, acting reasonably, is entitled to a sustained confidence in the independence of mind of those who sit in judgment of him and his affairs.”

 

[31]         Szilard v. Szasz was also quoted with approval by L.D. Barry, J. in Newfoundland Association of Public Employees and Scaplan v. Medical Care Commission (Nfld.) (1991), 90 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 17, as follows:

 

“[21]   Both counsel agreed that the law concerning bias from business or professional relationships, is properly set out in Szilard v. Szasz, [1955] 1 D.L.R. 370 (S.C.C.) at 351 where Rank, J. stated:

 

‘From its inception arbitration has been held to be of the nature of judicial determination and to entail incidents appropriate to that fact.  The arbitrators are to exercise their function not as the advocates of the parties nominating them, and a fortiori of one party when they are agreed upon by all, but with as free, independent and impartial minds as the circumstances permit.  In particular they must be untrammelled by such influences as to a fair-minded person would raise a reasonable doubt of that impersonal attitude which each party is entitled to.”

 

[32]         As a secondary issue, the parties have asked the Court, if it should find that the Arbitration Board did have jurisdiction to rule on bias or reasonable apprehension of bias on the part of Mr. McKelvie, to then go on and answer a second question.  The second question naturally is whether the Arbitration Board’s decision, reviewed according to the applicable standard of review, properly decided that there was in fact a reasonable apprehension of bias on the part of Mr. McKelvie. 

 

[33]         It is that second question that points out clearly the inappropriateness of the Board assuming jurisdiction over such a question.  The following issues arise from it so doing:

(1)     How can the Arbitration Board decide the issue without hearing from the impugned member?  Does the impugned Board member give evidence?

 

(2)     Is he/she to be sworn as a witness?

 

(3)     Is he/she subject to cross-examination?

 

(4)     What is the propriety of the remaining Board members ruling on the evidence of a fellow Board member, especially where there may be issues of credibility involved?

 

(5)     Are only the remaining two Board members the ones entitled to rule on the bias question?  What if it is a single member arbitration panel?

 

(6)     Who hears the bias challenge and rules upon it if two Board members are challenged for bias?

 

(7)     If the Board assumes jurisdiction and rules against the bias objection and then proceeds to hear the substantive portion of the matter, what constitutes the “record” for a judicial review of the “bias” portion of the Award?

 

[34]         I have great sympathy for the argument expressed by Mahoney, J.A. in Flamborough cited at para. [22] hereof.  A major principle in the establishment of an extrajudicial procedure for resolution of labour disputes by arbitration was that the procedure would be both summary and expeditious.  The passage of time has layered upon these processes many of the trappings of a full judicial proceeding thereby militating against the cost efficient and expeditious process initially envisaged by submitting such matters to arbitration.  There is, as Mahoney, J.A. correctly points out, a risk of the paralysis of tribunals at the whim of anyone willing to allege bias.  This is perhaps a greater risk at the Arbitration Board level where the same mechanisms as exist at the Court level are not available to punish frivolous objectors by awards of costs.

 

[35]         Similarly I am empathic to the notion expressed by Jones and de Villars that perhaps the choice of whether or not to proceed with a ruling of bias should be left to the Board and that the Board may make its decision based upon a weighing of the availability, costs and delays inherent in each option, together with any statutory requirements about quorum and representation. 

 

[36]         However these realistic and practical considerations cannot, in my view, overcome the principles set out in Szilard v. Szasz as quoted in paras. [30] and [31] hereof.  The parties to an arbitration are entitled to have their dispute arbitrated by arbitrators with “as free, independent and impartial minds as the circumstances permit.”  If the question of a reasonable apprehension of bias exists with respect to an arbitrator deciding the substantive portion of an arbitration, a fortiori that apprehension of bias is even greater when it comes to the arbitrator ruling on or participating in a decision of the Arbitration Board to determine whether that apprehension is reasonable.  He or she becomes a judge in his or her own cause.

 

[37]         This concern, combined with the practical considerations evidenced by the questions set out in para. [33] hereof caused me to conclude that it is in fact inappropriate for an Arbitration Board to rule upon a challenge to its jurisdiction based upon a reasonable apprehension of bias on the part of the Board or some member thereof.

 

[38]         Therefore the Award of the majority of the Arbitration Board on the question of a reasonable apprehension of bias with respect to Mr. Ronald McKelvie as a member of the Board is set aside.

 

How is the Bias Question to be Resolved?

 

[39]         Counsel have asked me to provide direction as to how the question of whether or not there is a reasonable apprehension of bias on the part of Mr. McKelvie is to be decided if I were to conclude, as I have, that the Arbitration Board had no jurisdiction to make such a ruling.  In my view the first issue to be determined is whether or not Mr. McKelvie is now prepared to recuse himself voluntarily from his appointment as a member of this Arbitration Board.  As indicated earlier, I have no record upon which to come to any conclusion that he should recuse himself.  The only “evidence” one has of Mr. McKelvie’s position is his own unsworn and unchallenged statement contained in his dissenting decision.  In light of the nature of such “evidence” and its manner of presentation, I feel it is not appropriate for this Court to comment on the issue.

 

[40]         The question of whether or not there is a reasonable apprehension of bias on the part of Mr. McKelvie ought to be determined by an inter partes application to have that question determined by this Court.  The matter should proceed with an application by counsel for Mr. McKelvie based upon affidavit evidence by Mr. McKelvie (if he should so wish) and by others setting out the factual background of his position.  The Union would have an opportunity to similarly file affidavit evidence in response thereto, with Mr. McKelvie having an opportunity to provide affidavits in reply.  The parties can, if they wish, seek to have the deponents examined on their affidavits in accordance with the Rules of Court and the parties may choose to (and should) file memoranda of argument in support of their respective positions.

 

[41]         In light of this decision it is unnecessary for me to consider the question as to whether or not there is in fact a reasonable apprehension of bias on the part of Mr. McKelvie and I decline to do so.

 

Costs.

 

[42]         The question of costs in this matter should be deferred until a resolution of the bias issue either by voluntarily recusal on the part of Mr. McKelvie or by a decision of this Court.  Therefore no award of costs is made at this time.
