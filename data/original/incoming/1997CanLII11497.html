Kelly, J.

 

The Province of Nova Scotia entered into an agreement with a group of companies in April of 1996 to build the first public-private toll highway in Canada. An application was made by a third party to disclose the principal document of the arrangement, which is referred to as the "Omnibus Agreement", pursuant to the Freedom of Information and Protection of Privacy Act, S.N.S. 1993, c. 5. After a recommendation by the Review Officer that the Omnibus Agreement be disclosed, the Appellant now appeals to this Court as provided by the Act to prevent disclosure of the Omnibus Agreement. Throughout this matter, the Province has been willing to meet the requests for the disclosure of the relevant information. Two Nova Scotia newspaper companies are parties seeking the release of the information.

 

 

1.         BACKGROUND OF APPELLANT'S POSITION

 

The Appellant (AHC), acting collectively, is Atlantic Highways Corporation and Atlantic Highways Management Corporation. Apparently both of these companies are associated with or are subsidiaries of Canadian Highways International Corporation whose primary business is the construction of highways in Canada and other parts of the world.

 

AHC submits that the Omnibus Agreement as a whole was developed through research and resources of AHC and Canadian Highways. They claim the document contains detailed excerpts of a Design Build Contract and taken as a whole details the manner in which AHC conducts it business. At the present time, Canadian Highways, through an affiliate company, is competing in New Brunswick with respect to a new toll highway project against some of the same companies which they had competed against in Nova Scotia for the Highway 104 Project. The toll highway project in New Brunswick, although a bigger project than the Highway 104 Project, will be structured as a public-private partnership with private financing.

 

The Highway 104 Project in Nova Scotia is structured as public-private partnership of the Province and the private sector. It is the first public-private partnership large infrastructure project in Canada with private financing and without government guarantees or recourse to government revenue. The Omnibus Agreement as a whole details arrangement and the mechanism by which this partnership has been structured. Canadian Highways submits that it was anxious, through AHC, to obtain a presence in Atlantic Canada, and as a result negotiated terms less favourable than it may have if it already had a presence in this area. They argue that release of this information may compromise its negotiation position in New Brunswick.

 

 

2.         PROCEDURAL BACKGROUND

 

The Appellant AHC is the contracting party with the Department of Transportation and Public Works as representing the Province, and is a "third party"within the meaning of that term in s. 3(m) of the Act. This matter was initiated by the application. for the disclosure of the records by Atlantic Canada Transportation Group Inc. (ACTG) which has not been a party in this appeal.

 

ACTG applied for disclosure of the Highway 104 project records pursuant to s. 6(1) of the Act, and on July 2, 1996, the Deputy Minister of the Department responded that the request had been granted in part, and that the Department was prepared to release the agreements signed between the Department and Atlantic Highways Corporation Inc., in particular the Omnibus Agreement, the Annual Maintenance Agreement, and an Interim Work Agreement regarding clearing, but that the release of this record would be suspended pending notification of the third party of the Department's decision to release the records. ACTG did not challenge the decision of the Department to grant the request in part only.

 

Subsequently, on June 12, 1996, and June 17, 1996, three additional applications for access to the same agreements were received from Mr. Brooke Taylor, Mr. David Rodenhiser on behalf of The Daily News and Mr. Dean Jobb for The Halifax Chronicle Herald, respectively. Mr. Taylor did not subsequently appear on the matter.

 

The Department was of the view that the applications were within s. 21 of the Act and that it was not entitled to disclose the records without first seeking the consent of the third party as required by s. 21(4) of the Act. Accordingly, the Department gave notice to AHC pursuant to s. 22(1) requesting its consent, and to the applicants, pursuant to s. 22(2), advising the applicants that the third party would be given an opportunity to make representations concerning disclosure. AHC refused to consent to the disclosure of the records to ACTG and the three subsequent applicants.

 

The Department then reaffirmed its decision to disclose the agreements, and so advised the applicants and AHC, pursuant to s. 23(2) of the Act. In that notice, as required by s. 23(3), the Department advised AHC of its right to request a review by the Review Officer pursuant to the Act. AHC exercised this right to have the Department's decision to disclose the records reviewed by the Review Officer pursuant to s. 32(2) of the Act.

 

On October 8, 1996, the Review Officer, acting under s. 39 of the Act, presented his written report, recommending therein that the Department of Transportation and Public Works disclose the Omnibus Agreement as planned by the Department, the Omnibus Agreement being the only record in contention before the Review Officer.

 

Acting in accordance with s. 40 of the Act, the Department informed the AHC that the agreements would be made available, subject to its right to appeal the Review Officer's decision. AHC then appealed to this Court, pursuant to s. 41 of the Act, and the Department, as required by s. 41(3), informed the applicants who had requested copies of the agreements accordingly.

 

The Act provides in s. 41(5) that in an appeal before this Court, the Review Officer is not a party. This Court has proceeded de novo as permitted by s. 42(1)(a), and has reviewed the records sought in this case in order to determine whether it should properly be withheld pursuant to s. 21 of the Act. A copy of the Omnibus Agreement and related documents has been provided separately, in a sealed envelope, for in camera examination by the Court pursuant to s. 42 of the Act.

 

There is no contest regarding the jurisdiction of the Court to deal with this appeal pursuant to s. 42(1) of the Act. I would also note that special procedural precautions were taken and an Order was issued to ensure a high level of confidentiality was maintained while counsel had access to the records and briefs of AHC in order to prepare for the hearing of this matter. A useful discussion on this procedural problem and other procedural matters in Freedom of Information applications may be found in Gerald J. Fahey and David S. Goodis, "Judicial Review of Decisions under Ontario's Information and Privacy Acts", The Advocates Quarterly, Vol. 18, No. 4, December 1996 at pp. 472-277.

 

 

3.         ISSUE

 

Does the Act entitle AHC to restrict the Province from releasing the Omnibus Agreement and related documents to The Halifax-Chronicle Herald and The Daily News?

 

 

4.         CONSIDERATION OF ACT

 

A.        THE ACT

 

The main, if not the sole, area in issue here is the interpretation of s. 21(1) of the Act and its application to the facts and circumstances of this case. Such interpretation, of course, involves a consideration of other parts of the Act and principally its expressed purpose.

 

This purpose is enunciated in s. 2 of the Act:

 

2.         The purpose of this Act is

 

(a)       to ensure that public bodies are fully accountable to the public by

 

(i)         giving the public a right of access to records,

 

(ii)        giving individuals a right of access to, and a right to correction of, personal information about themselves,

 

(iii)       specifying limited exceptions to the rights of access,

 

(iv)       preventing the unauthorized collection, use or disclosure of personal information by public bodies, and

 

(v)        providing for an independent review of decisions made pursuant to this Act; and

 

(b)       to provide for the disclosure of all government information with necessary exemptions, that are limited and specific, in order to

 

(i)         facilitate informed public participation in policy formulation,

 

(ii)        ensure fairness in government decision-making,

 

(iii)       permit the airing and reconciliation of divergent views;

 

(c)        to protect the privacy of individuals with respect to personal information about themselves held by public bodies and to provide individuals with a right of access to that information.

 

By this section the legislature seeks to make government bodies accountable to the public by ensuring there is a right of access to government records, at the same time restricting that access only to the extent necessary to protect certain personal information and other "necessary exemptions". Individuals are also guaranteed access to personal information about themselves.

 

Section 2 (b) (i) specifies that one of the general purposes of the Act is to:

 

(b)(i)    facilitate informed public participation in policy formulation,

 

(ii)        ensure fairness in government decision making,

(iii)       permit the airing and reconciliation of divergent views.

 

This emphasis is obviously to encourage general publication of government matters and public discussion and debate.

 

The legislative intent of the present Act is for broad access to the records of government bodies and generally to all government information. The right of access to all such information is limited only to the specific restrictions outlined in the Act. The predecessor of this Act had a different emphasis in that it acknowledged the right of access only to specifically enumerate information instead of the enumerated exception method. However, even in the interpretation of its predecessor Act, the Nova Scotia Court of Appeal concluded that that Act "should be construed literally in light of its stated purposes" and, that "public access ought not to be frustrated by the courts except upon the clearest grounds so that doubt ought to be resolved in favour of disclosure". See McLaughlin v. Halifax‑Dartmouth Bridge Commission (1993), 125 N.S.R. (2d) 288 at pp. 292-293. It is thus clear that the legislative intent of the current Act is for even broader public disclosure of information relating to the legislative, executive and judicial branches of government.

 

The onus on the Appellant AHC to prove that the information it seeks to restrict falls within the specific and limited exemptions of the Act - to show that the two applicants here, essentially The Halifax Chronical Herald and The Daily News, have no right of access to the Omnibus Agreement. (See s. 45(3)(b))

 

AHC submits that their right of access is limited by certain parts of s. 21(1) of the Act:

 

21(1)   The head of a public body shall refuse to disclose to an applicant information

 

(a)       that would reveal

 

(i)         trade secrets of a third party, or

 

(ii)        commercial, financial, labour relations, scientific or technical information of a third party

 

(b)       that is supplied, implicitly or explicitly, in confidence; and

 

(c)        the disclosure of which would reasonably be expected to

 

(i)         harm significantly the competitive position or interfere significantly with the negotiating position of the third party.

 

(ii)        result in similar information no longer being supplied to the public body when it is in the public interest that similar information continue to be supplied,

 

(iii)       result in undue financial loss or gain to any person or organization, or

 

(iv)       reveal information supplied to, or the report of, an arbitrator, mediator, labour relations officer or other person or body appointed to resolve or inquire into a labour-relations dispute.

 

(2)       The head of a public body shall refuse to disclose to an applicant information that was obtained on a tax return or gathered for the purpose of determining tax liability or collecting a tax.

 

(3)       The head of a public body shall disclose to an applicant a report prepared in the course of routine inspections by an agency that is authorized to enforce compliance with an enactment.

 

(4)       Subsections (1) and (2) do not apply if the third party consents to the disclosure.

 

The term 'trade secret' is defined in s. 1(n) of the Act as follows:

 

'Trade Secret' means information, including a formula, pattern, compilation, program, device, product, method, technique or process that

 

(i)         is used, or may be used, in business or for any commercial advantage;

 

(ii)        derives independent economic value, actual or potential, from not being generally known to the public or to any other persons who can obtain economic value from its disclosure or use;

 

(iii)       is the subject of reasonable efforts to prevent it from being generally known, and

 

(iv)       the disclosure of which would result in harm or improper benefit

 

More specifically, AHC claims the Minister should have refused to disclose the agreement because it would reveal information restricted by s. 21(1) of the Act.

 

 

B.        ARGUMENT

 

The Appellant's arguments in relation to the application of s. 21(1) to these circumstances can be conveniently dealt with in two parts. First, that the section should be read disjunctively, that is, if AHC can fit the Omnibus Agreement and related documents into any of the three general categories, the information should be restricted. They also alternatively submit that they have advanced evidence to satisfy the exempt requirements of s. 21, regardless of which interpretation the court accepts. I will deal with each in turn.

 

 

(1)       Interpretation of Section 21(1)

 

AHC argued for a disjunctive interpretation of s. 21(1) before the Review Officer and this court. In the review hearing AHC appears to have adopted the position that all it had to demonstrate was that the Omnibus Agreement contained one or more of the listed 21(1) categories. The Review Officer disagreed with this interpretation and concluded that AHC also must show that the Omnibus Agreement came within the (b) and (c) provisions of s. 21(1). Generally that it was provided by AHC to government in confidence, expressly or by implication, and further that the disclosure of the Agreement would reasonably be expected to cause one of the harms listed in subparagraphs 21(1) (c). As one basis for his finding that the three lettered subsections of s. 21(1) should be read conjunctively the Review Officer relied on McNairn and Woodbury, Government Information: Access and Privacy, Toronto: Carswell, 1992. In that work the authors, at pp. 4-3 and 4-4, conclude that all of the access statutes reviewed by them, including the Nova Scotia Act, provide for the exemption from disclosure of information of a financial, commercial, and technical nature, provided certain conditions are met. In particular, the authors conclude that s. 21 of the Nova Scotia Act requires that such information be supplied in confidence, and carry the potential for harm to the third party if disclosed. In other words, that the lettered parts of s. 21(1) should be read conjunctively.

 

In support for the contention that s. 21(1) should be read disjunctively counsel for AHC argued imaginatively that other sections of the Act were inconsistent with a conjunctive interpretation. He referred specifically to the definition of "trade secret", one of the items to be restricted in s. 21(1)(a), and noted that it included a requirement that a person or body alleging something should be classified as a trade secret had to show the information was dealt with in some degree of confidentiality (s. 1(m)(iii)) and "the disclosure of which would result in harm or improper benefit". He argued that these latter two requirements were reflections of ss. (b) and (c) of s. 21(1), and thus were indicative that the legislature did not intend that (b) and (c) of that section were also required elements of proof before information would be found to be restrictive.

 

In my opinion, this and other arguments for a disjunctive application of the lettered parts of s. 21(1) fail in the face of a common sense reading of the words and in their normal grammatical application. The use of the conjunctive "and" in the next-to-final entry of a list of requirements normally requires that the list be dealt with as conjunctive. All of such listed requirements must be satisfied to effect the requirements of the section. This interpretation is strengthened in that sub-paragraph (c) itself lists four sub-categories joined by the word "or" at the end of the second-last category. Thus the section incorporates both a disjunctive category and a conjunctive one. To read them otherwise is simply illogical and a breach of the presumption of consistent expression. I am also satisfied by applying a purposeful interpretation to this section of the Act that it is appropriate to read the subsections conjunctively. In these circumstances restrictions on the general purpose of this Act to give broad access to the public of such information should be restrictively interpreted.

 

I therefore conclude that s. 21(1) should be read conjunctively and that a party seeking to apply it to restrict information must satisfy the relevant authority or the court that the information satisfies each of the lettered sub-sections of s. 21(1).

 

For purposes of considering the application of s. 21(1), I suggest the burden the Appellant AHC must satisfy under that section to qualify the Omnibus Agreement as exempted information must be to satisfy the court of the following:

 

(a)       that disclosure of the information would reveal trade secrets or commercial financial, labour relations, scientific or technical information of a third party;

 

(b)       that the information was supplied to the government authority in confidence, either implicitly or explicitly; and

 

(c)        that there is a reasonable expectation that the disclosure of the information would cause one of the injuries listed in 21(1)(c).

 

For a similar treatment of the related Ontario section, see Ontario (Workers' Compensation Board) v. Ontario (Assistant Information & Privacy Commissioner (1995), 23 O.R. (3d) 31 (Div. Ct.) at p. 37.

 

 

(2)       Application of Subsection 21(1)

 

Having decided that AHC must satisfy each of the requirements outlined above, I will review briefly the submission of AHC on each.

 

(a)       Trade Secret or Commercial Information

 

AHC claims that the information it has developed and contributed to the Omnibus Agreement is both a trade secret and one of the categories of activities listed in 21(1)(a)(ii) - principally that if the Omnibus Agreement is released that it would reveal commercial or financial, or perhaps other of the categories in the list. A 'trade secret' is defined in the Act and there has been considerable discussion by counsel regarding its application to these circumstances. I am not satisfied on the evidence or the argument that AHC's position is significantly different if the information they seek to protect is categorized as 'trade secret' or one of the other in the s. 21(1)(a)(ii) list of categories of commercial information.

 

The nature of the commercial information or trade secret which AHC seeks to protect is based on the submission that "[t]he agreement as a whole was developed through the research, efforts and cost of AHC in devising a program or proposal for developing a public-private partnership to construct, maintain and operate a toll highway".

 

The Respondents contend that this commercial information is not proprietary to AHC because, in the form it appears in the Omnibus Agreement, it is the product of negotiation with the Province. The Province has advanced evidence which indicates it played a significant role in the creation of the Omnibus Agreement and it is willing, and indeed feels obligated, to release it to the general public. In particular, the negotiations commenced on the basis of AHC's response to the Province's 'Request for Proposal' which contains the basic requirements for the eventual contract. These requirements outlined the Province's minimum proposals in such areas as development, design, construction, financing, bonding, operations and maintenance.

 

I am satisfied from the evidence that many of the areas of the Omnibus Agreement which they would have the court classify as 'trade secret' or as 'commercial information', such as the contingency fund provisions, warranty provisions, bonding and some other provisions, were significantly influenced by the requirements of the Province as evidenced in their 'Request for Proposal', a public document, or was created or modified in the negotiation process with the Province.

 

As noted by the Review Officer in his remarks, and by counsel at the hearing, some of the areas of information sought to be protected by AHC has already been substantially been disclosed to the public by the Province in a press release and otherwise, and is already public information.

 

I thus conclude that the information AHC seeks to protect has either been already exposed to publication or is so intertwined with Provincial input by way of the requirements of the 'Request for Proposal' or modified by the negotiation process that it clouds AHC's claim to a proprietary interest in the information. I am therefore not satisfied by the Appellant AHC, after reviewing the evidence, including the specific clauses referred to by them in the Omnibus Agreement, that the information they seek to protect is one of the categories of information listed in 21(1)(a).

In the event that I am incorrect in finding that AHC must show the information it seeks to protect must satisfy all of the three parts of 21(1), I will briefly review the other two parts.

 

 

(b)       Supplied in Confidence.

 

To obtain the protection of s. 21(1)(b) of the Act, AHC must show that the material they seek to protect was "supplied, implicity or explicitly, in confidence". There is no doubt that AHC and the other parties involved with negotiations with the Province have sought to keep much of the information they have developed regarding such a project from their competitors. As I have stated above, in part the issue is whether the information that found its way into the Omnibus Agreement by way of the response to the Request for Proposal and the negotiation process is information in which they have a proprietary interest. It is clear from s. 95 of the Omnibus Agreement that AHC had made some effort to keep certain of its information confidential. That section reads as follows:

 

The Province recognizes that certain of the information contained in this Agreement is seen by AHC and AHMCL as proprietary or commercial confidential, accordingly, to the extent that it is practical to do so, the Province will consult with AHC and AHMCL prior to making public disclosure in respect to the subject matter of this Agreement. The parties recognize that this Agreement is subject to the provisions and disclosure requirements of the Freedom of Information and Protection of Privacy Act (Nova Scotia), any disclosure required by law or any disclosure required in the course of enforcement of arbitration proceedings.

 

It appears implicit in wording of the section that AHC sought confidential status and that this was not conceded by the Province. Nor was it conceded by the Province that AHC had a proprietary interest in the information in the agreement or that any of the parts of the agreement should be considered 'commercial confidential'. In other words it appears the AHC sought confidential status for parts of the agreement, that the Province acknowledged their request, but only agreed to consult with them when it was practical to do so before making the Agreement or parts of it public. The balance of the section is an acknowledgement of both AHC and the Province that the document is subject to public disclosure under the Act as a consequence of it being a government document or as a consequence of any enforcement process.

 

I accept that AHC appears to have submitted certain confidential information to the Province as part of the negotiations process and, if the process had not resulted in a contract, that they would likely have been able to keep such information confidential through the effects of the Act. However, the AHC proprietary interest in any such confidential information is now so clouded by the negotiating processes and by the significant and evidenced input of Provincial information that only strong proof evidencing such information as a distinct and severable part of the agreement would suffice. I do not find evidence of that nature before me in this hearing and I find AHC has not discharged its burden regarding the confidentiality aspect of s. 21.

 

 

(c)       Harm of Disclosure

 

AHC claims here that the disclosure of the commercial information in question would contravene two of the areas of harm listed in s. 21(1)(c) under subsections (i) and (iii). They argue that the release of the information might, pursuant to ss. (i), "harm significantly the competitive position" in the proposed New Brunswick highway construction of a similar nature or the information release would "interfere significantly with [their] negotiating position"with the New Brunswick government authority on that project. For these reasons, they also claim the release of the information would "result in undue loss" for them in the New Brunswick negotiations under ss. (iii).

 

The position of AHC in seeking an exemption for the information is that they will be in a less favourable position if the New Brunswick government has access to the terms of the Nova Scotia Contract, or they will lose their competitive advantage with their competitors if those companies have the advantage of AHC's "method of proceeding", which they alleged they have developed for the Nova Scotia Omnibus Agreement.

 

The other parties in this hearing point out that such losses of advantage are purely speculative. They are indeed, but all of such categories of s. 21(1)(c) must call for speculation; the possible future harm which might possibly be caused by the future release of information. In applying such speculative legislative requirements a court must make its decision on the basis of an assessment of the degree of probability of such harm.

 

Opposing counsel suggest there is an air of unreality to AHC's speculations. For example, they point out that New Brunswick may demand the release of terms of the Nova Scotia contract from AHC as a condition of entering into negotiations. Respondent's counsel also echo the previously discussed points that the Omnibus Agreement is not AHC's information in any event, that it is the product of negotiations with the Province and that significant parts of the material have already been disclosed to the public.

 

A review of the wording used in the 'harm of disclosure' subsection makes it clear that the legislature seeks evidence of more than the possibility of some loss; it requires that it be shown that the information "reasonably" be expected to "harm significantly" or "interfere significantly" in subsection (i) and "result in undue financial loss" in subsection (iii). Such modifiers would seem to imply that the legislature requires a logically and rationally based threshold of ‘speculative proof’ of ‘harm’ or damages of some substance.

 

I am not persuaded AHC has overcome the hurdles listed above, including the burden of demonstrating that it is their information. Although I am satisfied that the release of the information may be expected to cause one or both of the categories of harm to AHC, they have not satisfied me on the evidence to the extent that is required by subsection 21(1) that there is a sufficient prospect of the degree of harm that is contemplated by that subsection.

 

I should note that counsel for AHC has, during the course of this hearing, stated that he is limited in argument in the extent of the references he can make to the relevant parts of the Omnibus Agreement. He indicates this has hampered his ability to make the full argument he could make in relation to the specific material his client wishes to protect and to the extent of harm it could suffer. This particular type of legislation often is limiting in its application because briefs and argument run the risk of disclosure of the very information sought to be protected. The court has by various means sought to lessen this risk by the use of the tools available to it under the Act and by other procedural practices.

 

 

5.         CONCLUSION

 

Throughout this appeal runs the basic debate as to whether a contract negotiated by a government authority can be kept from the public because the release of it might cause significant harm to the other contracting party.

 

The Review Officer in his written reasons for his recommendation concluded that a private company cannot expect to keep private the information contained in an agreement signed with government, particularly when public funds are involved. I confess to some difficulty with this broad statement as there may be rare circumstances where it could be in the public interest to do so. For example if such a contract also involved other protected information of the Act such as certain personal information. However, the general statement is valid in most circumstances as it reflects the right of citizens to be informed of the use of public funds. The obvious danger is the use of the protection of 'commercial information' as a shield to keep from the public the information necessary to properly assess government acts. Here, of course, the government authority was willing to release the information, and the party contracting with government seeks to shield it from public view. The broad terms of the debate by counsel is whether AHC's interest in protecting information in the Omnibus Agreement from its competitors so exceeds the public right to know the details of this transaction that the information should be restricted under the Act.

 

I would also comment that AHC has not specifically argued that the court consider restricting part of the Omnibus Agreement and release the balance. This is a possible alternative under the Act, but only where such severance does not significantly affect the essence of the document. In any event, it would appear that the parts of this document that the appellant is most concerned with here are significant and appear to be essential to the understanding of the public of the overall contractual scheme.

 

This Act is an important part of the ongoing process of improving the democratic process in this Province. The past decisions of this jurisdiction and other jurisdictions have supported the basic purpose of this legislation, to provide protection to certain specified information that deserve privacy, and then to ensure the public has the information necessary to make an informed assessment of the performance of its government institutions.

 

In this appeal the Appellant has not satisfied me that the information it seeks to protect so clearly fits within any of the restricted categories of the Act that the public should not have access to the important information that is contained in the Omnibus Agreement.

 

The Respondents shall have their costs in this appeal in the amount of $1,000.00 each.
