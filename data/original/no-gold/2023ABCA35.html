The Court:

[1]               The appellant appealed property tax assessments on 22 multi-family residential buildings. The appeals raised similar issues, and were decided together by the Edmonton Composite Assessment Review Board. The appeals were unsuccessful, as was the subsequent application for judicial review of those decisions: Altus Group Ltd v Edmonton Composite Assessment Review Board, 2021 ABQB 322.

Facts

[2]               One of the appeals, which is representative of the 21 other appeals, was an appeal of the 2018 annual property tax assessment of a high-rise residential building in downtown Edmonton: Altus Group v The City of Edmonton, 2018 ECARB 01458. The property tax assessment on that property was set at $29,358,000, based on multiplying a Gross Income Multiplier of 12.65 times the Effective Gross Income for the building. The appellant argued before the Review Board that the Gross Income Multiplier was too high, and that it should be reduced to 11.31, resulting in an assessed value of $26,248,395.

[3]               The appellant supported its reduced Gross Income Multiplier by comparing the assessed property to a number of adjusted comparable sales. The City responded with a competing set of comparable sales, and argued that the Gross Income Multiplier it used resulted in a fair and equitable assessment. The parties challenged each other’s comparables and the adjustments made to them, and argued about the circumstances underlying various sales presented as comparable, together with the effect of economic conditions in Alberta.

[4]               The Review Board considered the evidence and arguments on the 22 appeals globally. The City had conceded that downward adjustments should be made with respect to 17 of the properties, because its own comparables suggested a lower Gross Income Multiplier was appropriate. The reductions conceded by the City were not, however, to the extent proposed by the appellant. Generally, the Review Board preferred the assessor’s approach:

[34] An analysis of the sales used by the Complainant from both a quality and quantity perspective shows that the Complainant’s requested GIM [Gross Income Multiplier] of 11.31 does not produce an accurate estimate of market value. Four of the six sales used by the Complainant to derive the 11.31 GIM are not reliable indicators of market value.

The Review Board discussed in more detail the comparative sales put forward by each party. The Review Board confirmed at para. 21 that “equity is not an issue in this complaint”.

[5]               The Review Board accepted the City’s recommended reductions with respect to the 17 properties, but otherwise affirmed the original assessments.

[6]               The appellant applied for judicial review under s. 470 of the Municipal Government Act, RSA 2000, c. M-26. The judicial review judge reviewed the Matters Relating to Assessment and Taxation Regulation, 2018, AR 203/217, noting that assessments must be based on market value:

5. An assessment of property based on market value

(a)        must be prepared using mass appraisal,

(b)        must be an estimate of the value of the fee simple estate in the property, and

(c)        must reflect typical market conditions for properties similar to that property.

An assessment review board must not alter an assessment that is fair and equitable, having regard to the provisions of the regulation and the assessments of similar properties in the same municipality: Municipal Government Act s. 467(2).

[7]               The reviewing judge concluded that the Board’s decisions were reasonable using the test in Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration) v Vavilov, 2019 SCC 65, [2019] 4 SCR 653:

3 I dismiss Altus’ application. I agree with the City that Altus is essentially rearguing the merits of the case and asking the Court to override the Review Board’s expertise and substitute a different decision. The Review Board’s decision that the actual sale price of the properties did not reflect the market value was reasonable on the evidence before the Board and supported by the legal framework. The decision of the Review Board to reject the sales comparables put forward by Altus is justifiable, intelligible, and transparent on the evidence before the Board. Finally, before the Review Board, Altus did not argue that the City’s proposed reduction of the assessed value for 17 properties was arbitrary. I decline to consider the issue as it was not raised before the Review Board.

The application for judicial review was accordingly dismissed, resulting in this appeal.

Issues and Standard of Review

[8]               The appellant raises the following issues:

1. Did the Board err by:

(a) conflating the independent principles of equity and market value;

(b) rendering the statutory standard of “market value” and its definition devoid of any meaning;

(c) creating an impossible test for a taxpayer to meet by holding that sales transactions must meet the City’s criteria of “typical”; and

(d) finding that a right to complain against an assessment does not allow for a challenge to a municipality’s assessment model?

2. Did the Chambers Judge err in refusing to consider an issue raised on judicial review on the arbitrariness of the Board’s acceptance of City recommended Gross Income Multipliers on the basis that the recommendations were not challenged at the Board, when in fact the substance of the hearing was that the Gross Income Multipliers were wrong?

3. Did the Chambers Judge err in the application of the standard of review and are the Board’s decisions unreasonable?

[9]               It is common ground that the standard of review of the Board’s decisions is reasonableness, as described in Vavilov. A reasonable decision is one that is based on an internally coherent and rational chain of analysis, and that is justified in relation to the facts and law that constrain the decision maker, and exhibits the requisite degree of justification, intelligibility and transparency: Vavilov at paras. 85, 99-107. To be reasonable, the analysis must be rational and logical, and demonstrate a line of reasoning that leads to the ultimate conclusion. The outcome must also be reasonable. It must be justified in relation to the constellation of law and facts that are relevant to the decision: Vavilov at para. 105.

[10]           This is a process of “review”, marked by judicial restraint and respect for the mandate and specialized expertise of decision makers: Vavilov at para. 75. The court is not to start by re-making the decision de novo, or deciding what decision it would have made, or deciding what decision would have been “correct”: Vavilov at paras. 83, 116, 124.

[11]           The appellant argues that a taxpayer is entitled to an assessment that is “both correct and equitable”. That is a fair statement of the objective, but on judicial review the issue is the reasonableness of the Review Board’s determination that the assessment is correct and equitable.

Market Value Assessments

[12]           The appellant’s first ground of appeal challenges several aspects of the Board’s approach to determining “market value” for the purposes of assessments. The appellant frames this as being a competition between “the dual statutory standards of equity and market value, and the appropriate interpretation when those two standards conflict”.

[13]           With limited exceptions, the ultimate goal of the assessment process is to determine the market value of the subject property. The Municipal Government Act provides:

s. 1(1)(n) “market value” means the amount that a property, as defined in section 284(1)(r), might be expected to realize if it is sold on the open market by a willing seller to a willing buyer;

This is a conventional definition of market value, and its application is further defined by the Matters Relating to Assessment and Taxation Regulation, 2018:

1 In this Regulation, . . .

(g) “mass appraisal” means the process of preparing assessments for a group of properties using standard methods and common data and allowing for statistical testing; . . .

5 An assessment of property based on market value

(a) must be prepared using mass appraisal,

(b) must be an estimate of the value of the fee simple estate in the property, and

(c) must reflect typical market conditions for properties similar to that property.

6 Any assessment prepared in accordance with the Act must be an estimate of the value of a property on July 1 of the assessment year.

7(1) The valuation standard for a parcel of land is

(a) market value, or

(b) if the parcel is used for farming operations, agricultural use value. . . .

9(1) When an assessor is preparing an assessment for a parcel of land and the improvements to it, the valuation standard for the land and improvements is market value . . .

There does not appear to be any obvious distinction between an assessment at market value and a “valuation standard” based on market value.

[14]           The use of the mass appraisal method is mandated by s. 5(a), but the overall objective of
s. 5 is still the “assessment of property based on market value”. The City acknowledges that the mass appraisal method is used by the assessor to prepare assessments, but does not apply to the Review Board’s review of an assessment following a complaint. The use of the mass appraisal method to assess based on market value, having regard to “typical market conditions”, does not displace the standard of “market value” with a standard of “average values”, “typical market prices” or “typical market values”. It follows that the Board is not obliged to look at “typical market values” if cogent, uncontroverted evidence of market value exists.

[15]           Both s. 5 and s. 6 of the Regulation refer to “estimates” of market value, reflecting the fact that it is often impossible to determine the exact value of a property at a given point in time. There may well be a range of market values that could be justified, and the Review Board’s confirmation of an assessment value in that range will not be disturbed on judicial review. However, the task is still to determine market value on the valuation date for each property, not a range. And where there has been an actual timely sale of the subject property, then it is possible to set the exact market value.

[16]           The concept of assessments based on market value is also tempered by what is called the “equity” principle. The purpose of the assessment process is to spread the tax burden fairly across the properties in the municipality, based on their relative value. The Municipal Government Act provides:

s 467(3) An assessment review board must not alter any assessment that is fair and equitable, taking into consideration

(a) the valuation and other standards set out in the regulations,

(b) the procedures set out in the regulations, and

(c) the assessments of similar property or businesses in the same municipality.

What is “fair and equitable” is a matter of discretion and judgment.

[17]           The appellant argues that the assessment method used by the City assessor, and upheld by the Review Board, does not comply with the enactments. It argues that the 22 challenged assessments are not based on market value, but on artificial values resulting from the unauthorized use of the mass appraisal method to develop “average” or “typical” values that are not market values. The appellant particularly points to the Review Board’s treatment of comparable market sales as being unreasonable and unexplained.

[18]           The appellant argues that the Review Board’s decisions do not satisfy “the dual statutory standards of equity and market value”, they do not give distinct consideration to “equitable assessment versus a correct assessment”, and they “conflate the issues of equity and market value assessments”. However, in the decisions under review the Review Board stated that “equity is not an issue”. The Review Board never expressly contrasted a market value assessment with an equitable assessment, and appears to have proceeded on the basis that “market value” was the only issue in play. The real issue appears to be an allegedly overly deferential attitude towards the City’s assessments, and an implied obligation on the taxpayer to show that any sale data inconsistent with the City’s assessment is “typical”.

The Chappelle Boulevard Assessment

[19]           The appellant’s primary argument is that the Board unreasonably failed to consider recent actual sales as being the best indicators of market value. For example, one of the 22 properties (located on Chappelle Boulevard) sold in June 2017 for $27,350,000 (a Gross Income Multiplier of 10.82) but its market value for assessment purposes as of July 1, 2017, only a few weeks later, was set at $30,916,500 (a Gross Income Multiplier of 12.64). The assessment of this property was one of the ones challenged, and this sale was also used as a comparable in the other complaints.

[20]           The Board’s decision on the assessment for the Chappelle Boulevard property is reported as Altus Group v The City of Edmonton, 2018 ECARB 01878:

[37] The Complainant’s sale #1 at 1071 Chappelle Blvd SW and sale #5 at 2910 141 Street SW are good indicators of value for the subject property. However, a sample size of two is not large enough to warrant a change to the assessed GIM.

[38] The Complainant’s sales are limited to a one-year period prior to the valuation date with sale dates between June 22, 2016 and June 9, 2017. While it is ideal to have comparable sales that occurred close to the valuation date, if the sample size is too small it can be enlarged through the use of older, time adjusted sales as the Respondent has done without compromising the analysis.

[39] In determining whether the sale price of the subject property represents market value, the Board is guided by the Matters Relating to Assessment and Taxation Regulation, 2018 AR 203/2017 section 5(c) which states that an assessment of property based on market value must reflect typical market conditions for properties similar to that property.

[40] While the sale price of the subject property is a good indicator of market value it is not, by itself, sufficient to set the assessment. In this case, the sale price does not reflect market conditions. The Board reviewed the Complainant’s two remaining comparables and the Respondent’s six comparables. When taking into account the differences in the properties including suite mix, suite size and location, the Board is satisfied that the 12.53 median GIM of the six comparables provided by the Respondent is a more reliable indicator of market value.

[41] The Board accepts the Respondent’s recommended value of $30,916,500 with a GIM of 12.23 based on the Respondent’s six sales. The Respondent recognized that the assessed GIMs for this building type were slightly higher than the sale GIMs of similar properties which led to the recommendation.

In short, the Board recognized that the actual sale price was a “good indicator of value”, but it concluded that a “sample size of two” was too small to gauge market value, and that overall the sale price did not “reflect typical market conditions” as required by s. 5(c) of the Regulation. This was one of the 17 properties where the City conceded that the assessment should be reduced, using a Gross Income Multiplier of 12.23 but otherwise the complaint was dismissed.

[21]           Determining the value of assessed properties and potential comparables is within the expertise of the Review Board, but the Board’s treatment of the Chappelle Boulevard sale does not demonstrate an internally coherent and rational chain of analysis supported by the facts and law that constrained its decision.

[22]           First of all, the Review Board’s concern about “sample sizes” and averages overlooks the point that in this complaint the Chappelle Boulevard sale was not a “comparable”. This was an actual sale of the very property being assessed, a sale that closed, after a year-long marketing, within weeks of the valuation date of July 1, 2018. Its “suite mix, suite size and location” were what they were; no adjustments were needed. The fact that the Chappelle Boulevard property only sold once that month is irrelevant. When reviewing a timely actual sale of the very property being assessed a sample size of “one” is the most compelling evidence of value. There is nothing to “average”.

[23]           Secondly, the Review Board’s conclusory statement that: “In this case, the sale price does not reflect market conditions” is, at a minimum, not transparent. If it is supported by this record, the Review Board did not explain why. The evidence was that this sale was an arm’s-length sale at the relevant date between a sophisticated buyer and seller, at a price squarely within the definition of “market value” in s. 1(1)(n). Absent any evidence of a distortion in the marketplace, such a sale is presumptively representative of “market conditions”: 697604 Alberta Ltd. v Calgary (City of), 2005 ABQB 512 at paras. 21, 24, 11 MPLR (4th) 295. The City argues that: “There is no legal or evidentiary rule requiring the sale price of a property to equate to its market value”, but s. 1(1)(n) is exactly such a rule. Unless there is some evidence that the sale was not an open market or arm’s length sale, the Review Board cannot just dismiss it as not reflecting market conditions.

[24]           The City argues that this sale was at a price lower than those of adjusted comparable sales in previous years. The appellant replied that the prices for multi-family residential buildings had been falling. Market value assessments are set on an annual basis, they are not fixed, and they do not invariably increase. Describing a sale as not being “typical” begs the question of the standard being applied. In a falling market a sale is not “atypical” just because it is lower than the City’s expectations, sales in previous years, or sale prices of different properties by different sellers at different times. The mass appraisal method does not allow the assessor to apply a historic Gross Income Multiplier just to maintain assessment values that prevailed in previous years. As the City acknowledged, its role is to determine the most accurate assessment of market value, not the highest possible assessment. The Review Board never grappled with these issues, and appears to have lost sight of the benchmark of “market value”.

[25]           Occasionally an arm’s-length sale by a willing seller to a willing purchaser (s. 1(1)(n)) would not occur under “typical market conditions” (R. 5(c)). A distress sale by an insolvent vendor may be an example. However, if there was something atypical about the “conditions” surrounding the Chappelle Boulevard sale it was incumbent on the Review Board to identify them before just dismissing the sale price as not being a clear indication of market value. The objective was still to determine market value, not “typical” value. “Typical conditions” relates to the marketplace or the transaction, not the price itself.

[26]           The City argues that the sale price of the Chappelle Boulevard property was “uncharacteristically low”. But compared to what? The statute “trusts the market” by defining market value in s. 1(1)(n) as the price that might be expected to be realized in a sale on the open market by a willing seller to a willing buyer. Unless the City can point to some anomaly in the market or the transaction the price achieved “characterizes” the market value. Just because the price does not fit the City’s assumptions or data, or is less than a previous assessed value, does not entitle the City to reject it as “atypical” or “uncharacteristic”. As the appellant points out, this approach sets an impossible standard for a complainant, because any data presented that contradicts the City’s data is just dismissed as not reflecting “typical market conditions”.

[27]           In conclusion, the Review Board’s treatment of the Chappelle Boulevard property is not justified in relation to the facts and law that constrained its decision, and the reasons do not exhibit the requisite degree of justification, intelligibility and transparency. If the Review Board thought there was some justification for its conclusion that this sale did “not reflect market conditions” it was incumbent on the Board to explain why. Some convincing justification is needed for assessing a property in excess of its actual sale price. The Chappelle Boulevard decision must be set aside.

Chappelle Boulevard as a Comparable

[28]           In other appeals the Chappelle Boulevard property was used as a comparable, for example in Altus Group v The City of Edmonton, 2018 ECARB 01458 where the Board concluded:

[37] The Complainant’s sale #1 at 1071 Chappelle Blvd SW and sale #5 at 2910 141 Street SW are good indicators of value for the subject property. However, a sample size of two is not large enough to warrant a change to the assessed GIM.

[38] The Complainant’s sales are limited to a one-year period prior to the valuation date with sale dates between June 22, 2016 and June 9, 2017. It is ideal to have comparable sales that occurred close to the valuation date; however, if the sample size is too small it can be enlarged through the use of older, time adjusted sales as the Respondent has done without compromising the analysis.

[39] While the Respondent’s additional market comparable is inferior to the subject property, it has a sale GIM of 12.11 which demonstrates a reasonable relationship between the sale GIM and the assessed GIM of 12.65.

While the Board acknowledged that the actual sale price of the Chappelle Boulevard property was a good indicator of value, it concluded that when considered with all the other evidence the assessment was reasonable.

[29]           As just noted (supra, paras. 19-27), the Review Board’s treatment of the Chappelle Boulevard transaction is problematic. When it is used as a comparable, it is acceptable to adjust it for time and other factors, but it must still be regarded as an accurate measurement of the value of the Chappelle Boulevard property on the date of the transaction.

[30]           The Board’s reference to a “sample size” is unclear, as it suggests a search for an “average” value, not a market value. The mass appraisal method is still a search for market value. The accepted way of dealing with comparables is to adjust them individually for time, age, condition, location and other relevant factors, not just to “average” all the prices of comparable sales. It is unclear if the assessor was using the past average value or Gross Income Multiplier of all multi-family properties as a standard or “typical” value, and then requiring a property owner to justify a variation from that average. The mass appraisal method allows the use of “common data”, but it does not negate the objective of setting a market value for individual properties. That may be an acceptable way to set an assessment based on mass appraisals, but once an individual property owner challenges an assessment the Review Board’s task is to assess its market value: Mountain View County v Alberta (Municipal Government Board), 2000 ABQB 594 at paras. 21, 25, 86 Alta LR (3d) 269, 272 AR 123.

The Boardwalk REIT Portfolio Sale

[31]           Before the Review Board the appellant relied on six comparable sales. Three of them were a sale about one year before the valuation date of a portfolio of three buildings to Boardwalk REIT. The assessments of these properties were also among the 22 complaints.

[32]           In the complaints of the assessments of these properties, the Review Board did not reduce the assessments to their 2016 sale price, stating in 2018 ECARB 01900 at para. 40:

The Board finds that the transaction of the subject property does not represent a sale under typical market conditions because it was part of a portfolio sale that did not have a market value allocation for each of the sold properties. The reported sale prices were based on an average sale price per suite without regard to the variables which affect the assessment value.

The Review Board treated the Boardwalk REIT sales as comparables in the same way in each other decision under review:

The Complainant’s sale #3 . . . sale #4, . . . and sale #6 . . . constitute a portfolio sale which sold for $93,019,750. The values stated in the transfer documents for each of the three properties were based on $182,750 per suite without regard to market area, suite mix, suite size or any other variable that may impact market value. [2018 ECARB 01458 at para. 36]

The Review Board’s conclusion was: “Four of the six sales used by the Complainant to derive the 11.31 GIM are not reliable indicators of value”. Apart from this conclusory statement, there is no indication what weight, if any, the Review Board gave to these comparables.

[33]           The City did not dispute that the Boardwalk REIT portfolio sale was an arm’s length sale between a sophisticated buyer and seller, and did put some weight on it in explaining its concessions on some of the values. The properties were marketed nationally by CBRE and were available for purchase individually or separately. The fact that three properties were sold as part of a portfolio did not affect the price. There is no indication in the reasons that the Review Board did not accept that this transaction was a recent open market sale by a willing seller to a willing buyer at “market value” as defined in s. 1(1)(n). There is no indication the sale did not occur under typical market conditions, even if the properties were eventually sold together.

[34]           The only reservation about this sale was the observation by the Review Board that the same unit price was allocated to each of the three properties in the portfolio “without regard to market area, suite mix, suite size or any other variable that may impact market value”. The appellant’s evidence was that this type of revenue property sold at a multiple of cash flow, without regard to these factors. If the Review Board rejected that explanation, it never explained why, or gave any other indication why these sales were “not reliable indicators of value” for any of the 22 properties whose assessments were challenged. Further, the Review Board’s objection to the averaging of the price data appears to be inconsistent with its approach to the Chappelle Boulevard property, where it implied averaging of sale prices was acceptable if the sample size was large enough.

[35]           As the appellant points out, the Review Board’s concern with “market area, suite mix, suite size or any other variables” in the Boardwalk REIT portfolio is also inconsistent with its refusal to consider the site-specific sale of the Chappelle Boulevard property.

[36]           The absence of an explanation of why the actual sale price of these buildings was not compelling evidence of their market value reveals many of the same issues as arose in the analysis of the Chappelle Boulevard sale. Further, even if the portfolio nature of the Boardwalk REIT transaction had some impact on it as a useful comparable, that did not justify ignoring it completely. If necessary, the price could have been allocated differently between the three properties, or other relevant adjustments could have been made. However, at the end of any adjustments to the price paid for the individual properties, the value for the portfolio would still have added up to $93,019,750, and the average Gross Income Multiplier would have been unchanged. Why this transaction was not compelling evidence of value is not reasonably explained.

The Remaining Properties

[37]            In summary, the Review Board’s reasons give no explanation for the weight or treatment given to these comparables that is justified in relation to the facts and law that constrained its decision. The same erroneous approach tainted all of the 22 assessments under appeal. Further, the Review Board’s reasons on this topic are lacking in transparency.

Systemic Controls

[38]           The judicial review judge noted that there are a number of controls built into the assessment system. For example, the Matters Relating to Assessment and Taxation Regulation, 2018 provides:

1 In this Regulation, . . .

(d) “assessment ratio” means the ratio of the assessment to an indicator of market value for a property; . . .

Section 14(3) provides that the median assessment ratio for any stratum of property must be between 0.950 and 1.050, with a coefficient of dispersion between 0 and 20.0. Section 22 allows the Minister to conduct audits of the system.

[39]           These systemic standards are important tools in ensuring that the assessment system as a whole is operating fairly. They do not, however, displace or dilute the right of a property owner to complain about the assessment of an individual property under s. 460 of the Act. An inaccurate assessment cannot be justified on the basis that the system as a whole is operating within the statistical parameters permitted by the Regulation: 534 Capital Corp. v Calgary (City), CARB 79715P-2015 at para. 21.

The City’s Concession

[40]           As noted, the City conceded that a modest reduction was warranted for the assessments of 17 of the 22 properties. While it generally upheld the methodology behind all 22 assessments, the Review Board accepted this concession and reduced the assessments on the 17 properties, but not to the extent proposed by the appellant. The appellant argues that this was arbitrary, anomalous, and inconsistent with the standard in Vavilov.

[41]           In an adversarial context it is not unreasonable for a tribunal to adopt a consent resolution of the dispute, or accept concessions made by one side or the other: Vavilov at para. 94. Settlement is to be encouraged, and the tribunal does not have to insist that the parties fight every issue to the end. Recognizing a concession does not oblige the tribunal to accept the rationalization for the concession. At the hearing the appellant did not call on the Review Board to reject this concession and proceed on an “all or nothing” basis. The City’s concession did not meet in full the appellant’s demands but it was not unreasonable for the Review Board to give effect to it.

Conclusion

[42]           In conclusion, the Review Board’s decisions are not reasonable or transparent, and all 22 complaints must be set aside. The appeal is allowed. The 2018 assessment of the Chappelle Boulevard property is to be adjusted to $27,350,000; that is the only reasonable outcome justified on this record: Vavilov at para. 142. The 2018 assessments of the other 21 properties are remitted back for reconsideration.
