Manitoba Court of Appeal

Manitoba Hydro Electric v. John Inglis Co.

Date: 1999-11-24

R.P. Sokalski and J.A. Baigrie, for appellants, John Inglis Co. and Inglis Ltd./Inglis Ltée.

P.B. Forsyth and K.T. Williams, for appellants, English Electric Co., English Electric Co. of Canada, Ltd., and GEC Alsthom International Canada Inc.

L.W. Bowles and J.J. Burnell, for respondent, Manitoba Hydro Electric Board.

Court File Nos. AI98-30-03996; AI98-30-03997

By the Court:—

Introduction

[1]                This appeal deals with an application by the respondent The Manitoba Hydro Electric Board (Hydro) under The Limitation of Actions Act, C.C.S.M., c. L150 (the Act) for the extension of limitation periods, in contract and in tort, with respect to a major explosion that occurred in one of its hydro generating stations in March of 1992.

[2]                The genesis of this action is found in the construction of a power generating plant by Hydro at Grand Rapids in Manitoba in 1963.

[3]                The motions court judge's descriptions of the appellants, which we adopt, are: a) John Inglis Co. Limited, Inglis Limited/ Inglis Limitée (John Inglis), a corporation which was registered under The Companies Act, R.S.M. 1940, c. 36, of Manitoba on November 13, 1946 and which on April 24, 1973, by amendment of articles changed its name to Inglis Limited/Inglis Limitée. John Inglis is described in the contract underlying this litigation as "John Inglis Co. Limited, English Electric Canada Division, a corporation with head office in the City of Toronto in the Province of Ontario and licensed and registered to carry on business in the Province of Manitoba"; and b) English Electric Company Limited (English Electric), which is a company incorporated in the United Kingdom. It is the beneficial owner of English Electric Company of Canada, Limited, now known as GEC Alsthom International Canada Inc.

[4]                In 1963, Hydro contracted with the appellant John Inglis to design and manufacture three turbines for the generating plant it was proposing to construct at Grand Rapids. The turbines were designed and developed essentially by English Electric, and manufactured by John Inglis.

[5]                Hydro issued a completion certificate on July 1, 1967, triggering the guarantee period under the contract of one year for all components other than the stay vanes and the runner blades. Significant problems had developed with respect to the stay vanes and runner blades that had been the subject of ongoing discussion and review among the parties. By supplementary agreement dated July 14, 1967, between Hydro and John Inglis, it was agreed that a 36-month guarantee period would apply with respect to the stay vanes and the runner blades.

[6]                On the same day, English Electric guaranteed to Hydro the performance by John Inglis,

… of the extended guarantee granted by the Supplementary Agreement dated as of July 14, 1967 between The Manitoba Hydro Electric Board and John Inglis Co. Limited.

[7]                On March 10, 1992, an explosion occurred which seriously damaged the outer head cover on one of the turbines. This allowed water and oil to escape into the powerhouse. Hydro claims that it suffered a loss by reason of this occurrence, including loss of profits, totalling approximately $30,000,000.

[8]                It is Hydro's position that the limitation period in tort could not have expired before the incident occurred since it could not have been discovered until the time of the failure. For the same reason, Hydro claims the limitation period in contract also had not yet expired. Hydro, on March 9, 1993, filed an application seeking an order that the limitation period did not commence to run until March 10, 1992, or in the alternative that it be granted an extension of time in which to commence an action against the respondents for damages allegedly suffered as a result of the failure. The application was supported by affidavit evidence.

[9]                As a result of an earlier decision of this Court and with the consent of all parties, Hydro's application was converted into a trial of the issue and the matter proceeded to hearing. Eventually, leave was granted to commence an action in tort and contract against John Inglis and English Electric and a declaration issued that the action was not statute-barred by the Act.

[10]           The facts on which Hydro based its application are set out in paras. 30 and 31 of the reasons of the motions court judge MacInnes J. [reported 128 Man. R. (2d) 199]:

The evidence adduced on behalf of Hydro was to the effect that there were substantial vibrations in unit 1 which occurred shortly after the installation and commencement of operation of that unit and that these vibrations caused cracks to occur in the studs which secured the outer head cover of unit 1. Over time, Hydro asserts that these cracks propagated ultimately resulting in a sufficient decrease of strength so that they were no longer capable of holding the outer head cover in place. In the result, the outer head cover on unit 1 was blown off damaging equipment and permitting water and oil to flood the powerhouse and the generating station.

Hydro's evidence was that it did not perform any inspection of the outer head cover studs from the time of installation until the explosion because it expected the studs to last as long as or longer than the effective working life of the unit and they received nothing from the manufacturer suggesting otherwise, or recommending periodic inspection. Having said that, Hydro acknowledged that it could have inspected the outer head cover studs had it seen fit to do so and, that had it inspected and discovered cracked studs, it would in all probability simply have replaced the studs and by reason of the relatively insignificant cost of so doing, likely would not have advanced any claim against the manufacturer in that regard. Unfortunately, however, the cracked studs were not discovered before the March 10, 1992 explosion and the damage resulting was, of course, substantial and substantially more than replacing some head cover studs.

[11]           John Inglis and English Electric sought to call evidence to rebut Hydro's allegations. MacInnes J. heard evidence from John Inglis and English Electric on the understanding that he would rule at the conclusion of the hearing whether such evidence could properly be received in an application of this nature. The evidence as set out in para. 32 of the reasons provided the following:

They assert firstly, that there is no evidence, or no reliable evidence of excessive vibration which would have caused cracking of the head cover studs. As well, they dispute the suggestion of small cracks initiating many years ago and propagating over time, leading to the explosion on March 10, 1992. They also assert that Hydro, as part of its normal maintenance program, and/or in the course of the substantial maintenance that was performed by Hydro upon unit 1, on at least two occasions during the lifetime of the unit, ought to have inspected and if necessary replaced the outer head cover studs. In short, the respondents assert that you could not have a situation whereby small cracks would begin many years ago and propagate over time to failure, but rather that the cracking would have begun only within a short time before failure, and further that if the studs had cracked years ago and the cracks had propagated over time, these should have become known to Hydro well before the explosion on March 10, 1992 had a reasonable inspection and maintenance program been followed.

[12]           Eventually, MacInnes J. refused to receive the evidence.

[13]           MacInnes J. then went on to make the following findings with respect to the issues that were still before him:

(a) He found that Hydro's application was wide enough to include a claim in tort at paras. 27 and 28:

The point, however, is that I am satisfied that the allegations contained in paragraph 13 and 14 of the Law affidavit do constitute claims in tort, paragraph 13 being allegations that apply to both Inglis and E.E. England [English Electric], and paragraph 14, to the latter only.

I conclude, as well, that the additional allegations of faulty design and manufacture of the studs constitute further particulars of the causes of action raised in the application and do not raise a new cause of action.

(b) That Hydro satisfied the requirements of s. 14(1) of the Act at para. 34:

… it is my view based upon the evidence available to me for consideration on this application, that it was the explosion of March 10, 1992 that gave rise to Hydro's learning of the various facts relating to their proposed cause of action, which facts were material within the meaning of s. 20(2) of the Act. Hydro's discovery of these material facts of a decisive character clearly occurred within twelve months prior to March 9, 1993, the date of its application. Further, in my view, it was only the incident of March 10, 1992 that could reasonably have given rise to Hydro's discovering the alleged deficiencies of which it now complains and the nature and extent of the damages for which it now seeks to sue. I conclude, therefore, that Hydro has complied with s. 14(1) of the Act.

(c) And finally, that Hydro complied with s. 15(2) of the Act at para. 35:

Section 15(2) requires that the motions judge consider the evidence adduced by or on behalf of the claimant and determine whether that evidence, in the absence of any evidence to the contrary, would be sufficient to establish the proposed cause of action. In other words, the claimant must demonstrate a prima facie case. I am satisfied on the evidence that Hydro has done so.

The Limitation of Actions Act

a) Section 15(2) of the Act

[14]           Section 15(2) of the Act states:

15(2) Where an application is made under section 14 to begin or to continue an action, the court shall not grant leave in respect of the action unless, on evidence adduced by or on behalf of the claimant, it appears to the court that, if the action were brought forthwith or were continued, that evidence would, in the absence of any evidence to the contrary, be sufficient to establish the cause of action on which the action is to be or was founded apart from any defence based on a provision of this Act or of any other Act of the Legislature limiting the time for beginning the action. [Emphasis added.]

[15]           John Inglis disputes the decision of the motions court judge that it was not open to it to lead evidence to contradict the testimony and documentary evidence filed by Hydro in support of its application. Of the 22 days of viva voce testimony heard during the course of Hydro's application, approximately three days were taken up with the examination and cross-examination of Dr. Cahoon, an expert called by John Inglis. The motions court judge, at para. 15, eventually held the latter's evidence to be inadmissible, reasoning that:

In my view, the language of s. 15(2) is clear. Its intent is to ensure that if the court is going to grant leave to extend the limitation period and permit suit, such suit should not be on its face of dubious merit but rather prima facie should be a suit which has a reasonable prospect of success. But to allow the respondent to call evidence could, and in many instances would likely, result in the full blown introduction of evidence on this preliminary application, resulting in substantially increased costs while the parties effectively go through two trials rather than one, the first one simply being for the purpose of deciding whether leave will be granted. In addition this might also lead to the rather unseemly result of the motions judge deciding that there was a reasonable prospect of success and therefore granting leave, whereas the trial judge, on essentially the same evidence, might conclude that there was no merit and dismiss the claim.

[16]           English Electric did not proffer any evidence and took no position with respect to this issue.

[17]           In argument, John Inglis forcefully asserted that, despite the words "in the absence of any evidence to the contrary" in s. 15(2), MacInnes J. could not reasonably consider the evidence submitted on behalf of Hydro — consisting exclusively of expert evidence — or determine its sufficiency under s. 15(2) in the absence of the contradictory expert evidence adduced by John Inglis. Having accepted the expert testimony of Hydro's experts, Dr. Tennesey and Mr. Pawlikewich, the motions court judge, according to John Inglis, was left in a position where he could not realistically assess the weight and credit to be attached to their testimony in the absence of viva voce evidence from the respondents. Cross-examination, it was urged, is not enough. Otherwise, as dramatically put in the factum of John Inglis, "Rather than being a guardian of the gate on leave applications, the Court is reduced to the function of doorman".

[18]           Mr. Baigrie, who presented this portion of the argument on behalf of John Inglis, also emphasized the use of the word "may" in s. 14(1) of the Act. Section 14 is the key provision that, as we shall shortly see, creates the statutory right to an extension of the limitation period under the defined circumstances contained in the Act. He argued that the court has an inherent discretion which should have been exercised in favour of hearing all of the evidence on the critical issue of the likely success of Hydro's action if allowed to proceed.

[19]           Finally, the motions court judge himself in his reasons was of the view that in certain "rare cases" a respondent could call evidence if of a critical and decisive nature. Where, Mr. Baigrie asks, should one draw the line?

[20]           To all of this Hydro simply responds that the wording of s. 15(2) — with its clearly stated prohibition against consideration of "evidence to the contrary" to that called in support of the application — is determinative of the issue. Hydro notes that there are two different tests provided by Part II of the Act (set out in ss. 14(1) and 15(2)). With respect to the latter, the section mandates that the court is not to look at evidence tendered by or on behalf of the respondents in determining if there is a prima facie case. Once it is determined on evidence presented by the applicant that the threshold of a reasonably arguable case has been met, only then is the motions court judge in a position, pursuant to the broad discretion given by s. 14(1), to permit evidence to be tendered to assist in the exercise of the discretion given only by that provision.

[21]           We agree with Hydro's position. There is ample authority to support it. See: Rebizant v. Greenwood (1998), 127 Man. R. (2d) 35 (Q.B.); Budd v. Cardoso (1995), 106 Man. R. (2d) 41 (Q.B.); and Steffensen v. Canadian Tire Corp. (1996), 109 Man. R. (2d) 143 (Q.B.). If the respondents were permitted to call evidence with respect to the merits of the case at the time of the application, it would in effect become a trial itself; and if the application was successful then a second trial would be conducted. This could not have been the intention of the Legislature and is entirely inconsistent with the overall scheme of the Act.

b) Section 14(1) of the Act

[22]           Section 14(1) permits the court to grant leave to begin or continue an action:

14(1)… if it is satisfied on evidence adduced by or on behalf of the applicant that not more than 12 months have elapsed between

(a) the date on which the applicant first knew, or, in all the circumstances of the case, ought to have known, of all material facts of a decisive character upon which the action is based; and

(b) the date on which the application was made to the court for leave.

[23]           John Inglis and English Electric argue that the motions court judge erred in concluding that the proposed claim against John Inglis in contract and tort, and against English Electric in tort, had a reasonable prospect of success.

[24]           Strong reliance is placed by John Inglis on the requirements of s. 20(3) of the Act which provides as follows:

20(3) For the purposes of this Part, any of the material facts relating to a cause of action shall be taken, at any particular time, to have been facts of a decisive character if they were facts which a person of his intelligence, education and experience, knowing those facts and having obtained appropriate advice in respect of them, would have regarded at that time as determining, in relation to that cause of action, that, apart from any defence based on a provision of this Act or any other Act of the Legislature limiting the time for bringing an action, an action would have a reasonable prospect of succeeding and resulting in an award of damages or remedy sufficient to justify the bringing of the actions.

[25]           In order to satisfy s. 14(1) of the Act, the applicant must show three things: that it learned of facts that are "material" within the sense of s. 22, and of a "decisive" character within the meaning of s. 20(3); and that this knowledge was acquired during the twelve months immediately preceding the date of the application. Einarsson v. Adi's Video Shop (1992), 76 Man. R. (2d) 218 (C.A.) at para. 13.

[26]           Lack of awareness of the "nature or extent of the injuries or damages" suffered is one of the grounds for granting an extension of time pursuant to the Act. See Heaman v. Isaac (1993), 89 Man.R. (2d) 63 (Q.B.) at para. 9.

[27]           John Inglis argues that the motions court judge erred in precluding it from tendering viva voce evidence with respect to whether Hydro knew or ought to have known of the alleged fatigue problem in the studs more than twelve months prior to March 10, 1993 (the date of the application). But the only evidence tendered by John Inglis was that of Dr. Cahoon who opined that the studs fractured at the same time and in series and that there was no prior warning. This hardly comports with the theory now advanced by the respondents that Hydro should have known of the fatigue problem well before the incident of March 10, 1992. In our opinion, the motions court judge was entirely correct when he held at para. 33:

… having ruled the evidence of Dr. Cahoon inadmissible, there is no evidence for my consideration on the application which supports the respondents' position.

[28]           Realistically, this is the end of the matter, but we pass on to consider the remainder of John Inglis's submission, given the importance of the issue and the time devoted in argument to it.

[29]           Simply stated, John Inglis's argument is that Hydro, as a sophisticated and expert client, has the "intelligence, education and experience" referred to in s. 20(3) and either did inspect or ought to have inspected the 120 studs in question in light of their essential and critical function of maintaining the outer head cover in position. Such a procedure could readily have been undertaken. Indeed, John Inglis says the studs are in close proximity to other areas subject to vibration concerns and could have and should have been inspected by non-destructive testing.

[30]           Even more aggressively, John Inglis asserts that it is open for this Court to conclude that Hydro, during the 1980 and 1986 overhaul of Turbine No. 1, in fact removed the outer head cover and/or the outer head cover studs. It was suggested that Mr. Pawlikewich, Hydro's witness, had changed his testimony with respect to whether the outer head cover had been removed. As well, there was evidence that the O-ring, acting as a watertight seal, had been removed and replaced. This suggests that the outer head cover had also been removed (which would require removal of the studs). Thus, the respondents say, there is evidence that the motions court judge could have accepted demonstrating "intervention and removal" of the outer head cover.

[31]           English Electric joined in the argument presented by John Inglis, and submitted that Hydro's maintenance records and testimony show that Hydro made a number of judgment calls with respect to maintenance of the turbines and, having elected not to do its own inspections of the outer head cover studs, must now bear the consequences. This is especially significant since it was well known that the joint between the outer head cover and the stay ring is the weakest connection with respect to the head cover components.

[32]           As previously stated in para. 13(b) of these reasons, MacInnes J., after reviewing the evidence, concluded that it was the explosion of March 10, 1992 which permitted Hydro to learn of the various facts relating to the proposed cause of action and that Hydro had satisfied the requirements of s. 14(1) of the Act.

[33]           Having considered the strong representations made by the respondents that there was evidence that the motions court judge could have accepted to conclude that Hydro knew or ought to have known of the fatigue problem with the studs long before March 10, 1992, we respond in two different ways. Firstly, this is not our sense of the weight of the evidence. Secondly, and more importantly, while there may well be evidence that the motions court judge could have accepted to support the inferences and conclusions urged by the respondents, there was ample evidence to the contrary that he did accept, and properly so. There is ample evidence to support this finding, particularly given the limited scope of the motions court judge's function. That is the end of the matter.

The Contract Documents

[34]           As might be expected in a matter involving the construction of turbines for a major hydro-electric facility, the contractual documents are numerous and complex. In this case, they consist of specifications prepared by Hydro's consulting engineer (H. G. Acres & Company Limited), the successful tender of John Inglis and the Hydro acceptance, together with numerous appendices. Along the way, numerous discussions, on-site meetings and negotiations took place between John Inglis, the contractor, and Hydro during the course of the design and construction of the project, including the turbines. There can be no doubt that Hydro, John Inglis and English Electric were experienced and sophisticated parties possessing or having access to the very best expertise in engineering, legal and contractual matters.

[35]           The general conditions of the contract between Hydro and John Inglis, at para. 21, contain a guarantee, the pertinent parts of which are as follows:

If, within twelve months from the date of the Purchaser's Completion Certificate, any part of the work becomes broken or defective or fails due to faulty or improper design, material, workmanship, manufacture, fabrication, shipment, delivery, erection or installation, or fails to meet the requirements of the contract, then the Contractor, upon notification in writing from the Purchaser, shall, as soon as possible thereafter, make good every such breakage, defect or failure without cost (including transportation and installation costs) to the Purchaser.

. . . . .

The Contractor's liability under this Section shall be in lieu of any warranty or condition implied by law as to the quality, design or fitness of the work for any particular purpose, and save as in this Section expressed, the Contractor shall not be liable for defects in any work after the work has been taken over by the Purchaser or for any injury or damage resulting from any such defects.

Provided the Contractor is not otherwise in default under the terms of the contract, and subject to the provisions of Section[s] 11 and 22 hereof, the Contractor's liability in respect of the work, whether in contract, tort or otherwise, shall cease upon the fulfilment by the Contractor of the Contractor's obligations under this Section; provided further that any part of the work made good under this Section shall be subject to all the provisions of this Section for a further period of twelve months from the date when the same has been made good as aforesaid.

Where more than one unit section of equipment is included in the work, the said period of twelve months shall in respect of each unit section be deemed to commence on the date of the Purchaser's Completion Certificate issued in respect of such unit section of the work.

[36]           Under the specifications, "A7 — Responsibility of the Contractor", it provided:

The contractor shall guarantee and be responsible for:

(a) The quality of all materials and workmanship entering into the completed work.

[37]           As we have already seen, significant problems occurred following substantial completion of the construction of the turbines with the stay vanes and runner blades. Vibrations were detected and were a matter of ongoing and intense discussion and review between all three parties to this lawsuit.

[38]           Eventually, a compromise was reached. By supplementary agreement dated July 14, 1967, between Hydro and John Inglis, it was agreed that Hydro would issue a completion certificate dated July 1, 1967, qualified by an attached list of outstanding deficiencies (dealing essentially with the turbine stay vanes and runner blades). The practical result was that the guarantee under general condition 21 was effective for its intendwfed one-year period to June 30, 1968, for all components other than the stay vanes and the runner blades. With respect to the latter components only, a 36-month guarantee period was provided to expire June 30, 1970. The final paragraph of the supplementary agreement stated:

It is agreed that all the obligations and liabilities of the Contractor under or arising out of the contract have been satisfied and discharged and no further claims exist with respect thereto, except to the extent of completion of the matters set out in the attached list of outstanding deficiencies and to the extent of the Contract's [sic] obligations under the guarantee of the equipment referred to in paragraphs 2 and 3 above.

[39]           Concurrently, English Electric executed its own independent guarantee, which after reciting:

And Whereas by letter dated June 18th, 1965, the Chairman and Chief Executive of the English Electric Company Limited advised Mr. D.M. Stephens, Chairman of the Manitoba Hydro-Electric Board, as follows:

"… that the turbines in question have been designed and developed essentially by the English Electric Company Limited and that our Company has every confidence in them. Furthermore, it is our intention to stand firmly behind the contractual commitments entered into by John Inglis Co. Limited with Manitoba Hydro, which we regard as our own commitments …"

guaranteed to Hydro the performance by John Inglis,

…of the extended guarantee granted by the Supplementary Agreement dated as of July 14, 1967 between The Manitoba Hydro Electric Board and John Inglis Co. Limited.

[40]           The completion certificate dated July 1, 1967 was duly forwarded to John Inglis on September 20, 1967. Finally, on June 30, 1970, Hydro provided John Inglis with the written acknowledgement:

Please be advised that on the basis of satisfactory periodic inspections of the turbine blades on Units 1, 2, and 3 and stay vanes for Units 1, 2, 3 and 4 the latest of which was carried out this spring with yourself in attendance, your firm is now relieved of their obligations in connection with warranties on the units.

[41]           Clearly, the outer head cover and studs were not part of the three-year "extended warranty".

[42]           In a nutshell, John Inglis argues that it is entitled on and after twelve months had expired from June 30, 1967 to the protection afforded by the guarantee. It was "not otherwise in default" of the contract, and indeed had never been formally notified of a claim by Hydro until it received the application for the extension of the limitation period under Part II of the Act. The clause only makes sense, it is argued, if it means that any defect had to be brought to John Inglis's attention in writing within the first twelve months after the completion certificate was issued.

[43]           John Inglis says Hydro wanted "closure" and got it by virtue of the extended guarantee and the introduction of English Electric's individual guarantee. The sophisticated parties allocated the risk between themselves and the court should not disturb this arrangement at this late date. See: Ontario Hydro v. Manitoba Hydro-Electric Board (1997), 124 Man.R. (2d) 128 (Q.B.); and Fraser Jewellers (1982) Ltd. v. Dominion Electric Protection Co. (1997), 35 C.C.L.T. (2d) 298, 148 D.L.R. (4th) 496 (Ont. C.A.).

[44]           Therefore, says John Inglis, there is no realistic prospect that the action will succeed, since whatever may be the provisions of Part II of the Act, the completion certificate issued by Manitoba Hydro on June 30, 1970 is a complete bar to the success of Hydro's action. Part II of the Act can apply only to the statutory limitation period and cannot affect a separate and independent contract between John Inglis and Hydro which resulted in a complete release and satisfaction of John Inglis's contractual obligations.

[45]           Hydro's response to the question of the meaning of the guarantee (and release), contained in para. 21 of the general conditions, is to assert an interpretation that it says flows from the plain wording of the general conditions and other relevant documents when considered in the context of the purpose and object of the contract as a whole. Simply stated, Hydro says that so long as it can be demonstrated that the studs cracked or began to crack within the first year of operation (or presumably prior thereto), the guarantee applies. The twelve-month "limitation" in the guarantee applies, Hydro argues, only to the defect or failure itself and not to the notification requirement, for which no specific time is provided. Assuming in such circumstances that the requirement is that notice be given within a reasonable time, how then, Hydro submits, could notice possibly have been given prior to the explosion in March 1992 which was the first occasion on which Hydro knew, or could reasonably have known, of the defect? It is an unambiguous, straightforward provision which should be given its plain meaning, namely, that the guarantee applies if "any part of the work becomes defective … within twelve months". At a minimum, Hydro asserts the issue is one that is reasonably arguable, and may well be eventually dependent on the findings of fact at trial with respect to the origin and timing of the fatigue cracks in the studs.

[46]           This proffered interpretation by Hydro, if ultimately accepted, is a complete response to the argument advanced so strongly by John Inglis that the guarantee and release provision in para. 21 of the general conditions bars Hydro's action regardless of any other justification for an extension of the limitation period under Part II of the Act.

[47]           In R. v. Gladue, [1999] 1 S.C.R. 688, 171 D.L.R. (4th) 385, in a unanimous decision, the Supreme Court reiterated at para. 25:

… the proper construction of a statutory provision flows from reading the words of the provision in their grammatical and ordinary sense and in their entire context, harmoniously with the scheme of the statute as a whole, the purpose of the statute, and the intention of Parliament.

[48]           As noted by L'Heureux-Dubé J. in Manulife Bank of Canada v. Conlin, [1996] 3 S.C.R. 415, 139 D.L.R. (4th) 426 (in dissent, but not on this issue), at para. 40:

… the rules which govern the interpretation of deeds and contracts generally are essentially the same as the rules for statutory interpretation.

[49]           The principal interpretative rule for contracts was stated by La Forest and McLachlin JJ., in a joint decision for the majority, in BG Checo International Ltd. v. British Columbia Hydro and Power Authority, [1993] 1 S.C.R. 12 at pp. 23-24, 99 D.L.R. (4th) 577:

It is a cardinal rule of the construction of contracts that the various parts of the contract are to be interpreted in the context of the intentions of the parties as evident from the contract as a whole: K. Lewison, The Interpretation of Contracts (1989), at p. 124; Chitty on Contracts (26th ed. 1989), vol. I, at p. 520.

See as well Bow Valley Husky (Bermuda) Ltd. v. Saint John Shipbuilding Ltd., [1997] 3 S.C.R. 1210, 153 D.L.R. (4th) 385, at para. 28 per McLachlin J., and at para. 118 per Iacobucci J.; Hillis Oil & Sales Ltd. v. Wynn's Canada Ltd., [1986] 1 S.C.R. 57 at p. 66, 25 D.L.R. (4th) 649; and Consolidated-Bathurst Export Ltd. v. Mutual Boiler & Machinery Ins. Co., [1980] 1 S.C.R. 888 at p. 901, 112 D.L.R. (3d) 49, per Estey J.

[50]           To this end, a court should endeavour to read the clauses of a contract in such a way that they can be reconciled with each other. Canada Deposit Insurance Corp. v. Canadian Commercial Bank (1991), 79 Alta.L.R. (2d) 294 (C.A.), appeal dismissed [1992] 2 S.C.R. 3.

[51]           Similarly, the court may refer to surrounding circumstances at the time of the signing of the contract. See White v. Central Trust Co. (1984), 7 D.L.R. (4th) 236 (N.B.C.A.). Sometimes subsequent conduct can be admissible (at least in Canada) as a guide to the interpretation of a written contract. See Ottawa (City) v. Canadian National Railways, [1925] S.C.R. 494, [1925] 3 D.L.R. 762; Firestone Tire and Rubber Co. of Canada v. Canada (Commissioner of Income Tax), [1942] S.C.R. 476, [1942] 4 D.L.R. 433; Turvey & Mercer v. Lauder (1956), 4 D.L.R. (2d) 225 (S.C.C.); and Hayduk v. Waterton; Flechuk v. Waterton, [1968] S.C.R. 871, 68 D.L.R. (2d) 562; and G.H.L. Fridman, Q.C., The Law of Contract in Canada, 3rd ed. (Scarborough, Ont.: Carswell, 1994) at 464.

[52]           In this instance, the plain wording of the guarantee in the context of the contract as a whole certainly suggests that it applies to defects which occur within one year as opposed to defects which appear within one year. (An interesting comparison can be made to the facts in Hunter Engineering Co. v. Syncrude Canada Ltd., [1989] 1 S.C.R. 426, 57 D.L.R. (4th) 321 which led the Supreme Court to the opposite conclusion.) However, it can also be argued that the plain meaning interpretation of the guarantee has an effect contrary to the intentions of the parties given the subsequent conduct of the parties and the terms of the June 1970 release. The key issue will undoubtedly be whether the plain meaning of the guarantee should prevail, or whether it results in an absurdity or defeats the intentions of the parties. But it is clear to us that Hydro has presented a prima facie argument that the provisions of the "guarantee" should be interpreted in its favour.

The Contract and English Electric

[53]           There are two interrelated issues to be considered:

a) the nature and extent of English Electric's potential contractual obligation to Hydro in light of the terms of its guarantee of July 14, 1967 as interpreted in the "context" of the minutes, memoranda, and other documents leading up to its execution; and

b) whether English Electric is entitled to take advantage of the "release provisions" contained in the guarantee, general condition 21, of the contract specifications between Hydro and John Inglis, as a result of Hydro's letter of June 30, 1970 confirming that John Inglis's obligations under the contract had been satisfied.

[54]           Dealing with the first issue, as problems developed, caused by vibrations following the installation of the first turbine, much discussion, as we have already seen, took place involving John Inglis, English Electric, and Hydro, together with their consultants. (Turbine No. 1, the subject matter of this litigation, was installed and electricity first generated on March 20, 1965.) As early as June 1965, English Electric, in a letter to the chairman of Manitoba Hydro, confirmed:

… that the turbines in question have been designed and developed essentially by the English Electric Co., Ltd., and that our company has every confidence in them. Furthermore, it is our intention to stand firmly behind the contractual commitments entered into by John Inglis with Manitoba Hydro, which we regard as our own commitments.

[55]           The significance of this wording, of course, is that it is the very same as that incorporated in the July 14, 1967, guarantee from English Electric to Hydro. Notwithstanding this undertaking by English Electric, however, the documents make it clear that Hydro always looked to John Inglis to fulfill all its obligations arising out of the turbine contract. Concerns continued long after 1965 with respect to vibration in the turbine runner blades and stay vanes. Eventually, this led to the extended guarantee of English Electric as earlier reviewed. English Electric was never a party to the main contract between Hydro and John Inglis. Its sole contractual liability is therefore dependent upon the interpretation of its own guarantee to Hydro of July 14, 1967, in which it declared its "intention to stand firmly behind the contractual commitments" and then guaranteed the performance by John Inglis "of the extended guarantee" granted by the supplementary agreement dated July 14, 1967, between Hydro and John Inglis.

[56]           In examining the precise terms of the English Electric guarantee, it is significant that the advice given by the chairman of English Electric to the chairman of Hydro, made in 1965, that "it is our intention to stand firmly behind the contractual commitments [of John Inglis], which we regard as our own commitments" is contained within the preamble of the English Electric guarantee. The guarantee itself indicates that in consideration of Hydro issuing its completion certificate on July 1, 1967, English Electric guarantees to Hydro "the extended guarantee granted by the supplementary agreement bearing the same date made between Hydro and John Inglis" (underlining added). Scrutiny of the July 14, 1967 agreement between Hydro and John Inglis indicates that the only "extension" beyond the provisions of para. 21 of the general conditions is the three-year guarantee (as opposed to one year) for the stay vanes and runner blades.

[57]           What was extended by the supplementary guarantee between Hydro and John Inglis, in our opinion, was the period of the guarantee (from one year to three years) with respect to the runner blades and stay vanes and no more. This and this alone is what English Electric's agreement of July 14, 1967 refers to. By standing firmly behind John Inglis's contractual responsibilities, English Electric did not, in our opinion, assume any additional legal obligations.

[58]           We readily conclude that there is no prima facie or reasonably arguable case against English Electric in contract and the motions court judge was entirely correct in so deciding (see paras. 26 and 41 reasons for judgment).

[59]           Given this conclusion, it becomes self-evident, in our opinion, that English Electric is not entitled to rely upon the release provisions of the contractual guarantee, at para. 21 of the general conditions, consequent upon the confirmation (understandably directed only to John Inglis) by Hydro dated June 30, 1970 that John Inglis was relieved of any further obligations in connection with the warranties under the contract. Throughout the entire affair, including the intensive discussions and negotiations that led up to the execution of the agreement of July 14, 1967, between Hydro, John Inglis and English Electric, it is clear that, while English Electric was "standing behind" its design and John Inglis its sole contractual obligation was created by the extended guarantee of July 14, 1967.

[60]           In London Drugs Ltd. v. Kuehne & Nagel International Ltd., [1992] 3 S.C.R. 299, 97 D.L.R. (4th) 261, the Supreme Court in a landmark decision relaxed the strict rules of privity of contract beyond the established exceptions of agency and trust, so that employees of a corporation actually performing the contracted work were entitled to the protection of a limitation of liability clause despite the fact that they were not personally parties to the contract. This was because "commercial reality and common sense require that it should" (at p. 415). Critical to the creation of the new exception is the intention of the contracting parties. Thus, on the facts of London Drugs at p. 448:

… a threshold requirement for employees to obtain the benefit of their employer's contractual limitation of liability clause is the express or implied stipulation by the contracting parties that the benefit of the clause will also be shared by said employees.

[61]           This essential point was reiterated again in Edgeworth Construction Ltd. v. N.D. Lea & Associates Ltd., [1993] 3 S.C.R. 206, by McLachlin J. for the Court at pp. 216-17, 107 D.L.R. (4th) 169. (On the facts of Edgeworth Construction, the Court concluded that the contractual provisions and other surrounding circumstances were "entirely consistent" with the conclusion that the protection was not intended to be extended beyond the contracting party.)

[62]           Most recently in the decision of Fraser River Pile & Dredge Ltd. v. Can-Dive Services Ltd., [1999] S.C.J. No. 48 (QL) [now reported 176 D.L.R. (4th) 257], Iacobucci J., writing for a unanimous court, readily concluded that a waiver of subrogation provision entered into between sophisticated commercial clients, which expressly extended the benefit of such waiver to an ascertainable class of third-party beneficiaries, did confer the benefit of such a provision on the third parties who were expressly contemplated and included within the precise terms of the waiver provision.

[63]           Here careful examination of the contract, including the limitation of liability provision, makes it very clear that John Inglis alone is responsible for making good any defects (other than the extended guarantee of English Electric as just reviewed). There is no express language suggesting that the contract and limitation of liability clause was meant to benefit anyone else. Likewise, it does not appear that it can be implied that the limitation of liability clause was meant to benefit English Electric. The circumstances here are not analogous to those of employees actually charged with doing the work contemplated by the parties to the contract, as in London Drugs, or a specifically named beneficiary in a complex commercial arrangement as in Fraser River Pile & Dredge Ltd. Rather, similar to Edgeworth Construction, English Electric was a separate large professional firm which had the ability to protect itself in a variety of ways, if it chose to do so. Doubtless, it chose not to do so because of the very limited nature of its contractual relationship with Hydro.

[64]           Under these circumstances, it can hardly be gainsaid that by some mysterious reasoning English Electric became entitled to the protection of the very contractual provisions that it was so astute to avoid, simply because it guaranteed John Inglis's performance of the extended guarantee with respect to the stay vanes and runner blades.

[65]           In its factum, Hydro argued that "English Electric cannot, however, rely upon the limitation of liability to protect it from its own torts, committed independently of the contract". But this raises an entirely different issue from the question of English Electric's ability to seek the protection afforded by the provisions of the contract between Hydro and John Inglis.

Liability in Tort

[66]           We therefore turn to English Electric's potential liability in tort and the question whether an extension of time for such a potential cause of action was properly raised in Hydro's application.

[67]           English Electric, supported by John Inglis, argues that Hydro has not pleaded a cause of action in tort. This is an issue of particular significance to English Electric. As we have already observed, Hydro has no case against English Electric in contract apart from any claim that might have arisen under English Electric's limited guarantee given on July 14, 1967.

[68]           For the purpose of this issue, Hydro's application under s. 14(1) of the Act and the supporting affidavit of Robert Law constitute the "pleadings" which must be examined to determine whether or not material facts that raise a claim in tort have been alleged.

[69]           English Electric argues that by its application Hydro intended to seek leave to sue only in contract. Counsel for English Electric explain in their factum:

English Electric acknowledges that it was the intention of Hydro to sue in tort and contract eventually, but submits that Hydro only sought leave in respect of the causes of action framed in contract. … References were made to the fact and belief of Hydro that it had a right, separate from the Application, to bring forward an action in tort with respect to the 1992 failure. …Hydro represented that it was its intention to issue a claim in tort in any event and therefore wished to have the Application for leave determined in respect of the action in contract in order that they might be joined in one pleading.

This perception by Hydro changed dramatically as at July 8, 1997, when we suspect that some research on the issue of limitation periods threatened to undermine Hydro's confidence in the view that the limitation period in tort had not expired. Their concern … proved to be well founded, as recently clarified by this Court in Rarie v. Maxwell [(1998), 131 Man.R. (2d) 184], namely that Part II of the Act occupies the whole field of discoverability and that accordingly, judge-made discoverability has no application in Manitoba.

[70]           Hydro does not dispute the essential facts underlying that scenario and those opinions., nor could it in the face of correspondence between the parties and the case management memoranda that are part of the record. However, as we will see, it is the factual situation that is put forward, and not the label put on the facts, that will determine the sufficiency of the pleadings.

[71]           MacInnes J. observed, citing the comment of Diplock L.J. in Letang v. Cooper, [1964] 2 All E.R. 929 at 934 (C.A.), at para. 26, that "[i]t is clear that a cause of action is simply a factual situation, the existence of which entitles one person to obtain from the court a remedy against another person".

[72]           The issue of the sufficiency of pleadings that is raised in the context of this appeal is not one that has received a great deal of attention in Canadian decisions. However, in Scotsburn Co-operative Services Ltd. v. W.T. Goodwin Ltd., [1985] 1 S.C.R. 54, 16 D.L.R. (4th) 161, Dickson J. (as he then was), writing for the majority, cited at p. 65, without comment but with apparent approval, the English practice and authorities, including Re Vandervell's Trusts (No. 2), [1974] 3 All E.R. 205 (C.A.), in which Lord Denning M.R. had written at p. 213:

It is sufficient for the pleader to state the material facts. He need not state the legal result. If, for convenience, he does so, he is not bound by, or limited to, what he has stated. He can present, in argument, any legal consequence of which the facts permit.

[73]           Lord Denning M.R. had expressed the same principle in slightly different words in an earlier case, Karsales (Harrow), Ltd. v. Wallis, [1956] 2 All E.R. 866 (C.A.). He had written at p. 869:

… I have always understood in modern times that it is sufficient for a pleader to plead the material facts. He need not plead the legal consequences which flow from them. Even although he has stated the legal consequences inaccurately or incompletely, that does not shut him out from arguing points of law which arise on the facts pleaded.

[74]           Lord Denning's dicta in Re Vandervell's Trusts (No. 2) was applied in 384238 Ontario Ltd. v. Canada (1983), 8 D.L.R. (4th) 676 (F.C.A.) (leave to appeal refused, [1984] 1 S.C.R. v), and in R. v. Imperial General Properties Ltd., [1985] 1 F.C. 344, 16 D.L.R. (4th) 615 (C.A.) (leave to appeal refused, [1985] 1 S.C.R. ix, 16 D.L.R. (4th) 615n).

[75]           Not alluded to by the motions court judge are the recent Canadian cases which have established that a given wrong may give rise to two different forms of action, one in contract and the other in tort. See, for example, Central Trust Co. v. Rafuse, [1986] 2 S.C.R. 147, 31 D.L.R. (4th) 481, BG Checo International Ltd. v. British Columbia Hydro and Power Authority, and Winnipeg Condominium Corporation No. 36 v. Bird Construction Co., [1995] 1 S.C.R. 85, 121 D.L.R. (4th) 193. It is not necessary in this appeal to review and consider the trend of the law towards concurrency in tort and contract that these decisions have heralded. It is sufficient for our purposes to observe that in those decisions, the Supreme Court has recognized that a common law duty of care may arise from the facts pleaded that also support a claim for breach of contract.

[76]           These principles of law are not in dispute. The parties join issue on the motions court judge's application of the principles to the facts of the case.

[77]           MacInnes J., after stating the legal principle, reviewed specific paragraphs of Mr. Law's affidavit. He concluded at para. 27 "that the allegations contained in paragraph [sic] 13 and 14 … do constitute claims in tort, paragraph 13 being allegations that apply to both Inglis and E.E. England [English Electric], and paragraph 14, to the latter only".

[78]           We set out hereunder paras. 13 and 14 of Mr. Law's affidavit and, as well, para. 15, which, English Electric argues, "restates and summarizes the factors referred to in paragraphs 13 and 14 and establishes that they are in fact particulars of the breach of the Agreement by all of the Respondents".

13. Hydro has retained Dr. Wayne Tennesey, a metallurgist, and Alex Gerrard of Crippen, Acres, Wardrop, consulting engineers, to investigate the cause of the explosion. Both Dr. Tennesey and Alex Gerrard agree that the loss resulted from a combination of two factors:

(a) the bolts or studs used to secure the outer head cover were of an inferior quality and did not meet the standards specified;

(b) the units, when first installed and operated, vibrated excessively as the result of improper design. Before the problem was corrected, the vibration caused some of the studs to crack. Over the course of the succeeding years, the cracks expanded until the studs could no longer secure the head cover.

14. I am advised by Dr. Tennesey that the designer of the turbines fell below the standard of care expected of engineers in the design of equipment of this nature in:

(a) designing the turbines so that they vibrated to the extent that they did;

(b) failing to recognize that the vibrations that did occur might have caused fatigue cracks in various components throughout the unit and, in particular, to the outer head cover studs.

15. It is Hydro's position that the Respondents were in breach of the said Agreement and, in particular, those terms referred to in paragraph 7 hereof. In providing studs of inferior quality, the Respondents breached their contractual obligations to supply proper materials of sufficient strength, and in designing turbines subject to excessive vibration, the Respondents breached their contractual obligations to design the turbines in a proper manner so as to ensure satisfactory performance and durability.

[79]           The motions court judge reasoned at para. 26:

In deciding this issue I refer specifically to paragraphs 13, 14 and 15 of the Law affidavit. While those paragraphs are perhaps not as artfully drafted as one might wish, nevertheless, the following is clear to me:

1. Paragraph 13(a) effectively alleges that the loss resulted from a combination of two factors:

(a) the bolts or studs used to secure the outer head cover were of an inferior quality and did not meet the standard specified;

(b) the units when first installed and operated vibrated excessively as a result of improper design, that before the problem was corrected the vibration caused some of the studs to crack and that over the course of the succeeding years the cracks expanded until the studs could no longer secure the head cover.

The above are allegations of both deficient manufacture and/or design. They are claims both in contract and in tort. As against Inglis, a party to the contract, they can be viewed as claims either in contract or in tort. As against E.E. England [English Electric], not a party to the contract, they are claims in tort only.

2. Paragraph 14 effectively alleges that the designer of the turbines fell below the standard of care expected of engineers in the design of equipment of this nature. The paragraph then provides particulars, including designing the turbines so that they vibrated to the extent that they did, and failing to recognize that the vibrations that did occur might have caused fatigue cracks in the various components throughout the unit and in particular to the outer head cover studs. While I will address this later in my reasons, it is clear to me on the evidence adduced by the plaintiff that the designer of the turbines was E.E. England [English Electric] and that there is a real basis for arguing it was also the designer of other components including the outer head cover studs.

3. Paragraph 15 is somewhat in error in that it refers to the respondents being in breach of the agreement, whereas in fact, only Inglis was a party to the contract, and therefore it, only, could have been in breach of it. With that correction, however, it is clear that the allegations referred to in paragraph 15 are allegations of breach of contract.

[80]           Clearly, as MacInnes J. recognized, the affidavit could have been drafted more felicitously, and with the advantage of hindsight, it undoubtedly would have been. Felicity of expression, however, is not the issue on the appeal; the issue is the sufficiency of the "pleadings" and, more particularly, whether the motions court judge's conclusion as to their sufficiency finds support in the record.

[81]           In subpara. 13(a) of the affidavit, it is asserted that the head cover bolts or studs "did not meet the standards specified". That allegation clearly raises a contractual claim against John Inglis. With nothing more, it is unlikely that the allegation could raise a claim in tort either against John Inglis or against English Electric. But there is more to the pleading. It is also alleged that the head cover bolts or studs "were of an inferior quality". The motions court judge appears to have concluded that that allegation was more than a redundant phrase that added nothing to the contractual claim; that it raised a breach of a common law duty of care against both John Inglis and English Electric. That is a reasonable conclusion for him to have reached on the application before him.

[82]           English Electric challenges the finding of the motions court judge that "the designer of the turbines was [English Electric] and that there is a real basis for arguing it was also the designer of other components including the outer head cover studs". That was an allegation put forward in para. 6 of the affidavit: the "turbines were designed and developed primarily by the Respondent [English Electric]". While the nature and extent of the role of English Electric in the design of the turbines and its components, and the legal consequences flowing therefrom, will likely become live issues at the trial of the action, there was ample evidence before the motions court judge on Hydro's application to support these findings and the implicit finding that there was a relationship of sufficient proximity to create a common law duty of care.

[83]           In para. 14, it is alleged that "the designer of the turbines fell below the standard of care expected of engineers in the design of equipment of this nature". It is not alleged that the standard of care arises out of or is defined by contractual provisions or specifications. It is a claim sounding in tort.

[84]           The inelegant and sometimes inaccurate drafting of Mr. Law's affidavit is the cause of much, it not all, of the difficulties that surround this issue. As we have noted above, the affidavit was filed in support of an application that was originally intended to address the limitation period affecting Hydro's contractual claims. Mr. Law, in para. 6, wrongly identified English Electric Company of Canada, Limited as a party to the contract. He then went on to say that English Electric "assumed the contractual commitments of [John Inglis] and English Electric Company of Canada, Limited pursuant to the said Agreement by means of a letter dated June 18, 1965". That assertion is not established on the record before the court. Further paragraphs of the affidavit refer to the responsibilities of John Inglis, English Electric Company of Canada, Limited, and English Electric pursuant to the contract and, in para. 15, to their breach of its terms.

[85]           These are the circumstances that led counsel for English Electric to argue:

We submit that paragraph 15 restates and summarizes the factors referred to in paragraphs 13 and 14 and establishes that they are in fact particulars of the breach of the Agreement by all of the Respondents.

[86]           MacInnes J. was of a different view. He recognized the error in para. 15 and concluded that it alleged breach of contract against John Inglis, the only party to the contract with Hydro. It was open to him in the circumstances to find that the claims advanced in paras. 13 and 14 sounding in tort were not circumscribed by para. 15. This ground of appeal must fail.

Conclusion

[87]           In the result, therefore, the appeals are dismissed with costs to Hydro.

Appeal dismissed.
