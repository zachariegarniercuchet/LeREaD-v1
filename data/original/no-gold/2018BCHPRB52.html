Inquiry Committee Disposition Decision Review – Summary:

Stage 1 hearing of an application for review of an inquiry committee (IC) disposition under s.50.6 Health Professions Act (HPA) – disposition confirmed. After the Complainant’s probationary employment was terminated, she lodged a complaint against the Registrant, one of the Complainant’s co-workers who had raised concerns with the employer. The College investigated and the IC concluded that the conduct and competence of the Registrant were satisfactory. The Panel noted that the Registrant considered the Complainant’s complaint to the College to be an abuse of process and an attempt to discredit the Registrant and others through false and unsubstantiated allegations. On its own initiative, the Panel requested a submission from the Complainant to show cause why her application for review should not be summarily dismissed. The Panel decided not to proceed by summary dismissal, and instead confirmed the disposition. The Record showed that the investigator obtained the Registrant’s response to the complaint, the Complainant’s reply, the Registrant’s sur-reply, and associated communications with the parties to address the key allegations and produce an informed and reasoned disposition. The Panel held that the investigation was adequate and commensurate with the seriousness of the allegations; to require more would have caused vexation or abuse of process. The Record showed that the Complainant’s speculation that one of the IC members who decided the complaint was in conflict of interest had no basis, because that person was not in attendance at the meeting. The disposition fell within the range of reasonable options.

 

I               INTRODUCTION

[1]           On December 5, 2016, the Complainant made a complaint (the “Complaint”) to the College about the Registrant. The Complaint was investigated by the College’s inquiry committee (the “Committee”). At its May 30, 2017 meeting, the Committee resolved to:

Take no further action. Conduct and competence of registrant satisfactory.
Concern re: vexatious nature of complaint and that it was advanced in bad faith by complainant.

[2]           On June 27, 2017, the Committee’s disposition was communicated to the Complainant by letter, with the stated reasons expressed as conclusions without support.

[3]           On July 26, 2017, the Complainant applied for review of the disposition under s.50.6 of the Health Professions Act, R.S.B.C. 1996, c.183 (the “Act”) – as already defined in the header to this decision. The College provided the records of the investigation and disposition which included the relevant minutes of the May 30, 2017 Committee meeting redacted of unrelated business (the “Records”). First, on August 30, 2017, the College provided the records of the investigation and disposition which included a single minute of the May 30, 2017 meeting with all else redacted. Later, upon my direction, the College provided minutes showing the opening and closing particulars. I sought these as records necessary, in the ordinary course, to disclose establishment of the meeting (with attendance listed and declarations of conflict of interest - a relevant point here) and its conclusion without any actions that could nullify the implementation of the disposition.

[4]           This matter was assigned to me for a Stage 1 hearing. At this stage I may do either of the following:

(a)  Confirm the disposition of the Committee under s.50.6(8)(a) of the Act if the Application may be fairly, properly and finally adjudicated based only on the application for review, the Record, and submissions or evidence, if any, from the Complainant without submission from the College or Registrant.

(b)  Determine that the Application requires a Stage 2 hearing upon which the Review Board would request submissions from the College and Registrant, and then provide the Complainant with a right to a reply submission.

[5]           Rule 9 of this Review Board’s, Rules of Practice and Procedure for Reviews allows me on my own initiative to consider summarily dismissing an application. On December 29, 2017, and supplemented on January 5, 2018, the Complainant was notified and given an opportunity to make a submission on the matter proceeding by way of summary dismissal. She was asked to show cause why her Application to the Review Board should not be summarily dismissed on the grounds that it is vexatious or made in bad faith. This is not the same as determining whether the Complaint, itself, was vexatious or made in bad faith.

[6]           I have determined that I do not need to proceed by summary dismissal, but confirm the disposition of the Committee as set out below without need of hearing from the College.

II              Background

A.           Workplace Dispute

[7]           The Complainant is a Registered Social Worker.  The Complainant and the Registrant worked together while the Complainant was a probationary employee. The Complainant’s employment was terminated about three months into the probationary period.

[8]           The Complainant took legal action against the employer to contest the termination. During this legal action, the Complainant received disclosure of her records of employment, which revealed complaints and concerns about her that had been raised by co-workers, including the Registrant. The Complainant then lodged the Complaint while the legal action continued.

[9]           The Complaint was about the Registrant’s workplace conduct and work habits, which the Complainant considered to be in violation of College standards, including the standard that requires the Registrant to comply with the Mental Health Act, R.S.B.C. 1996, c. 288 (the “MHA”).

B.           The Complaint

[10]        The Complaint was well laid out with a chronology, and with allegations corresponding to citations of specific sections of the College’s Professional Standards for Psychiatric Nursing.  I have summarized the substance of the Complaint below. I need not address nor determine who was in the right or wrong.

(a)  The Complainant described the Registrant’s “professional regard and conduct toward” the Complainant with reference to a significant incident in which the Complainant alleged that the Registrant was “verbally aggressive, hostile, and communicated with [the Complainant] in a very unprofessional manner”. [Record pg. 7]

(b)  The Complainant characterized this behaviour as part of a “bullying and harassment campaign” against her by a group of employees that included the Registrant. This “campaign” included registering complaints against her because she advocated for a patient (something the Complainant felt that she was required to do in her role as a Registered Social Worker). These complaints made their way into the Complainant’s employment record. In relation to the significant incident referenced above, the Complainant was of the view that the Registrant had displayed a “very callous, careless response that lacked any kind of empathy for [the Complainant’s] patient, for his level of increased anxiety over the situation, or his individual psychosocial stressors and how these related to his personal risks.” [Record pg. 5]

(c)  The Complainant alleged that “The employer also recorded a statement that [the Registrant] is alleged to have made that I wasn’t a ‘team player’.” The Complainant described being a team player in “an incredibly toxic workplace culture that had normalized bullying”. [Record pg. 10]

(d)  The Complainant alleged that the Registrant made damaging statements about her to the employer: Statements that “[t]he employer has been repeatedly requested to have [the Registrant] provide specific, concrete details to support …[and that the Registrant] has not done so and/or the employer has not provided any” that were provided. [Record pg. 11]

(e)  Under the heading “Workplace Bullying and Harassment”, the Complainant alleged that making such statements was a breach of WorkSafeBC policy, the employer’s “Respectful Workplace Policy” and, being false (in her view), were College ethics and standards code breaches and violations of s.398 of the Criminal Code, R.S.C., 1985, c. C-46, as well as defamation and slander. [Record pgs. 12-15]

(f)    According to the Complainant, the Registrant “consistently (or at all)” conducted and recorded nursing assessments and care plans which the Complainant perceived as violations of ethics and standards codes. [Record pgs. 15-18]

(g)  The Complainant alleged that the Registrant “was chronically breaching the [MHA] by failing to complete the required statutory forms related to involuntary detention of patients and discharge”. [Record pgs. 18-19]

(h)  The Complainant alleged that the Registrant’s documentation standards were “quite low” and that she “regularly failed to document” various plans, activities, execution times for orders and seclusion of patients.  With respect to seclusion of patients, the Complainant said “I do not specifically know if [the Registrant] engaged in confinement of patients on the unit by locking them in their bedrooms”; but the Complainant alleged that this was a common practice that also went undocumented. [Record pgs. 20-21]

C.           Investigation

[11]        The investigation of the Complaint proceeded by providing the Registrant with a right to respond, followed by the Complainant having a right of reply to that response. This reply included a new or modified claim such that the Committee’s investigator gave the Registrant a right of sur-reply. These steps are summarized below.

[12]        On February 3, 2017, the Registrant responded to the Complaint through her counsel. That response alleged that the Complainant was retaliating against the Registrant “as well as other members of the College and members of other regulated bodies.” (I note that there is no information in the Record of such other filings.) The Registrant described the Complaint and the other (alleged) filings as an attempt to “discredit [the Registrant] and others through false and unsubstantiated allegations in an effort to erode [the Registrant’s] credibility and professional integrity.” It was called an abuse of process. [see Record pg. 68 as to all]

[13]        The Registrant’s response was completely at odds with the Complaint. It addressed the significant incident and contested the negative characterization of each event. The response addresses the issues by heading as follows –

(a)  Regarding the work environment the Registrant disagreed with the Complainant’s characterization of the workplace as toxic, or one in which bullying was normal. The Registrant claimed to be “astonished at these claims.”

(b)  Regarding provision of comments to the employer that ended up on the Complainant’s employment records, the Registrant says that she provided them “with honesty and out of a duty to report.” She asserted that it is not professional misconduct to provide feedback to the employer and further that: “It is not the role of the College to assess whether or not feedback on a probationary employee provided in good faith constituted professional misconduct.” [Record pg. 73]

(c)  Regarding the Registrant’s documentation practices, the Registrant noted that the work unit had “undergone extensive audits, two in the last two years, on many aspects of care including documentation and charging, in order to achieve Accreditation” as a designated Mental Health Facility. She also identified the systems in place for plans and care documentation which would have been part of the assessment for accreditation. [Record pgs. 74-75]

(d)  Regarding the claim of “chronically breaching the [MHA]” the Registrant denied ever breaching the MHA, and noted that it is insufficient evidence when the Complainant admitted that she “did not once see any evidence that [the Registrant] had completed these official, legally required forms for any patients who were admitted to or discharged from [the unit]”. [Record pg. 76] (I note here that there is no evidence that the Complainant has legal expertise to interpret the MHA. I also add that the statute imposes obligations on “the director of designated facility” and physicians for involuntary admissions, not nurses. The only reference to a “nurse” is regarding voluntary admissions and bears no reference to paperwork.)

[14]        The Complainant’s reply indicated that she mistook statements made by the Registrant’s Counsel for those of a College investigator. She was “shocked at the vicious, slanderous and entirely unprofessional tone and the conclusions made…”. The Committee’s actual investigator contacted the Complainant to explain. The investigator then invited the Complainant to resubmit her reply if she so wished. However, the Complainant’s reply reasserted the Complaint without correcting the error.  Nevertheless, the investigator found that the reply contained a new allegation that warranted granting the Registrant a sur-reply. (The exact nature of the new allegation is not relevant to this decision.)

[15]        The Registrant’s sur-reply addressed the new allegation as “entirely unfounded and untrue” generally describing it as “a repetition of the unsubstantiated allegations from [the Complainant’s] original Complaint and so [the Registrant will] continue to rely on [her] Response submission dated February 3, 2017.”

D.           The Application for Review

[16]        The Complainant’s application for review here is based upon the investigation of the Committee being inadequate and/or the disposition being unreasonable that may be summarized under four headings:

(a)  Because she had “absolutely no idea of what actual investigation was completed by the CRPNBC.” [emphasis in original; CRPNBC = College].

(b)  That the disposition could not be reasonable due to a failure to conduct a “full investigation with an external, competent and unbiased inspector/investigator” consistent with transparency, accountability, and principles of administrative and procedural fairness.

(c)  That if a certain member of the Committee did not recuse herself due to conflict of interest then this should be investigated.

(d)  That she requested an “investigation into the bigger picture of the [Committee’s] disposition of this complaint.” (This I take to be a general allegation of inadequate investigation and unreasonable disposition.)

III            Analysis

A.           Review, Not Rehearing or on Merits

[17]        The questions in a review such as this are whether the investigation was adequate and whether the disposition was reasonable. As reiterated in many previous decisions, under the Act a review is not a rehearing of the merits of a complaint, and except when new evidence is permitted, the review is exclusively on the record. Indeed, the review could not be a re-hearing strictly speaking because a hearing has not occurred. Inquiry committees do not conduct adjudicative hearings or make adjudicative findings of fact. Inquiry committees perform what has been described as performing a more informal “screening” function.

[18]        While inquiry committees have been described that way labeling it as performing a “screening” function, at best (if truly appropriate), downplays the significance of its role. It also downplays an inquiry committee’s power to terminate proceedings on a complaint without referral to a discipline hearing. An inquiry committee plays a critical role in the exercise of a professional health college’s larger public interest mandate. “Screening” is not simply about whether to send the matter to a discipline hearing - a rare event. Screening also involves inquiry committees making provisional findings and assessments whether evidence supports a determination that a registrant’s conduct had fallen below the regulatory standard. (Accordingly, a termination of proceedings is not strictly ‘final’ in terms of subject matter or res judicata.) Screening is also about whether, in order to protect the public interest, the inquiry committee should seek to exercise one of the several other remedial options reflected in ss.33(6)(b), 33(6)(c) and 36(1) of the Act, which remedies are no less important just because they depend on a registrant’s consent.

[19]        The Review Board was created precisely because the legislature recognized that each inquiry committee plays a significant role with meaningful consequences, which otherwise had no independent oversight. This requires the Review Board to ensure that its mandate is exercised in compliance with the Act and in a meaningful fashion to ensure that a particular disposition under review reflects an adequate investigation and a reasonable disposition. In doing so the Review Board may send the matter back with directions for reconsideration through to the Review Board itself, specifying the decision on the merits – an act for which ‘deference’ is not addressed here.

B.           Adequacy of the Investigation

(i)            Test for Adequacy

[20]        As has been stated in many Review Board decisions, an investigation does not need to be exhaustive or perfect to be considered as adequate. It is based upon the circumstances, which necessarily vary from case to case. The test of adequacy will be met if the reviewer is satisfied that it was conducted in a manner consistent with the requirements of the Act, with sufficient steps taken to obtain the information relevant to the inquiry committee’s assessment of the key issues. The measure of satisfaction is whether the steps taken were proper or fair, and appropriately thorough and diligent, such that they could produce an informed and reasoned disposition commensurate with the seriousness of the matter. I also note, that in this transparency matters consistent with and to the same degree as the other listed obligations under s.16(2)(i.1) of the Act require procedures be “transparent, objective, impartial and fair”. This is consistent with (if not enhanced) by s.16(1)(b) that responsibilities acting “in the public interest”.

(ii)          Investigative Steps

[21]        Also expressed in several earlier decisions of the Review Board, an adequate investigation must take certain key investigative steps that may be described as:

(a)  seeking all key information;

(b)  querying all key sources of information; and

(c)  addressing each key issue (or allegation) of a complaint.

[22]        I am satisfied that the required investigative steps were taken in this case, considering the allegations and subject matter, and that it could produce an informed and reasoned disposition of the kind made. It is important to note that not every allegation is a key issue and not every issue is subject to regulatory scrutiny or is in the public interest. An investigation need not be driven into a fishing expedition by allegations lacking a substantive basis. The key allegations and elements of this investigation depended upon weighing the word of one against the other, and of considering circumstances, including the subject matter –a workplace dispute (in the broadest sense). Investigation need not ‘drive to ground’ information, sources, or issues where it is highly improbable that further investigation would be helpful to discover evidence of matters concerning regulatory oversight. The Complainant has not shown what information and sources would reveal more in support of any portion of the Complaint of regulatory substance.

[23]        In the circumstances, sufficient evidence could be derived from the submissions received by the investigator in the Complaint, the response, reply and sur-reply, and associated communications with the parties. I also find that the investigation was commensurate with the seriousness of the allegations. Indeed, to require more would be to enhance the very thing that the reasons of the Committee identified, by causing vexation or abuse of process.

[24]        With respect to the request for an order that an investigation be conducted by an “external, competent and unbiased inspector/investigator”, given that I have found the investigation to have been adequate in the circumstance, I need not contemplate such an order. In any event, the Committee is entitled as “master of its own house” to establish its own practices and procedures subject, as always, to principles of administrative justice. Without directly addressing the need for competence, there is no evidence that the investigator was not competent, nor is there evidence that the investigator was biased. The Complainant’s confusion that the Registrant’s counsel was a Committee investigator is not a valid basis on which to allege or support review for bias.

IV           Reasonableness of the Disposition

[25]        In assessing the reasonableness of a decision, my scope of authority under the Act is clear. I am not to conduct a new or further investigation nor review the evidence on the merits and substitute my decision for that of the Committee because I might have reached a different conclusion. Accordingly, I cannot conduct an investigation of my own, despite the Complainant’s request for an “investigation into the bigger picture of the [Committee’s] disposition of this complaint”.

[26]        My mandate in reviewing the disposition of the Committee is limited. I am to determine whether the disposition fell within the range of reasonable options in the circumstances. That said, a disposition cannot be reasonable if it arises from a breach of administrative and procedural fairness, or the obligations of the decision-maker by law. As stated, the Act requires the College to establish and employ procedures that are transparent, objective, impartial and fair (s.16 (2)(i.1) of the Act).

[27]        It is a basic principle of administrative law that reasons cannot be merely conclusory (unsupported) or restatement of evidence, positions or criteria which the decision-maker is supposed to consider. Simply put, a statement that an inquiry committee is “satisfied” with someone’s competence or conduct does not address the reasons for and reasonableness of a disposition. In the disposition of this Complaint, however, I find that exceptions to this principle, specifically obviousness, or other extraordinary circumstances, apply to reduce the need for reasons.

[28]        The test is whether the parties can discern the reasons for the disposition and that they arose from a full and fair consideration within jurisdiction. Reasons may be brief, even perfunctory, provided they are justified, transparent, and intelligible within the context. Reasons may be obvious from the circumstances looking at the records or even from the known intimate involvement of a party in the process. This includes whether the deciding criteria (fact or law) was obviously met. (See: MacKenzie v. LeBlanc, 2007 BCSC 768 (CanLII), and Northwestern Utilities Ltd. v. Edmonton (City), 1978 CanLII 17 (SCC), [1979] 1 S.C.R. 684.) Here, given the involvement of both parties to the ‘workplace dispute’, and the context of the Complaint, the Committee’s disposition, though short, conveys sufficient reasons to satisfy the test.

[29]        In this case, the Committee noted a concern about bad faith and the vexatious nature of the Complaint. While it was not expressly stated as a reason I find that it is probable that this is the reason for the disposition that the Committee take no further action, having determined that the conduct and competence of the Registrant were satisfactory.

[30]        Similarly, notwithstanding that the letter of June 27, 2017 did not specify that it was sent by or at the direction of the Committee, and that there was no resolution (or any standing order) that clearly authorized it to be sent, I find that that it is probable that it was authorized and was the Notice of Disposition under s.34 of the Act. Indeed, the letter asserts that it was sent pursuant to s.34 at its outset and was sent over the signature of legal counsel with the contents tracking exactly the Committee’s disposition and reasons.

[31]        Notwithstanding my findings above, the Complainant speculated that one of the Committee members was in “conflict of interest” but did not recuse herself. This is an allegation of bias that the Complainant appropriately raised. If confirmed, it would be a valid concern. However, the minutes from the relevant meeting show that the person of concern was not in attendance, so this is a non-issue.

[32]        Returning to whether the disposition of the Committee was "reasonable" I find that it fell within the range of the reasonable options in the circumstances.

[33]        Given the foregoing I need not consider the whether the Complainant sought the College to address any matters already dealt with in another proceeding, or whether I should or am able to decide whether the Complainant was asking the College to exceed its jurisdiction in regulation of its registrants.

V             Conclusion

[34]        I have found that I may fairly, properly and finally adjudicate the Application at this Stage 1, and have found the investigation of the Complaint to be adequate and its disposition to be reasonable. For all the reasons above, I order that the Committee’s disposition of the Complaint is confirmed under s.50.6(8)(a) of the Act.
