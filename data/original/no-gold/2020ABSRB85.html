DEMAND FOR PAYMENT
AND ORDER SUSPENDING AND TERMINATING ENTRY RIGHTS

 

IT IS DEMANDED the Operator pay FOUR THOUSAND SIX HUNDRED and 00/100 DOLLARS ($4,600.00) to the Applicant. If this amount is not paid by the date of termination, below, the Board may direct the Minister to pay ONE THOUSAND ONE HUNDRED AND FIFTY and 00/100 DOLLARS ($1,150.00) to the Applicant for the years 2016 and 2017.

IT IS ORDERED that if the Board does not receive satisfactory evidence that the $4,600.00 has been paid in full to the Applicant, then without further notice the Operator’s right to enter the Site shall be suspended and terminated under section 36(5) of the Act at 4:30 p.m. on the dates below. This shall not affect any of the Operator’s obligations in regards to the Site, nor any other person’s rights against the Operator. The Right of Entry Instrument remains in place for purposes of shutting-in, suspension, abandonment, and reclamation.

•       Suspension effective from February 23, 2020, lasting 15 days.

•       Termination effective from March 9, 2020.

 

DECISION AND REASONS

 

[1]               The Applicant filed an application under section 36 of the Act seeking recovery of unpaid compensation due under a Surface Lease Agreement for the above Site dated January 9, 1981 (the “Right of Entry Instrument”).

ISSUES

[2]               The issues before the Panel are:

(1)     Is money past due and unpaid by the Operator to the Applicant under a surface lease agreement or compensation order?

(2)     If money is past due and unpaid, is there any reason why the Board should direct the Minister to pay less than the full amount owing?

(3)     Should the Board suspend and terminate the Operator’s entry rights and direct the Minister to pay the Applicant out of the General Revenue Fund under section 36(6) of the Act?

DECISION

[3]               The Panel decides:

(1)     Pursuant to the Right of Entry Instrument, $4,600.00 is payable to the Applicant by the Operator and the evidence satisfactorily proves non-payment for 2016 and 2017.

(2)     Directing the Minister to pay the full amount owing by the Operator is unjustified and the Applicant is entitled to a total amount of $1,150.00 for the years 2016 and 2017.

(3)     Unless the Board receives satisfactory evidence that $4,600.00 has been paid in full to the Applicant, the Operator’s entry rights shall be suspended and terminated according to the preceding order and the Board may direct the Minister to pay the amount of $1,150.00.

ANALYSIS

Issue 1:  Is there money past due and unpaid by the Operator to the Applicant under a surface lease agreement or compensation order?

[4]               After a review of the evidence, the Panel finds that $4,600.00 is owed by the Operator to the Applicant for annual payment due under the Right of Entry Instrument. This amount is calculated as two payments of $2,300.00 due on the anniversary of the Right of Entry Instrument for the years 2016 and 2017.

[5]               The current Certificate of Title confirms the Applicant is the owner of the Land and was the owner when the rentals became due. Evidence of a surface lease and the rental rate is supported by documentation on file. The Applicant’s Director, James Praskach, swore a statutory declaration stating that the rentals have not been paid. The Panel notes that the statutory declaration does not contain a signing date or location, but does have the Commissioner’s name and expiration date. The Panel has decided to accept the statutory declaration as evidence in spite of its flaws.

[6]               The well licence is registered in the name of the Operator. The Site has not been reclaimed, and accordingly the Right of Entry Instrument remains in effect.

Issue 2: If money is past due and unpaid, is there any reason why the Board should direct the Minister to pay less than the full amount owing?

[7]               The Panel’s decision to direct the Minister to pay out of the General Revenue Fund is discretionary, per Devon Canada Corp v Alberta (Surface Rights Board), 2003 ABQB 7 at ¶29.3 (“Devon”). This was confirmed by the Alberta Court of Queen’s Bench in Provident Energy Ltd v Alberta (Surface Rights Board), 2004 ABQB 650 (“Provident Energy”).

[8]               This Decision is restricted to making a determination of the amount of compensation the Operator owes and what the Board should direct the Minister to pay out of public money. This decision does not absolve the Operator of the responsibility to pay the full amount of compensation owing. It is not a compensation review under section 27 of the Act and it does not amend the Surface Lease Agreement, or affect the amount owing to the Applicant.

[9]               The Court in Devon makes it clear that the Panel must consider whether to direct the Minister to pay the full amount of money owing or a reduced amount if it finds that the payment of the full amount is unjustified, patently unreasonable, or constitutes unjust enrichment. At paragraph 29.2 of Devon, the Court provides some direction on factors the Board should consider:

            The Surface Rights Board has developed an expertise in quantifying land values, inconvenience, weed control, damage to topsoil and oil company techniques. Some legal expertise is required to determine whether a lease is valid and subsisting. No expertise is needed to determine whether rent has been paid. In determining which factors are relevant to decide whether a direction should be made that the province pay the rental arrears, the Board has much experience and thus is better aware of how best to exercise this discretion.

[10]           There are two factors particularly important for considering annual compensation and whether directing the Minister to pay the full amount owing is unjustified. When the Board reviews the rate of compensation under section 27 of the Act, the factors considered under section 25(1)(c) and (d) are the loss of use by the owner of the area of the surface lease and the adverse effect of the lease on the remaining land of the owner. The Board also assesses the nuisance, inconvenience, and noise that might be caused by or arising from or in connection with the operations of the operator. As noted above, this is not a review of compensation under section 27, however, the loss of use and adverse effect are components of fair compensation which the Board can consider when determining if directing the Minister to pay the full amount owing is justified.

[11]           When considering the loss of use of the lease area, the Panel examines the status of the area taken and whether the Applicant is using the site to generate income or otherwise. The Panel also considers the adverse effect on the remaining land or the nuisance, inconvenience, and noise that might be caused by or arising from or in connection with the operations of the operator. The purpose of annual compensation under the Act is to compensate the owner for loss and adverse effect arising from the surface lease and the operations of the operator.

[12]           Where, at the relevant time, there is ongoing loss of use or adverse effect; equipment, fencing and facilities remain on site; the site is compacted preventing production; or there are reclamation activities or other damages, it is less likely that there would be a reduction in the amount that the Minister is directed to pay. On the other hand, if the site is near reclamation and the owner has full use of it, the loss of use and adverse effect would be almost nonexistent and directing the Minister to pay the full amount owing under the lease would not be justified. Likewise, if the site is reclaimed, but no Reclamation Certificate is issued, the Board may determine that there should be a significant reduction in the amount that the Minister is directed to pay. For example, in Provident Energy, in finding that the Board was correct in following Devon and exercising its discretion to reduce the payments to the landowners in an amount reflecting the loss incurred, the Court stated at paragraph 27:

As Sirrs J. held in the Devon case, the application of Section 36 is discretionary and even if a land owner shows sufficient evidence that a lease exists, the Board is not bound to order compensation. If the Board was bound to do so, this would amount to a fettering of its discretion.

[13]           The Board may determine the amount the Minister is directed to pay by considering of a combination of factors relevant to loss of use and adverse effect. Some compelling factors include facilities, fencing, and equipment remaining on the lease; operator activity on the lease; the condition of the land within the lease, including compaction, foreign materials, and changes of elevation; how the remaining land is used and how the site impacts this use; if and to what extent the owner is using the lease for production, including cropping and grazing; and the existence and extent of nuisance, inconvenience, and noise.

[14]           As stated in Devon (at para 29.3), the section 36 process provides a “pragmatic, inexpensive solution to the surface owner when rent under a surface lease is not paid.” At the same time, the process must be fair and provide an opportunity for the parties to provide submissions. In this context, the Panel considered whether further proceedings are necessary to establish the impacts of the Surface Lease and any operations on the Land. In Husky Oil Operations Limited v Scriber, 2013 ABQB 74, the Court determined that the Board is entitled to consider what it would accept as evidence and whether, in doing so, it might open a plethora of inquiries which would lead to protracted, inefficient, and inaccessible Board proceedings. To prevent a cumbersome and inefficient process in determining section 36 applications that are typically unopposed by the operator, the Panel will conduct this hearing by written submissions and consider the Applicant’s application, statutory declaration, and written submissions and documents provided. The Panel finds this process to be fair and sufficient to establish whether the Minister should be ordered to pay the full amount or a portion of the money owed.

[15]           Regarding loss of use, the Panel notes that an Alberta Energy Regulator search confirms that the wellsite was listed as an abandoned site as of July 31, 1998 and its license status has not changed since that time. The Surface Lease remains on the Title, and the Site has not been fully reclaimed, leaving the Lease in effect until a Reclamation Certificate issues pursuant to section 144 of the Environmental and Protection Act, RSA 2000 c E‑12.

[16]           The Application contains a Condition of Land Form wherein the Applicant is asked to describe the condition of the surface lease area. In response to the Applicant’s completed Condition of Land Form, Surface Rights Board administration sent a letter to the Applicant explaining that changes in the condition of the surface lease area could result in reduced payment and the Applicant was invited to make submissions. In response, the Applicant filed further submissions with the Board on February 28, 2019.

[17]           In its submissions, the Applicant conceded that the gas well is inactive and there is no equipment or fencing on the Site. The Site is within pivot irrigated land and is cropped annually. The soil within the Site is compacted, noticeably rough, and undulating which impacts seeding and harvesting of low-growing crops. The Applicant was unable to estimate reduced yield because the Site is located near the margin of irrigated and non-irrigated land.

[18]           The Site is cropped and the Leased land is back in production, therefore, the loss of use is greatly diminished as the Applicant is once again using the Lease to generate income. The Applicant reports the production is lower over the Site and there is some difficulty seeding and harvesting due to uneven soil, suggesting there is still some minor loss of use.

[19]           Regarding adverse effect, the impact on remaining Lands at the relevant time (2016 and 2017) is minimal because the wellsite is not in operation and has not been for many years. The well was abandoned in August 1998 and the Board finds that Operator activity ceased at approximately the same time, with the exception of incomplete reclamation work that occurred five to seven years prior to the date of the owner’s submission. There is no evidence of impact on remaining Lands on the relevant time period and there is no nuisance, inconvenience, or noise that might be caused by the operations of the Operator.

[20]           Because the loss of use and the adverse effect is greatly diminished, the Panel finds that directing the Minister to pay the full amount of unpaid rentals out of public money is unjustified and would cause unjust enrichment.

[21]           In this case, the Panel determines a reduction in the amount it directs the Minister to pay by 75% of annual compensation, from $2,300.00 per year to $575.00 per year, to reflect a significant decrease in the loss of use and adverse effect suffered by the Applicant as a result of the surface lease.

[22]           This decision does not absolve the Operator of the responsibility to pay the full amount of compensation. Again, the Panel notes the Operator remains responsible to pay the full amount and if the Operator does not make payment of the full amount owing to the Applicant, its entry rights will be suspended and terminated according to the preceding order, after which the Board may direct the Minister to pay a total of $1,150.00 to the Applicant out of the General Revenue Fund under section 36(6) for the years 2016 and 2017.
