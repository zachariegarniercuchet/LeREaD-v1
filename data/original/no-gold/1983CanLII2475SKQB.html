[1]             Scheibel, J.: This is an application pursuant to s. 24(1) of the Charter of Rights and Freedoms for relief in respect of an alleged infringement of rights guaranteed by the Charter. The applicants are three corporations engaged in various phases of the manufacture, distribution and sale of business forms and related products, and fifteen individuals who were connected with one or more of these corporations by virtue of their employment in some capacity relating to the actual business operations of one or more of the corporations. All fifteen individuals have been ordered by the Restrictive Trade Practises Commission (herein referred to as “the Commission”) constituted under the Combines Investigation Act, R.S.C. 1970, c. C-23 (herein referred to as “the Act”) to appear before a hearing officer to give oral testimony under oath in connection with the business operations of the applicant corporations.

[2]             The applicants, corporate and individual, object to this proposed form of inquiry because they fear that their rights under the Charter are and will be violated. The respondents are L.A. Couture, Q.C., the Hearing Officer appointed to receive the oral testimony of the individual applicants, the Commission, and Lawson A.W. Hunter, Director of Investigation and Research of the Combines Investigation Branch of the Department of Consumer and Corporate Affairs.

I

Relief Sought:

[3]             The relief being sought by the applicants is set out in the amended notice of motion as follows:

1) A declaration that the proposed inquiry procedure is repugnant to the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms;

1a) A declaration that the provisions of s. 45 of the Combines Investigation Act are, pursuant to s. 52 of the Constitution Act, 1982, of no force and effect;

2) A mandatory order directing the Director of Investigation and Research and the respondent, L.A. Couture, Q.C., to give effect to the rights of the applicants herein as such rights are guaranteed in the said Charter;

2) A declaration that, in the absence of an according of the principles of fundamental justice by the respondents to the applicants during the course of the said inquiry, a refusal by a witness to answer and the action of counsel in so advising a witness is not an offence within the meaning of sections 40 and 41 of the Combines Investigation Act;

4) An interim order staying the respondents from proceeding with the said inquiry until such time as this Motion can be heard;

5) An order restraining the respondents from depriving or continuing to deprive the applicants of the principles of fundamental Justice in the said inquiry.

II

Issues:

[4]             The issues presented for determination in this case are as follows:

1) Whether, having regard to the nature of the relief sought, the commencement procedure adopted by the applicants in this case is authorized by the Queen’s Bench Rules for Saskatchewan, and if not, whether s. 24 of the Charter operates to modify or dispense with procedural rules in force in the province governing the manner in which proceedings in this court shall be commenced.

2) Whether this court is a court of competent jurisdiction to entertain an application for a declaration respecting the constitutional validity of federal legislation where the legislation is impugned as being contrary to the Charter of Rights and Freedoms; and whether this court has jurisdiction to adjudicate upon a claim alleging that rights guaranteed by the Charter have been infringed by federal administrative action where that claim is unconnected with the constitutional validity of the legislation pursuant to which the administrative action was taken.

3) Whether the inquiry procedure authorized by Part II of the Act is invalid as infringing the rights of the applicants under s. 7 of the Charter. In this regard the following subsidiary issues are raised:

a) Is the right not to be compelled to give self-incriminating evidence within the range of interests protected by the right to life, liberty and security of the person;

b) What standard of judicial review of alleged interferences with the rights protected by s. 7 is contemplated by the phrase “fundamental justice”;

4) Whether the provisions of s. 45 of the Act are inconsistent with the rights guaranteed by s. 7 and s. 11(d) of the Charter.

III

Facts:

[6]             In pursuance of his duty to investigate, the Director, at various dates between August, 1981, and February, 1982, authorized his representatives to attend at the business premises of the three corporate applicants for the purpose of examining and taking away documents which in the opinion of the Director or his representatives were capable of affording evidence relevant to the inquiry. These documents were subsequently reproduced on microfiche and the originals returned to the applicant corporations.

No issue arises in these proceedings as to whether the provisions of s. 10 have been complied with or whether, assuming such compliance, the procedure authorized by s. 10 is unlawful as infringing upon the constitutional guarantee against unreasonable search and seizure. (This issue has been dealt with by the Alberta Court of Appeal in Southam Inc. v. Hunter (1983), 42 A.R. 108; 3 C.C.C. (3d) 497.)

Counsel for the applicants took the position that to proceed with this type of hearing or inquiry would amount to a denial of natural justice as enshrined in the Charter and therefore the witnesses had a good and sufficient reason not to testify.

[12]          Counsel for the applicants requested access to and copies of all the microfiche filed with the hearing of ficer. The hearing officer denied this request. The entry into evidence of these documents was objected to by counsel for the applicants.

IV

Commencement Procedure:

[16]          The applicants have combined in their prayer for relief a request for both a declaration and an injunction. In seeking this relief, the applicants have proceeded by notice of motion. However, under the practice of this court up to the enactment of the Charter, these remedies could be obtained only in proceedings commenced by a statement of claim, with one exception: interlocutory applications for an injunction may be obtained upon notice of motion, or in certain limited cases, upon ex parte application. Thus, the question that arises in this case, is whether the applicants have employed the proper commencement procedure.

[17]          The relevant Queen’s Bench Rules are reproduced below.

13(1) Except as otherwise provided, every action shall be commenced by issue of a statement of claim in form 2.

441(1) All applications both in court and in chambers shall be by notice of motion except where otherwise specially provided.

[18]          The applicants rely on Rule 441(2) as authorizing the commencement of these proceedings by notice of motion. It is contended that insofar as s. 24(1) of the Charter provides for an application to a court of competent jurisdiction Rule 441(2) is applicable. In my respectful view, this contention cannot be sustained.

[19]          It is unnecessary in this case to determine the precise scope of Rule 441(2). It suffices to say in relation to this case that the expression “application” in Rule 441(2) does not connote the same thing as the expression “application” when considered in the context of s. 24(1) of the Charter. In Rule 441(2), the expression “application” refers to the manner in which proceedings may be commenced. A statute employing the same expression to indicate that proceedings may be taken to enforce a claim or right which the statute has created, comes within the purview of the rule only if it may be inferred from the context that the expression “application” as used in the statute is intended to refer to the mode of proceeding and not merely to the fact that relief is available.

[20]          In this connection, I would observe that the provisions of s. 24(1) of the Charter are substantive in nature. They operate to create a substantive right to relief. The expression “application” in this context is intended to refer simply to the fact that the substantive right to relief which the section gives may be invoked by proceedings taken in a court of competent jurisdiction for that purpose. An “application” in this sense is a compendious term which denotes the types of proceedings which by law are available to secure the form of relief sought. Accordingly, s. 24(1) of the Charter does not come within the purview of Rule 441(2) so as to permit the commencement of these proceedings by notice of motion..

[21]          If there be any doubt on this point, I would add that if the applicants were right in their contention that proceedings commenced pursuant to s. 24(1) of the Charter fall within the ambit of Rule 441(2), then the types of proceedings which could be commenced by notice of motion would be greatly expanded, as s. 24(1) does not restrict the kind of relief which a court of competent jurisdiction may grant in an application pursuant to that section.

[22]          The applicants argue, in the alternative, that Rule 5(4) may be invoked to cure the defect in the commencement procedure. Rule 5(4) provides as follows:

5(4) The court shall not set aside any proceedings or the document by which the proceeding was begun solely on the ground that the proceeding was required by any of these rules to be initiated by a commencement document other than the one employed.

[23]          It should be noted that the curative provisions of Rule 5(4) only apply in situations in which an improper “commencement document” has been employed. In my view a notice of motion is not a commencement document. Thus, Rule 5(4) is not applicable to the present situation.

[24]          However, the fact that Rule 5(4) is not applicable does not mean the proceedings must necessarily be set aside. Rule 5(4) merely deals with one specific type of non-compliance. The Rules dealing with irregularities and non-compliance generally are Rules 5(1) and 5(2). These rules provide as follows:

5(1) Unless the court otherwise orders, any procedural defect, including a failure to comply with these rules, shall be treated as an irregularity and shall not nullify a proceeding, any step taken in the proceeding, or any document, or order made therein.

(2) Where there has been a failure to comply with these rules, the court may, at any time and on such terms and conditions as it thinks just,

(a) set aside a proceeding, either wholly or in part;

(b) set aside any step taken in a proceeding, or a document, or order made therein;

(c) allow an amendment to the pleadings under the rules therefor;

(d) amend any defect or error in a proceeding, in order to secure the Just determination of the real matters in dispute;

(e) make such order as may seem just.

[25]          Rules 5(1) and 5(2) make it clear that non-compliance with the Rules does not automatically nullify the proceedings. The court is given a broad discretion to treat noncompliance as it thinks just.

[26]          In this case the respondent has not objected to the commencement procedure. The facts are not in dispute. All parties are apparently satisfied that all of the evidence is before me.

[27]          Furthermore, in fairness to the applicants, it should be noted that, aside from the general statement in s. 24(1), that an application may be made to a court of competent jurisdiction, the Charter is silent as to the appropriate commencement procedure. The courts have been left with the task of formulating workable rules of procedure to deal with the plethora of litigation arising under the Charter. As the development of procedural rules is still in the infant stage, I am prepared to be somewhat tolerant of what I perceive to be procedural defects.

[28]          For all of these reasons, I would hold that it is not conducive to the ends of justice to set aside these proceedings on the ground that they have been improperly commenced. However, it is hoped that in the future, counsel will commence this type of action by statement of claim rather than by notice of motion.

V

Jurisdiction

[29]          In this case the focal point of the issue of jurisdiction is s. 18 of the Federal Court Act, S.C. 1970-71-72, c. 1. By virtue of that section the Federal Court, Trial Division has exclusive original jurisdiction to entertain proceedings for inter alia, declaratory and injunctive relief against a “federal board, commission or other tribunal”, and to entertain proceedings brought against the Attorney General where the object of these proceedings is to obtain relief against “a federal board, commission or tribunal”.

[30]          For convenience, the relevant provisions of the interpretation section and the provisions of s. 18 are reproduced below.

2. In this Act . . .

(g) ‘federal board, commission or other tribunal’ means any body or any person or persons having, exercising or purporting to exercise jurisdiction or powers conferred by or under an act of the Parliament of Canada, other than any such body constituted or established by or under a law of a province or any such person or persons appointed under or in accordance with a law of a province or under section 9-6 of The British North America Act, 1867; . . .

(m) ‘relief includes every species of relief whether by way of damages, payment of money, injunction, declaration, restitution of an incorporeal right, return of land or chattels or otherwise;

(q) ‘Trial Division’ means that division of the court called the Federal Court-Trial Division.

18. The Trial Division has exclusive original jurisdiction

(a) to issue an injunction, writ of certiorari, writ of prohibition, writ of mandamus or writ of quo warranto, or grant declaratory relief, against any federal board, commission or other tribunal; and

(b) to hear and determine any application or other proceeding for relief in the nature of relief contemplated by paragraph (a), including any proceeding brought against the Attorney General of Canada, to obtain relief against a federal board, commission or other tribunal.

[31]          Also relevant to the issue of jurisdiction is s. 24(1) of the Charter which provides as follows:

24(1) Anyone whose rights or freedoms, as guaranteed by this Charter, have been infringed or denied may apply to a court of competent jurisdiction to obtain such remedy as the court considers appropriate and just in the circumstances.

[32]          I wish to state at the outset that in my view the expression “court of competent jurisdiction” refers to the court which, apart from the Charter, possesses the requisite jurisdiction to entertain proceedings against the person or body being impleaded, and to grant the relief sought. Where an application for relief is brought under s. 24 of the Charter, it must not be presumed that s. 24 operates to fill any lacunae in the jurisdiction of the court selected by the applicant as the appropriate forum.

[33]          A similar view has been expressed by Eberle, J., in Re Regina and Brooks et al. (1983), 1 C.C.C.(3d) 506, at page 509 (Ont. H.C.):

I do not think that it can be said that, by the use of those words in s. 24(1), namely, ‘a court of competent jurisdiction,’ Parliament intended to give all jurisdiction in all matters to all courts. That, it seems to me, is an interpretation going far beyond the phrase itself and the context in which it appears. It seems to me that in giving a person a right to apply to a court of competent jurisdiction, as s. 24 does, the section refers to and points to the court or courts of competent jurisdiction with respect to the matter that is sought to be enforced under s. 24.

[34]          In the formal notice served upon the Attorney General of Canada pursuant to s. 8 of the Constitutonal Questions Act, R.S.S. 1978, c. C-29, the applicants advised that the constitutional validity of s. 18 of the Federal Court Act was being challenged. However, in the original notice of motion and in the amended notice of motion filed subsequent thereto, there is no mention of s. 18 of the Federal Court Act as being among the statutory provisions whose constitutional validity was being impugned in these proceedings. Therefore, I do not propose to deal with the issue of the competency of Parliament to enact s. 18, other than to comment on the scope of the exclusive jurisdiction granted to the Federal Court, Trial Division by s. 18 of that Act.

[35]          The scope of s. 18 of the Federal Court Act has recently been considered by the Supreme Court of Canada in Jabour v. Law Society of British Columbia et oil. and Attorney General of British Columbia et al., [1982] 5 W.W.R. 289; 43 N.R. 451. That case arose, in part, out of the commencement of an inquiry by the Director of the Restrictive Trade Practises Commission into “the production, purchase, sale and supply of legal services and related products in the Province of British Columbia”. The Law Society of British Columbia commenced an action in the Supreme Court of British Columbia in which the Society sought declarations that the Act was not applicable to it, or to the extent to which it purported to be applicable, it was ultra vires the Parliament of Canada, together with an injunction preventing the Director from continuing with the inquiry.

[36]          Counsel for the Attorney General of Canada argued that the Federal Court, Trial Division had exclusive jurisdiction over declaratory or injunctive relief against the Attorney-General of Canada and against a federal tribunal such as the Restrictive Trade Practises Commission. However, the Supreme Court of Canada held that the exclusive jurisdiction granted to the Federal Court, Trial Division by s. 17 and s. 18 of the Federal Court Act did not preclude a Provincial superior court from declaring a statute enacted by Parliament to be beyond the constitutional authority of Parliament.

[37]          The issue of whether a statute or portion thereof, is inconsistent with the Charter is not a constitutional question in the traditional sense. Before the enactment of the Constitution Act, 1982, which entrenched the Charter of Rights and Freedoms as part of the Constitution of Canada, constitutional issues arose only when legislation was challenged as being beyond the jurisdictional limits of Parliament of the Legislatures.

[38]          The role of the judiciary in dealing with these traditional constitutional issues has been described by Lord Watson, in Union Colliery Company of British Columbia Limited v. Bryden, [1899] A.C. 580 (P.C.), at page 585:

In assigning legislative power to the one or the other of these parliaments, it is not made a statutory condition that the exercise of such power shall be, in the opinion of a court of law, discreet. In so far as they possess legislative jurisdiction’, the discretion committed to the parliaments, whether of the Dominion or of the provinces, is unfettered. It is the proper function of a court of law to determine what are the limits of the Jurisdiction committed to them; but, when that point has been settled, courts of law have no right whatever to inquire whether their Jurisdiction has been exercised wisely or not.

[39]          However, the enactment of the Constitution Act, 1982, has altered the role of the judiciary in Canada. The courts are now called on to review the substance of legislation to determine whether the legislation imposes unreasonable restrictions on the rights and freedoms guaranteed by the Charter.

[40]          The Charter is part of the Constitution of Canada. Section 52(1) of the Constitution Act, 1982, states that “any law that is inconsistent with the provisions of the Constitution is, to the extent of the inconsistency, of no force or effect”. Thus, where legislation is challenged as being inconsistent with the rights and freedoms as guaranteed by the Charter, the constitutional validity of the legislation is at issue. Therefore, the reasoning in the Jabour case, supra, applies with equal force to situations, such as the present situation, in which legislation is challenged as being inconsistent with the fundamental rights and freedoms guaranteed by the Charter.

[41]          For this reason, I conclude that s. 18 of the Federal Court Act does not confer exclusive jurisdiction on the Federal Court to decide whether federal legislation is inconsistent with the rights and freedoms guaranteed by the Charter. This court has jurisdiction to make such a determination.

[42]          However, it does not follow from this conclusion that s. 24(1) applications are never within the exclusive jurisdiction of the federal courts. The nature of the violation must be considered. If the violation is the result of actions taken by a federal board that are not expressly authorized by statute, then no issue as to the validity of legislation arises. In my opinion, s. 24(1) applications for relief in respect to actions of a federal board remain within the exclusive jurisdiction of the federal courts.

[43]          The Supreme Court of Canada has pointed out in Howrath v. National Parole Board (1975), 3 N.R. 391; 50 D.L.R.(3d) 349, at p. 351, that supervisory jurisdiction over federal boards has been wholly transferred from the superior courts of the Provinces to the Trial Division of the Federal Court by s. 18 of the Federal Court Act. The Charter has not effected a jurisdictional realignment of the courts. The fact that actions of a federal administrative body are challenged as infringing or denying fundamental rights and freedoms protected by the Charter does not change the supervisory nature of such an action. Where the validity of legislation is not brought into issue, such applications remain exclusively within the jurisdiction of the federal courts: see, for example, Gandam v. Minister of Employment and Immigration [1983), 20 Sask. R. 123; 140 D.L.R.(3d) 363 (Sask. Q.B.); and Jackson v. Minister of National Revenue (1983), 21 Sask. R. 221.

[44]          In the present case the applicants seek a declaration that the proposed inquiry procedure is repugnant to the Charter. There are two aspects to the inquiry procedure that are challenged. The first is the procedure set forth in the Act; the second is the procedure established by Mr. Couture, in his capacity as an appointee of the Restrictive Trade Practises Commission.

[45]          The role that the Commission plays in determining the procedure to be followed in the examination of witnesses under s. 17(1), has recently been the subject of an extensive review by the Federal Court of Appeal in Restrictive Trade Practices Commission et al. v. Irvine et al. (1982), 41 N.R. 375; 65 C.C.C.(2d) 108 (presently on appeal to the Supreme Court of Canada). Chief Justice Thurlow states at p. 118:

As there is no provision prescribing the procedure for the examination of witnesses under s. 17(1), either at the inquiry stage or at any other stage, in my opinion, it is for a member of the Commission to determine what procedure should be followed. What the Commissioner prescribes will, no doubt, depend on the particular proceeding. . . . [W]hether or not cross-examination by persons whose conduct is under investigation or by their counsel is to be permitted in the examination in the course of an inquiry of persons under oath under s. 17(1) is, in my opinion, a matter for the decision of a member of the Commission.

[46]          The Chief Justice went on to rule that it is for the Commission, subject to the provisions for privacy contained in s. 27(1) of the Act, to determine what persons will be permitted to attend the examinations of a person under s. 17(1). The only limitation of the Commission’s discretion in this regard is that he does not have the right to bar counsel who represents the person being examined, since that right is specifically provided in s. 20(1) of the Act.

[47]          I would conclude that, while this court has jurisdiction to grant a declaration with respect to the inquiry procedure set forth in the Act, it does not have jurisdiction to supervise the discretionary actions of the Commission or its appointee. Accordingly, I will deal only with those aspects of the present application related to the validity of the Act.

VI

The Inquiry Procedure:

[48]          The main ground on which the applicants rely in challenging the inquiry procedure set forth in Part II of the Act, is that the investigative procedure authorized by s. 17 infringes their rights and freedoms under s. 7 of the Charter,

[49]          Section 7 of the Charter provides as follows:

Everyone has the right to life, liberty and security of the person and the right not to be deprived thereof except in accordance with the principles of fundamental Justice.

[50]          The portions of s. 17 of the Act which deal with the compelling of evidence, provide as follows:

17(1) On ex parte application of the Director, or on his own motion, a member of the Commission may order that any person resident or present in Canada be examined upon oath before, or make production of books, papers, records or other documents to such member or before or to any other person named for the purpose by the order of such member and may make such orders as seem to him to be proper for securing the attendance of such witness and his examination, and the production by him of books, papers, records or other documents and may otherwise exercise, for the enforcement of such orders or punishment for disobedience thereof, all powers that are exercised by any superior court in Canada for the enforcement of subpoenas to witnesses or punishment of disobedience thereof.

(2) Any person summoned under subsection (1) is competent and may be compelled to give evidence as a witness,

(3) A member of the Commission shall not exercise power to penalize any person pursuant to this Act, whether for contempt or otherwise, unless, on the application of the member, a judge of the Exchequer Court of Canada or of a superior or county court has certified, as such judge may, that the power may be exercised in the matter disclosed in the application, and the member has given to such person twenty-four hours notice of the hearing of the application or such shorter notice as the Judge deems reasonable.

[51]          Section 17(1) authorizes a member of the Commission to order, either ex proprio motu or upon ex parte application of the Director, the examination upon oath of any person resident or present in Canada and the production by that person of relevant documents which are within his power to produce.

[52]          It will be observed that such an order combines the features of both the subpoena duces tecum and the subpoena ad testificandum. On the one hand, it compels the witness to testify as to facts within his personal knowledge which is the function of the subpoena ad testificandum; and on the other, it compels the witness to produce documents which is the function of the subpoena duces tecum. Of course, an order under s. 17 may be concerned only with compelling the witness to give testimonial evidence without requiring production of documents. In the present case, the formal orders directed to the several applicants whose affidavits have been filed in support of this application, require the witness to give testimonial evidence only.

[53]          A person to whom a s. 17(1) order is directed may be compelled under s. 17(2) to give evidence as a witness.

[54]          Under s. 17(3), the member whose order it is has the same powers with respect to the enforcement thereof as any superior court in Canada has for the enforcement of subpoenas to witnesses, including the power to punish for disobedience thereof. However, insofar as the power sought to be exercised is punitive in naure, the member must, as a precondition to punishing an offending party, apply to a court of competent jurisdiction for an order certifying that the power may be exercised.

[55]          Also relevant to the issue of the compulsion of evidence is s. 20 of the Act which provides that:

20(1) A member of the Commission may allow any person whose conduct is being inquired into and shall permit any person who is being himself examined under oath to be represented by counsel.

(2) No person shall be excused from attending and giving evidence and producing books, papers, records or other documents, in obedience to the order of a member of the Commission, on the ground that the oral evidence or documents required of him may tend to criminate him or subject him to any proceeding or penalty, but no oral evidence so required shall be used or receivable against such person in any criminal proceedings thereafter instituted against him, other than a prosecution for perjury in giving such evidence or a prosecution under section 122 or 124 of the Criminal Code in respect of such evidence.

[56]          Section 20 provides a measure of protection against the abuse of the broad investigative powers authorized under s. 17. Section 20(1) permits any person being examined under oath to be represented by counsel. Section 20(2) extends a limited protection against self-incrimination by providing that no oral evidence that may tend to criminate a witness shall be used against such witness in any subsequent criminal proceedings.

[57]          Briefly stated the applicants contend that s. 7 of the Charter operates to guarantee the right to life, liberty and security of the person, and that a necessary concomitant of one or more of these rights is the right not to be compelled to give self-incriminating evidence. The applicants argue that s. 17, which compels the giving of evidence, and s. 20, which provides that a person is not excused from giving evidence on the ground that it may incriminate him, constitute an interference with their right to life, liberty and security of the person that is not in accordance with the principles of fundamental justice.

[58]          In order to pronounce on the merits of the applicants’ position with respect to the investigative powers authorized by s. 17 of the Act, it is necessary to first examine the standard of judicial review authorized by the phrase “fundamental justice”, and the scope of the phrase “life, liberty and security of the person” as contained in s. 7 of the Charter.

a) The Scope of Life, Liberty and Security Of The Person.

[59]          The respondents contend that the scope of s. 7 of the Charter is limited by the enumerated rights in s. 8 to s. 14. More specifically, they argue that since the right not to give self-incriminating evidence is dealt with in s. 11(c) and s. 13 of the Charter, a more general right against self-incrimination cannot be within the scope of s. 7.

[60]          The applicants do not rely on s. 11(c), presumably, because the rights under s. 11 only arise where a person is charged with an offence. The applicants contend instead that the specific rights provided in s. 11(c) and s. 13 should not be construed as limiting the scope of rights in s. 7. Rather, they are specific examples or emanations of the general rights secured by s. 7.

[61]          These submissions necessitate a consideration of the nature of the relationship between the broad rights conferred by s. 7 and the more specific legal rights provided in ss. 8 to 14.

[62]          The starting point of such a consideration is Curr v. The Queen (1972), 7 C.C.C.(2d) 181, 26 D.L.R.(3d) 603 (S.C.C.). The Curr case involved a challenge to the breathalyzer provisions of the Criminal Code, specifically s. 223 (now s. 235), on the grounds that these provisions constituted a violation of certain rights protected by the Canadian Bill of Rights, R.S.C. 1970, App. III. The only issue relevant for present purposes, was whether the compelling of breath samples was a violation of s. 1(a) or s. 2(d) of the Bill of Rights. These sections provide as follows:

1. It is hereby recognized and declared that in Canada there have existed and shall continue to exist without discrimination by reason of race, national origin, colour, religion or sex, the following human rights and fundamental freedoms, namely,

(a) the right of the individual to life, liberty, security of the person and enjoyment of property, and the right not to be deprived thereof except by due process of law; . . .

2. Every law of Canada shall, unless it is expressly declared by an Act of the Parliament of Canada that it shall operate notwithstanding the Canadian Bill of Rights, be so construed and applied as not to abrogate, abridge or infringe or to authorize the abrogation, abridgement or infringement of any of the rights or freedoms herein recognized and declared, and in particular, no law of Canada shall be construed or applied so as to . . .

(d) authorize a court, tribunal, commission, board or other authority to compel a person to give evidence if he is denied counsel, protection against self incrimination or other constitutional safeguards .

[63]          Laskin, J. (as he then was), in delivering the majority judgment in Curr, held that s. 2(d) did not apply because the term “other authority” as used in s. 2(d) did not refer to peace officers. However, after concluding that s. 2(d) did not provide a privilege against compulsory self-incrimination on the facts of that case, the learned Justice, went on to consider whether there was a residual scope to the privilege that fell within s. 1(a).

[64]          Since the respondents put much reliance on his statements in this regard, I wish to quote his conclusion at some length. At page 202 he states:

I do not think that the Canadian Bill of Rights can be construed as having taken a piecemeal approach to the privilege against self-crimination. The history of the privilege as a Canadian derivative from the English common law is accurately reflected in the way it is expressed in s. 2(d): see Wigmore on Evidence, vol. 8 (McNaughton revision, 1961), s. 2250, p. 284 ff. The scope or policy of the privilege may be another thing, as it has proved to be under its constitutional formulation in the United States. Unless s. 2(d), where the privilege is expressed, yields room to take policy beyond history - and I have already dealt with s. 2(d) in this respect - I do not think that s. 1(a), where there is no reference to the privilege and whose words provide no historical warrant for embracing it, can be taken to include in its protection an extension of the privilege beyond what is found in s. 2(d).

No doubt, the generous words of s. l(a) may bring to mind matters other than protection against self-crimination for which protection may be sought thereunder, failing their specific mention elsewhere in the Canadian Bill of Rights. I do not propose to speculate on them; their day of decision may come, but in this case 1 am concerned with a submission that although self-crimination is expressly dealt with in one provision of the statute, this court should find another expression thereof in another provision of the same statute where it is not expressly mentioned,

There is a distinction to be drawn in respect of the privilege which is commanded by the experience with it in this country and in the United States. The point in the criminal process at which the privilege can be asserted is one thing; what the privilege embraces at that point is something else. It is my conclusion that the point of assertion has been fixed in s. 2(d) and I do not think I can invoke s. l(a) to shift it to a stage which would make it effective against s. 223.

[65]          The respondents contend that the reasons of Mr. Justice Laskin apply with equal force to the present consideration. They suggest that s. 11 (c) of the Charter sets the point at which the right not to be compelled to give evidence arises, and s. 7 cannot be invoked to give rise to a similar right at an earlier stage.

[66]          While there is some force in this suggestion, I do not believe that it necessarily follows that since s. 1(a) of the Bill of Rights cannot be invoked to enlarge upon the rights specifically enumerated elsewhere in that legislation, that s. 7 of the Charter cannot be invoked to enlarge upon the rights provided elsewhere in the Charter.

[67]          It cannot be over-emphasized that the enactment of the Constitution Act, 1982, and the Charter contained therein, has fundamentally changed the role the courts play in enforcing rights and freedoms. In my view, in light of this fundamental change, it is incumbent on the courts to review the issues that arise under the Charter without being unduly influenced by pre-Charter decisions.

[68]          The usefulness of cases decided under the Bill of Rights, in determining issues which arise under the Charter, has been considered by Tallis, J.A., in R. v. Therens (1983), 23 Sask. R. 81; 33 C.R.(3d) 204, (Sask. C.A.). In that case the meaning of the term “detained” as used in s. 10 of the Charter was in issue. It was argued that the meaning of the word “detained” should be the same as that attributed to the term in R. v. Chromiak (1980), 29 N.R. 441; 12 C.R.(3d) 300; 102 D.L.R.(3d) 368, (S.C.C.). In rejecting this view Tallis, J.A., stated at page 220:

I am of the opinion that the principles enunciated in R. v. Chromiak and R. V. Dedman [(1981), 23 C.R.(3d) 228] are not determinative of the issues in this case. While cases under statutes such as the Bill of Rights, R.S.C. 1970, App. III, may be of interpretative assistance, it must be remembered that the Charter stands on an entirely different basis. It is not a mere cannon of construction for the interpretation of federal legislation: vide R. v. Drybones, [1910] S.C.R. 282, 10 C.R.W.S. 334, 71 W.W.R. 161, [1970] 3 C.C.C. 355, 9 D.L.R.(3d) 473.

[69]          I am in full agreement with the view expressed by Mr. Justice Tallis.

[70]          Furthermore, support for the view that the Curr case is not particularly helpful, in light of the constitutional entrenchment of the Charter, can be found in the reasons of Laskin, J., in the Curr case itself. In reaching his conclusion Mr. Justice Laskin appears to have been influenced by the fact that under the Bill of Rights the courts have a statutory jurisdiction rather than a constitutional jurisdiction.

At page 191 supra, he states:

Assuming that ‘except by due process of law’ provides a means of controlling substantive federal legislation - a point that did not directly arise in R. v. Drybones - compelling reasons ought to be advanced to justify the court in this case to employ a statutory (as contrasted with a constitutional) jurisdiction to deny operative effect to a substantive measure duly enacted by a Parliament constitutionally competent to do so, and exercising its powers in accordance with the tenents of responsible government, which underlie the discharge of legislative authority under the British North America Act, 1867.

[71]          In Morgentaler v. The Queen (1975), 4 N.R. 277; 20 C.C.C.(2d) 449 (S.C.C.), Chief Justice Laskin again alluded to the importance he attached to the fact that the Bill of Rights was a statutory instrument. At page 461 he states:

This court indicated in the Curr case how foreign to our constitutional traditions, to our constitutional law and to our conceptions of judicial review was any interference by a court with the substantive content of legislation. No doubt, substantive content had to be measured on an issue of ultra vires even prior to the enactment of the Canadian Bill of Rights, and necessary interpretative considerations also had and have a bearing on substantive terms. Of course, the Canadian Bill of Rights, introduced a new dimension in respect of the operation and application of federal law, as the judgments of this court have attested. Yet it cannot be forgotten that it is a statutory instrument, illustrative of Parliament’s primacy within the limits of its assigned legislative authority, and this is a relevant consideration in determining how far the language of the Canadian Bill of Rights should be taken in assessing the quality of federal enactments which are challenged under s. 1(a). There is as much a temptation here as there is on the question of ultra vires to consider the wisdom of the legislation, and I think it is our duty to resist it in the former connection as in the latter.

[72]          The enactment of the Constitution Act, 1982 has, changed the fundamental principle underlying the Curr case. The courts now have a constitutional jurisdiction to review legislation; the Charter is not merely a statutory instrument. Therefore, in my view, it is incumbent on the courts to reconsider the reasoning in Curr in light of these fundamental changes.

[73]          I would also note that the Curr decision has recently been reviewed by the British Columbia Court of Appeal in Reference Re Section 94(2) of Motor Vehicle Act R.S.B.C. 1979, c. 288 (1983), 33 C.R.(3d) 22. That court in dealing with the issue of whether s. 7 of the Charter should be given substantive effect, chose not to treat the statements made in Curr, with respect to s. 1(a) of the Bill of Rights, as being determinative of that issue.

[74]          For the above reasons, I conclude that the Curr case is not determinative of the relationship between s. 7 and ss. 8 to 14. The proper relationship between these provisions should be determined through an examination of the Charter itself, bearing in mind the purpose and the constitutional nature of that document.

[75]          It is suggested that a rule of statutory interpretation, expressed in the maxim expressio unius est exclusio alterius - the expression of one thing is the exclusion of another - should apply. As a matter of statutory interpretation, it is argued, the express right not to be compelled to be a witness provided in s. 11(c) necessarily precludes the implication of a similar right in s. 7. It is also argued that the protection against self-incrimination provided in s. 13 precludes the implication of similar protection in s. 7.

[76]          The problem with this argument is that it assumes that a constitutional document should be construed according to the ordinary rules of statutory interpretation. The fallacy of such a view has been pointed out in Minister of Home Affairs v, Fisher, [1980] A.C. 319; [1979] 3 All E.R. 21. That case involved the interpretation of the word “child” as used in the Bermuda Constitution Act, 1967. It was argued that the Constitution should be interpreted the same as any English statute, thus, a presumption that the word “child” means ligitimate child should apply. Lord Wilberforce, in giving the opinion of the Privy Council, dealt with this argument as follows at page 329:

. . . [T]he question must inevitably be asked whether the appellants’ premise, fundamental to their argument, that these provisions are to be construed in the manner and according to the rules which apply to Acts of Parliament, is sound. In their Lordships’ view there are two possible answers to this. The first would be to say that, recognizing the status of the Constitution as, in effect, an Act of Parliament, there is room for interpreting it with less rigidity, and greater generosity, than other Acts, such as those which are concerned with property, or succession, or citizenship. On the particular question this would require the court to accept as a starting point the general presumption that ‘child’ means ‘legitimate child’ but to recognize that this presumption may be more easily displaced. The second would be more radical: it would be to treat a constitutional instrument such as this as sui generis, calling for principles of interpretation of its own, suitable to its character as already described, without necessary acceptance of all the presumptions that are relevant to legislation of private law.

It is possible that, as regard the question now for decision, either method would lead to the same result. But their Lordships prefer the second. This is in no way to say that there are no rules of law which should apply to the interpretation of a constitution. A constitution is a legal instrument giving rise, amongst other things, to individual rights capable of enforcement in a court of law. Respect must be paid to the language which has been used and to the traditions and usages which have given meaning to that language. It is quite consistent with this, and with the recognition that rules of interpretation may apply, to take as a point of departure for the process of interpretation a recognition of the character and origin of the instrument, and to be guided by the principle of giving full recognition and effect to those fundamental rights and freedoms with a statement of which the Constitution commences. In their Lordships’ opinion this must mean approaching the question what is meant by ‘child’ with an open mind.

[77]          Approaching the interpretation of the Charter in this manner, guided by the principle of giving full recognition and effect to the rights and freedoms guaranteed therein, and considering the language used in the Charter, I see no reason to view ss. 8 to 14 as imposing limits on the rights bestowed by s. 7.

[78]          Section 26 of the Charter indicates an intention that the enunciated rights should not be construed as excluding rights not expressly mentioned. Section 26 of the Charter provides as follows:

26. The guarantee in this Charter of certain rights and freedoms shall not be construed as denying the existence of any other rights or freedoms that exist in Canada.

[79]          In addition, s. 1 provides that the rights guaranteed by the Charter are subject “only to such reasonable limits prescribed by law as can be demonstrably justified in a free and democratic society”. In light of this general limitation it would seem unnecessary and inappropriate to read further restrictions into s. 7.

[80]          In my view, the specific rights enumerated in s. 8 to s. 14 are specific examples or emanations of the general right to life, liberty and security of the person, and the specific mention of these rights serves to reinforce the general rights secured by s. 7, rather than to restrict them.

[81]          Although the specific rights enumerated in s. 8 to s. 14 may come within the scope of the compendious phrase “life, liberty and security of the person”, the framers of the Charter in placing certain specific rights outside of s. 7 afforded them an additional measure of sanctity. Under s. 7 a person may be deprived of his rights if the deprivation is in accordance with the principles of fundamental justi’ce. The specific rights in ss. 8 to 14 are not so limited.

[82]          If the relationship between s. 7 and the following sections is viewed in this way, s. 11(c) does not preclude a right not to be compelled to be a witness against oneself from arising before a person is charged. Rather, s. 11(c) provides additional protection by setting the point at which the right not to be compelled to be a witness against oneself is no longer subject to possible deprivation in accordance with the principles of fundamental justice.

[83]          Similarly, s. 13 guarantees to a witness the specific right not to have self-incriminating evidence used against him in other proceedings. This is a separate right which arises regardless of whether the witness testifies voluntarily or under compulsion. This positive right conferred by s. 13 should not be taken as a constitutional adoption of the statutory scheme established by s. 5 of the Canada Evidence Act, R.S.C. 1970, c. E-10. There is nothing in s. 13 that expressly makes a person compellable to give self-incriminating evidence. Although s. 13 is similar to s. 5(2) of the Canada Evidence Act, and s. 20(2) of the Combines Investigation Act, there is no equivalent to s. 5(1) of the Canada Evidence Act, or to s. 17(2) of the Combines Investigation Act, contained in the Charter. The restrictive provisions of these latter sections should not be read into the Charter as necessarily implicit in the positive rights conferred by s. 13.

[84]          For these reasons I would hold that the specific rights in s. 11(c) and s. 13 do not necessarily preclude a more general right against the compelling of self-incriminating evidence from being within the scope of s. 7. It remains to be considered whether the compelling of such evidence, as authorized by s. 17 of the Act, is an interference with a person’s right to liberty and security of the person.

[85]          I do not propose to attempt any exhaustive definition of the range of rights encompassed by the phrase “life, liberty and security of the person”. Indeed, it would be impossible to define the scope of this phrase with any degree of exactness. The boundaries of this broad right will undoubtedly be developed by the courts interstitially as different claims arise.

[86]          In the present case the applicants allege that the phrase includes a right not to compelled to give self-incriminating evidence. This claim does not require any judicial exploration of the penumbra of the right to life, liberty and security of the person. Although the right to life is not in issue here, in my view, the privilege against self-incrimination is deeply rooted in the right to liberty and security of the person.

[87]          The history of the privilege against self-incrimination is well documented in Levy, Origins of The Fifth Amendment, New York: Oxford University Press, 1968. Levy points out that the privilege against self-incrimination was at the root of the historic struggle for individual liberty.

[88]          Furthermore, the phrase “security of the person” includes a right to personal dignity and a right to an area of privacy or individual sovereignty into which the state must not make arbitrary or unjustified intrusions. These considerations also underly the privilege against self-incrimination.

[89]          In the landmark American case, Miranda v. Arizona, 384 U.S. 436; 16 L. ed. (2d) 694; 86 S.Ct. 1602, the United States Supreme Court examined the historical and policy underpinnings of the privilege against self-incrimination. Chief Justice Warren, in delivering the opinion of the court, made the following remarks at page 460:

Thus we may view the historical development of the privilege as one which groped for the proper scope of governmental power over the citizen. . . . All of these policies point to one overriding thought: the constitutional foundation underlying the privilege is a respect a government - state or federal - must accord to the dignity and integrity of its citizens.

[90]          The policy underlying the privilege against self-incrimination is also examined at length in Wigmore on Evidence, vol. 8 (McNaughton Revision, 1961), s. 2251. Wigmore concludes that there are only two valid purposes to the privilege. He identifies these purposes at page 318:

The first is to remove the right to an answer in the hard cores of instances where compulsion might lead to inhumanity, the principal inhumanity being abusive tactics by a zealous questioner. The second is to comply with the prevailing ethic that the individual is sovereign and that proper rules of battle between government and individual require that the individual not to be bothered for less than good reason and not to be conscripted by his opponent to defeat himself.

[91]          Section 7 of the Charter has given constitutional recognition to the principle that the individual is sovereign, and that there are limits to the valid exercise of governmental authority over the individual. One such limit, that is deeply rooted in the individual’s right to “liberty and security of the person”, is that a person should not be compelled to incriminate himself.

[92]          I am mindful that the Ontario Court of Appeal in R. v. Altaeimer (1983), 1 C.C.C.(3d) 7, has held that the Charter does not confer a broad privilege against self-incrimination. In that case the Ontario Court of Appeal referred to a brief review of the common law privilege against self-incrimination made by Dickson, J., in R. v. Marooux and Solomon (1976), 4 N.R. 64; 24 C.C.C.(2d) 1, at page 5 and cited his conclusion as follows:

In short, the privilege extends to the accused qua witness and not qua accused, it is concerned with testimonial compulsion specifically and not with compulsion generally.. . .

[93]          The Ontario Court of Appeal in stating that the Charter does not confer a broad privilege against self-incrimination was merely concluding that s. 11(c) and s. 13 of the Charter apply only to testimonial compulsion. However, the court did not consider the nature of the rights secured by s. 7. In my view the Altseimer case should not be viewed as holding that the compelling of self-incriminating evidence is not an interference with the rights secured by s. 7.

[94]          The respondents raise an additional issue related to the proper scope of s. 7. They contend that even if s. 7 does encompass the right not to be compelled to give self-incriminating evidence, the beneficial provisions of s. 7 do not extend to corporations. They argue that the term “everyone”, as used in s. 7 does not include corporations. Thus, they argue, the corporate applicants cannot enjoy rights secured by s. 7.

[95]          Although this issue of whether or not the beneficial provisions of s. 7 extend to corporations may be critical in certain applications, it is not necessary to decide this issue in the present case.

[96]          As I have indicated earlier in these reasons, this court’s jurisdiction in this matter is limited to a determination of the validity of the impugned provisions of the Act. If s. 17 is inconsistent with the rights of the individual applicants, then it is of no force or effect. In my view, the possibility that the same provision may not be inconsistent with the rights of the corporate applicants is irrelevant. If the provision is inconsistent with any rights secured by the Charter, it must be held invalid. It is not the proper function of this court to rewrite the impugned provision in order to remedy the inconsistency by making it applicable only to persons whose rights may not be protected.

[97]          However, before leaving this issue, I would like to make some general comments on the respondents’ submissions.

[98]          I do not believe that the issue of whether the rights guaranteed by s. 7 extend to corporations can be determined on a narrow and technical interpretation of the term “everyone”. Regard must be had to the nature of the rights provided by that section.

[99]          This approach was adopted in Southern Inc. v. Hunter et al. (1982), 42 A.R. 109; 68 C.C.C.(2d) 356 (Alberta Q.B.), by Mr. Justice Cavanagh in determining whether the term “everyone” as used in s. 8 of the Charter includes corporations. He expressed his view as follows at page 364:

I would hold that ‘everyone’ as used in section 8 should include all human beings and all entities that are capable of enjoying the benefit of security against unreasonable search. This then would include corporations. That interpretation would not be inconsistent in the other sections where the word ‘everyone’ is used where only human beings can enjoy the rights given.

[100]       This statement has been adopted by Scollin, J., in Balderstone v. R. and Manitoba, Attorney General of (1983), 19 Man. R.(2d) 321; 2 C.C.C. (3d) 37 (Manitoba Q.B.). In that case, Mr. Justice Scollin concluded that a corporation is entitled to the legal rights guaranteed by the Charter, including those secured by s. 7.

[101]       A similar approach to that adopted by Cavanagh, J., has also been taken by at least one member of the British Columbia Court of Appeal in Re PPG Industries Canada Ltd. and Attorney General of Canada (1983), 3 C.C.C.(3d) 97. In that case the court was called upon to determine whether the right to a jury trial provided in s. 11(f) of the Charter should be extended to corporations. The issue turned on the meaning to be given to the term “person”. Nemetz, C.J.B.C., delivering one of the majority judgments stated at page 103:

In my view, the context dictates that a restricted meaning be given to the word ‘person’. In the context of section 11(f), ‘person’ must mean a natural person, since it is only such natural persons who can be subjected to imprisonment for five years or more.

[102]       The approach adopted in these cases seems to me to be a sensible one. There is no need to ascribe to the term “everyone” a technical meaning, and then to adopt that meaning wherever that term appears.

[103]       Since the term “everyone” is capable of including non-natural persons, in my view, it should be construed as including such where the rights secured are capable of being enjoyed by non-natural persons. Such an approach is in accordance with the principle of giving a broad and liberal interpretation to a constitutional document.

[104]       In summary, it is my opinion that s. 7 should be given a broad scope, that the specific rights enumerated in s. 8 to s. 14 should be viewed as reinforcing the broad general rights secured by s. 7, and that the right to “life and liberty of the person” secured by s. 7 encompasses a right not to be compelled to give self-incriminating evidence.

(b) The Standard of Fundamental Justice

[105]       Having concluded that a right not to be compelled to give self-incriminating evidence is within the scope of s. 7, it becomes clear that Part II of the Act constitutes an interference with this right. Section 17 makes a person compellable, and s. 20(2) provides that a person is not excused from giving evidence on the ground that the evidence may tend to incriminate him. However, the crucial issue is whether this interference is in accordance with the principles of fundamental justice.

[106]       A determination of this issue necessarily involves a consideration of what standard of judicial review is contemplated by the phrase “principles of fundamental justice”. Does this phrase contemplate substantive review or merely procedural review? In either case what yardstick is suggested to measure whether legislation, which interferes with protected rights, is in accordance with the principles of fundamental justice?

[107]       If the phrase “principles of fundamental justice” merely contemplates procedural review, then legislation is open to attack only on the ground that the procedure prescribed or adopted for the achievement of certain policies falls below a minimum acceptable standard recognized as being indispensable for the enjoyment of life, liberty and security of the person. On the other hand, if the phrase contemplates substantive review, then legislation is also open to attack on the ground that the policies are in themselves incompatible with the enjoyment of such rights.

[108]       The Bill of Rights contains a similar provision to s. 7 of the Charter, except s. 1(a) of the Bill of Rights uses the phrase “due process of law” instead of “the principles of fundamental justice”. The courts in interpreting the due process clause in the Bill of Rights have consistently held that all that is meant by this phrase is that established legal pro cedure must be followed: see for example, Re State of Wisconsin and Armstrong (1973), 32 D.L.R.(3d) 265 (F.C.A.).

[109]       The fact that the framers of the Charter decided not to use the due process of law clause in the Charter would seem to indicate a desire that s. 7 be given a broader interpretation than that attributed to s. 1(a) of the Bill of Rights.

[110]       In Curr v. The Queen, supra, Laskin, J. (as he then was), although he did not expressly rule out the possibility that the due process clause in s. 1(a) of the Bill of Rights could be used as a means of challenging the validity of substantive legislation, indicated that he would be reluctant to employ a statutory jurisdiction to deny effect to substantive legislation. As I have pointed out earlier, the entrenchment of the Charter has removed this constraint. The courts now have a constitutional jurisdiction to deny effect to substantive legislation that is inconsistent with the provisions of the Charter. Section 52 of the Constitutional Act, 1982 expressly imposes this duty on the courts.

[111]       The phrase “principles of fundamental justice” is used in s. 2(e) of the Bill of Rights. This section has received judicial consideration by the Supreme Court of Canada in Duke v. The Queen (1972), 28 D.L.R.(3d) 129. In that case Chief Justice Fauteux remarked as follows at page 134:

Under s. 2(e) of the Bill of Rights no law of Canada shall be construed or applied so as to deprive him of ‘a fair hearing in accordance with the principles of fundamental justice’. Without attempting to formulate any final definition of those words, I would take them to mean, generally, that the tribunal which adjudicates upon his rights must act fairly, in good faith, without bias and. in a judicial temper, and must give to him the opportunity adequately to state his case.

[112]       This definition clearly contemplates a procedural standard of review. However, the context in which the phrase is used in s. 2(e) must be considered. It is used in reference to the right to a fair hearing which is a procedural right. The Chief Justice was not merely defining the phrase “principles of fundamental justice”, he was considering what is meant by the phrase “a fair hearing in accordance with the principles of fundamental justice”. In my view, the statement of Chief Justice Fauteux is not particularly helpful in determining the meaning of the phrase as it appears in s. 7. In s. 7 the phrase “principles of fundamental justice” appears in an entirely different context.

[113]       In this regard, I would refer to a note by John D. Whyte in (1983), 32 C.R.(3d) 107. Professor Whyte states at page 114:

Section 2(e) of the Canadian Bill of Rights, R.S.C. 1970, App. III, in a context which is clearly limited to the notion of fair hearing, uses the phrase ‘fundamental Justice’, and this incident of use may be what led some to believe that ‘fundamental Justice’ was merely an alternative to ‘natural justice’. Section 7 of the Charter is, of course, devoid of any such contextual limitation and, from the point of view of initial impressions, there is neither any reason to assume that ‘fundamental justice’ must be given the restricted meaning of ‘natural Justice’ nor any reason not to see ‘principles of fundamental justice’ as encompassing the question of whether substantive concepts of justice have been complied with. After all, the natural reading to give to the word ‘justice’ is not a set of procedural standards, but rather a political virtue applicable to the allocation of benefits and punishments.

[114]       The issue of whether s. 7 has a substantive effect has been discussed in a number of recent cases. Several of these have held that s. 7 is merely procedural: see for example, R. v. Gallant (1983), 70 C.C.C.(2d) 213 (Ont. Prov. Ct.); R. v. Holman (1982), 28 C.R.(3d) 378 (B.C. Prov. Ct.); Re Balderstone v. The Queen, supra, and, Re Jamieson v. The Queen (1983), 70 C.C.C.(2d) 430 (Que. Sup. Ct.). Other cases have attributed a substantive effect to s. 7: see for example, R. v. Rolbin (1982), 1 C.R.R. 186 (Que. Ct. of Sess.), affirmed (1983), 2 C.R.R. 166 (Que. Sup. Ct.); R. v. Carriere (1983), 32 C.R.(3d) 117 (Ont. Prov. Ct.); R. v. Hayden (1983), 33 C.R.(3d) 363 (Man. Prov. Ct.); and, R. v. Campagna (1983), 70 C.C.C.(2d) 236 (B.C. Prov. Ct.).

[115]       The only appellate court to deal with this issue, that has come to my attention, is Reference Re Section 94(2) of Motor Vehicle Act, supra. In that case the British Columbia Court of Appeal examined the phrase “principles of fundamental justice” and concluded at page 30 as follows:

. . . [T]he meaning to be given to the phrase ‘principles of fundamental justice’ is that it is not restricted to matters of procedure but extends to substantive law and that the courts are therefore called upon, in construing the provisions of s. 7 of the Charter, to have regard to the content of legislation.

[116]       For all of these reasons, I would conclude that the phrase “principles of fundamental justice” should not be interpreted as limiting the courts to a review of procedural matters. Rather, it has opened the door to a review of the substance of legislation that interferes with a person’s right to life, liberty and security of the person.

[117]       The more fundamental question is what yardstick is suggested by the phrase “principles of fundamental justice” against which the validity of substantive legislation can be measured? What sort of considerations justify an interference with the right to life, liberty and security of the person?

[118]       Some insight into this difficult problem can be gained by examining those cases that have held substantive legislation to be of no force or effect on the ground that it constituted an interference with s. 7 rights, and this interference was not in accordance with the principles of fundamental justice.

[119]       In R. v. Hayden, supra, Judge Kopstein held that s. 97(b) of the Indian Act, R.S.C. 1970, c. I-6, which created an offence of being intoxicated on an Indian reserve was inconsistent with s. 7 and thus, of no force and effect. Judge Kopstein considered the meaning of the “principles of fundamental justice” as follows at page 367:

Section 7 of the Charter permits deprivations of liberty only in accordance with the principles of fundamental Justice. Considering the disruption of life, the assault upon human dignity and the degradation associated with being taken into custody by a police agency, a humanitarian consideration which I conceive to be a principle of fundamental justice is that law should not jeopardize the right of the liberty of any person save and except where the weight of compelling factors such as the need for public protection, or the need to preserve public order, or the need of law enforcement agencies to investigate crime and preserve evidence, or the principles of general or individual deterrence - the legitimate interests of society which justify an -interference with personal liberty - override the importance of the right to liberty. The absence of such overriding factors in the promulgation or perpetuation of a law which authorizes arrest ignores that principle of fundamental justice.

[120]       Judge Kopstein went on to review the various provincial liquor acts and concluded that the tendency among the majority of jurisdictions across this country is to avoid arrest and punitive detentions for intoxication alone. He went on to hold, at page 370, that under s. 97(b) of the Indian Act:

The power to arrest and the loss of liberty which will result from the exercise of that power does not rest upon any factor which is so important to the interests of society as to bring it within a principle of fundamental justice.

[121]       In Reference Re Section 94(2) of Motor Vehicle Act, supra, the British Columbia Court of Appeal held that a section of the Motor Vehicle Act, R.S.B.C. 1979, c. 288 that created an offence of absolute liability with a mandatory minimum term of imprisonment was inconsistent with the Charter. In deciding that the impugned legislation constituted a deprivation of liberty not in accordance with the principles of fundamental justice, the Court of Appeal relied heavily on statements made by Dickson, J., in R. v. Sault Ste. Marie (1978), 21 N.R. 295; 40 C.C.C.(2d) 353, (S.C.C.), to the effect that it is contrary to fundamental principles of penal liability to convict a person of a crime involving a substantial term of imprisonment, where that person has acted without guilty intent.

[122]       In R. v. Campagna, supra, Judge Paradis also held that the same provision of the British Columbia Motor Vehicle Act was inconsistent with the Charter. He also referred to Mr. Justice Dickson’s reference to fundamental principles of penal liability in R. v. City of Sault Ste. Marie. Judge Paradis, however, went on to further support his finding that the impugned legislation was not in accordance with the principles of fundamental justice, as follows at page 246:

In the Canadian legal fabric, offences which carry a minimum penalty of a term of imprisonment are rare. I have searched in vain for any such offence which imposes a burden of absolute liability. The fact that s. 94.1(2) and (3) stand alone in that respect is significant. A serious problem was seen to exist and Draconian measures were thought necessary to deal with it. However, those measures went far beyond what has, in the past, been seen as reasonable by any other Legislature.

[123]       Judge Paradis, at page 246, then referred to the following quote by Mr. Justice Rand in his article “Except by Due Process of Law” (1961), 2 Osgoode Hall, L.J. 171:

At certain dimensions we can without doubt pronounce legislation outside of the bounds of traditional process, as where the issue of river-pilot licenses is limited to persons of blood relation to presently licensed pilots; or legislation that eliminates most women as barmaids; or legislation that would take property for an exclusively private purpose, or for a public purpose without compensation. With such departures from the norm of living tradition we have no difficulty and they give a clue to the character of action which due process condemns.

[124]       Judge Paradis then stated his conclusions as follows at page 247:

In my view, to automatically deprive a citizen of his liberty by a process of absolute liability can only be seen as a ‘departure from the norm of living tradition’.

[125]       In R. v. Carriere, supra, Ontario Provincial Court Judge Belanger held that the legislation creating writs of assistance constitute a violation of the right to life, liberty and security of the person that is not in accordance with the principles of fundamental justice. In holding that s. 7 should be given a substantive effect he stated at page 130:

It follows that those legal processes hitherto recognized by Parliament and the courts are no longer necessarily the standards justifying deprivation of the right to liberty and security of the person.

He went on to state his reasons for finding writs of assistance to be inconsistent with s. 7 of the Charter as follows at page 131-32:

I am of the view that writs of assistance offend the principles of fundamental justice. The history of the development of our criminal law is also the history of the development of restrictions and controls on the actions of individuals charged with the enforcement of that law. Our democratic society’s will, legislatively expressed, has progressively become more hesitant to grant wide discretion to the enforcer lest he, quite apart from wrongful or illegal motives, confuse his duty with his own view of the law and lend his own colour to events giving rise to collectively defined rights and wrongs. To this date the only working method devised to adequately monitor his actions has been the interposition of independent judicial scrutiny at every significant juncture in the criminal process. It is a method which is far from perfect, because it is cumbersome and often inexpedient, but it is difficult to conceive of a better one.

One such juncture - and a most crucial one - arises when police forces are required to invade a person’s privacy in order to obtain evidence of crime. There can be no doubt that privacy cannot be held up in all circumstances as a shield to prevent the detection of crime, but I conceive that it is so important and fundamental a concept in our society that interference with it should occur only when it is clear and has been demonstrably made clear that it cannot stand as an impediment to our collective right to protection from crime. Convenience and expediency merit consideration only to the extent that they are reconcilable with that concept.

[126]       Several themes reoccur in these cases that are possible yardsticks for reviewing substantive legislation. These are: the historical development of rights protected by the common law, the legislative tradition in Canada as found in an analogous existing legislation, and community standards or the “living tradition”. There is also reference to the need for compelling factors to justify placing the collective interests of society ahead of the rights of the individual.

[127]       While I am not suggesting that these concepts make substantive review a simple matter of application, I do believe that these concepts can provide useful guidance.

[128]       With this by way of background, I now turn to examine whether the interference with the applicants’ right to liberty and security of the person, as authorized by s. 17 of the Act, is in accordance with the principles of fundamental justice.

[129]       The common law privilege against self-incrimination developed out of public resentment of the Court of Star Chamber procedure, and in particular the oath ex officio. (For a complete historical development of the privilege see: Levy, Origins Of The Fifth Amendment, supra.)

[130]       Professor Ratushny traces the development of the privilege in his book, Self-incrimination In The Canadian Criminal Process, Toronto: Carswell, 1979. He points out that the offensive nature of the Star Chamber procedure was that arbitrary power was exercised in subjecting persons to inquiries under oath for the purpose of making out an accusation against them. He states at page 172:

The repugnance was found in the procedure whereby a person would be called before Judges without any specific allegation of wrong-doing on his part and subjected to a broad range of questions.

[131]       During the 16th century the common law courts began to restrict the Star Chamber by granting prohibition against the administering of the oath ex officio. In so doing, they began to state the general rule, nemo tenetur prodere seipsum, or no one is bound to betray himself. Professor Ratushny refers to this development at page 169:

Although the Latin phrase has been the subject of considerable analysis, its precise origins are not clear. However, it is quite possible that in the 16th century common law courts, it was merely an impressive way of stating the existing common law rule that no man was bound to produce the accusation against himself.

[132]       A classic statement of the common law privilege against self-incrimination that evolved from these historic roots is found in Lamb v. Munster (1882), 10 Q.B. 110. At page 113 Stephen, J., states the rule as follows:

It is not that a man must be guilty of an offence and say substantially, ‘I am guilty of the offence, but I am not going to furnish evidence of it.’ I do not think the privilege is so narrow as that, for then it would be illusory. The extent of the privilege is I think this: the man may say, ‘if you are going to bring a criminal charge, or if I have reason to think a criminal charge is going to be brought against me, I will hold my tongue. Prove what you can, but I am protected from furnishing evidence against myself out of my own mouth’.

[133]       Canadian legislative tradition has long since parted company with the common law privilege. The Canada Evidence Act of 1893 contained a provision substantially the same as the present s. 5. (For a review of the history and debate surrounding the original Canada Evidence Act see: Noble, ‘The Struggle To Make The Accused Competent In England And Canada’ (1970), 8 Osgoode Hall, L.J. 249.)

[134]       The original Canada Evidence Act of 1893 made the accused competent but not compellable, and made other witnesses compellable to answer questions which may tend to incriminate them, while protecting them from use of the evidence in subsequent criminal proceedings against themselves.

[135]       Criminal trials were clearly the focus of concern that lead to the removal of the common law privilege against self-incrimination, but the language was not restricted to criminal proceedings. As administrative bodies began to play an increasing role in the investigation of criminal activities, s. 5 of the Canada Evidence Act, and its provincial equivalents, were applied to these forms of inquiry. However, the removal of a witness’s privilege against self-incrimination in administrative inquiries gives rise to entirely different considerations.

[136]       The focus of the criminal proceedings is the conduct of the accused; its purpose is to determine the guilt or innocence of the accused. In criminal proceedings the accused’s privilege against self-incrimination is protected, at trial, by his statutory right of non-compellability, and at the pre-trial stage, by the exclusionary rules of evidence.

[137]       A witness in a criminal proceeding is compellable to testify as to the activities or conduct of the accused. The removal of the witnesses’ privilege against self-incrimination may result in the witness being compelled to reveal self-incriminating evidence. But that is not the purpose of the compulsion. The compelling of self-incriminating evidence is merely incidental to the purpose of the criminal proceeding.

[138]       The removal of the privilege from witnesses in criminal proceedings reflects a policy that the incidental incrimination of a witness must not be allowed to thwart the legitimate purpose of the proceedings: the just determination of the guilt or innocence of the accused.

[139]       An administrative inquiry, on the other hand, may be directed at uncovering illegal activity on the part of the witness. The denial of that witness’s privilege against self-incrimination in this situation may result in the witness being compelled to assist in an investigation into his criminal activity.

[140]       This result has been recognized by Dickson, J., in Di Iorio and Fontaine v. Warden of the Common Jail of Montreal and Brunet et al. (1977), 8 N.R. 361; 73 D.L.R.(3d) 491 (S.C.C.). In that case the appellants were found guilty of contempt for refusing to answer questions before the Quebec Police Commission Inquiry into organized crime. They appealed their convictions, inter alia, on the ground that the Commission’s power interfered with their right to protection against self-incrimination. At page 539 Dickson, J., stated:

Whether or not one agrees with a result which may force a person to assist in an investigation of his criminal activity, the provisions of s. 5 of the Canada Evidence Act and both federal and provincial Inquiries Acta compel such a result.

[141]       Mr. Justice Dickson’s statement reflects the prevailing principle, at that time, of parliamentary supremacy. This principle, however, has now been limited by the entrenchment of the Charter. Under the Charter a result is no longer justified solely on the ground that it is dictated by duly enacted legislation, it must also be in accordance with the principles of fundamental justice.

[142]       The compelling of a person to assist in an investigation of his own misconduct, of course, does not occur in all administrative inquiries. In my view, this result is dictated only where the administrative body has formed a suspicion that certain persons are involved in criminal activity and the inquiry is used to gather evidence of the criminal activity of those persons. In other words, at least one of the purposes of the inquiry is to assist in the eventual criminal prosecution of specific suspects.

[143]       Section 8 of the Act authorizes the commencement of an inquiry. This section provides as follows:

The Director shall

(a) on application made under section 7.

(b) whenever he has reason to believe that

(i) a person has contravened or failed to comply with an order made pursuant to section 29, 29.1 or 30,

(ii) grounds exist for the making of an order by the Commission under Part IV.1, or

(iii) an offence under Part V or section 46.1 has been or is about to be committed, or

(c) whenever he is directed by the Minister to inquire whether any of the circumstances described in subparagraphs (b)(i) to (iii) exists,

cause an inquiry to be made into all such matters as he considers necessary to inquire into with the view of determining the facts.

[144]       The grounds for commencing an inquiry are all predicated on a suspicion of wrongdoing formed by the Director. There is no requirement that the Director disclose the grounds for his suspicions, or the identity of the persons suspected. In fact there is no requirement that the suspicion be reasonable.

[145]       Once the inquiry has been commenced, s. 17 can be invoked to compel a person, who may be a suspect, to answer an unrestricted range of questions, without any requirement that a person be advised of any allegations against him.

[146]       Section 18(1) of the Act does make some provision for informing suspects of allegations against them. The provision reads as follows:

18(1) At any stage of an inquiry,

(a) the Director may, if he is of the opinion that the evidence obtained discloses a situation contrary to any provision in Part V, and

(b) the Director shall, if the inquiry relates to an alleged or suspected offence under any provision of Part V and he is so required by the Minister, prepare a statement of evidence obtained in the inquiry which shall be submitted to the Commission and to each person against whom an allegation is made therein.

However, it will be noted that the suspect has no right to be informed of an allegation against him. The matter is in the discretion of the Director or the Minister.

[147]       Finally, in my view, s. 17 may be an integral step in an eventual criminal prosecution of a suspected person. The immediate result of the inquiry is either a referral of evidence to the Attorney General of Canada under s. 15(1) or a report to the Minister under s. 19(1). In either case the evidence gathered may form the basis for a subsequent criminal prosecution.

[148]       I would conclude that the procedure authorized by s. 17 of the Act brings us full circle back to the original concerns out of which the privilege against self-incrimination developed. It authorizes the arbitrary compelling of a person to assist in his own prosecution.

[149]       Furthermore, in my view, there are no compelling factors to justify the result dictated by s. 17. The public interest in orderly competition is not so compelling as to override this serious interference with the right to liberty and security. In fact, the public has an interest in not allowing this form of compulsory self-incrimination.

[150]       This public interest is recognized in Wigmore on Evidence (3d Ed. 1940), vol. 8, at p. 309:

[A]ny system of administration which permits the prosecution of trust habitually to compulsory self-disclosure as a source of proof must itself suffer morally thereby. The inclination develops to rely mainly upon such evidence, and to be satisfied with an incomplete investigation of the other sources. The exercise of the power to extract answers begets a forgetfulness of the Just limitations of that power. The simple and peaceful process of questioning breeds a readiness to resort to bullying and to physical force and torture. If there is a right to an answer, there soon seems to be a right to the expected answer, that is, to a confession of guilt. Thus the legitimate use grows into the unjust abuse; ultimately, the innocent are jeopardized by the encroachments of a bad system. Such seems to have been the course of experience in those legal systems where the privilege was not recognized.

[151]       I am also of the opinion that the protection against subsequent use of self-incriminating testimony provided in s. 20(2) of the Act and s. 13 of the Charter does not justify the result dictated by s. 17. The protection against subsequent use of self-incriminating evidence may be adequate in situations in which the witness is not an accused or a suspect, but such protection falls short of the standard of fundamental justice in situations in which the witness is compelled to assist in an investigation of his own misconduct.

[152]       After much consideration, I am forced to the conclusion that s. 17 of the Act is inconsistent with the individual applicant’s rights secured by s. 7 of the Charter.

[153]       Furthermore, in my view, s. 17 is not saved by s. 1 of the Charter, As I have indicated the public interest is not such as to make this interference with fundamental rights demonstrably justified in a free and democratic society.

[154]       Accordingly, I would hold s. 17 of the Combines Investigation Act to be of no force or effect.

VII

Section 45 Of The Act:

[155]       The applicants also contend that s. 45 of the Act is inconsistent with their rights under s. 7 and s. 11(d) of the Charter.

[156]       Section 45 provides as follows:

45(1) In this section

‘agent of a participant’ means a person who by a document admitted in evidence under this section appears to be or is otherwise proven to be an officer, agent, servant, employee or representative of a participant; ‘document’ includes any document appearing to be a carbon, photographic or other copy of a document; ‘participant’ means any person against whom proceedings have been instituted under this Act and in the case of a prosecution means any accused and any person who, although not accused, is alleged in the charge or indictment to have been a co-conspirator or otherwise party or privy to the offence charged.

(2) In any proceedings before the Commission or in any prosecution or proceedings before a court under or pursuant to this Act,

(a) anything done, said or agreed upon by an agent of a participant shall prima facie be deemed to have been done, said or agreed upon, as the case may be, with the authority of that participant;

(b) a document written or received by an agent of a participant shall prima facie be deemed to have been written or received, as the case may be, with the authority of that participant; and

(c) a document proved to have been in the possession of a participant or on premises used or occupied by a participant or in the possession of an agent of a participant shall be admitted in evidence without further proof thereof and is prima facie proof.

(i) that the participant had knowledge of the document and itS contents,

(ii) that anything recorded in or by the document as having been done, said or agreed upon by any participant or by an agent of a participant was done, said or agreed upon as recorded and, where anything is recorded in or by the document as having been done, said or agreed upon by an agent of a participant, that it was done, said or agreed upon with the authority of that participant.

(iii) that the document, where it appears to have been written by any participant or by an agent of a participant, was so written and, where it appears to have been written by an agent of a participant, that it was written with the authority of that participant.

[157]       The applicants argue that this provision, in authorizing the blanket introduction of documentary evidence, without such evidence being proven as a fact, is a violation of s. 7 that is not in accordance with the principles of fundamental justice. They also argue that the section raises a prima facie presumption with regard to documentary evidence that is inconsistent with the presumption of innocence guaranteed by s. 11(d).

[158]       The respondents raise a preliminary objection that s. 45 only applies “in proceedings before the Commission or in prosecution or proceeding before a Court under or pursuant to this Act”. Since the hearings before Mr. Couture do not fall within this category, s. 45 cannot be invoked at the present stage of the inquiry. The respondents contend that until the section is invoked, the applicants have no status to challenge its validity.

[159]       On the face of it, the respondent’s objection has some merit. Section 24(1) contains a statutory standing provision. An application under s. 24(1) may be brought by “anyone whose rights and freedoms, as guaranteed by this Charter, have been infringed or denied” (emphasis added). This would seem to rule out an application in respect of an impending violation. However, for the several reasons that follow, it is my view that this section should not be construed so narrowly.

[160]       First, this is not an ordinary statute; it is a constitutional document which “guarantees the rights and freedoms set out in it”.

[161]       The famous statement made by Lord Sankey, L.C., in Edwards v. Attorney General for Canada, [1930] A. C. 124 (P.C.), in reference to the British North America Act are equally applicable to the Constitution Act, 1982, and the Charter of Rights contained therein. His Lordship stated at page 136:

The British North America Act planted in Canada a living tree capable of growth and expansion within its natural limits. The object of the Act was to grant a Constitution to Canada. . . .

Their Lordships do not conceive it to be the duty of this Board - it is certainly not their desire - to cut down the provisions of the Act by a narrow and technical construction, but rather to give it a large and liberal interpretation. . . .

[162]       I would also refer to the following statement made by Tallis, J.A., of the Saskatchewan Court of Appeal in R. V. Therens, supra, at page 221, which is to the same effect:

Our nation’s constitutional ideals have been enshrined in the Charter and it will not be a ‘living’ charter unless it is interpreted in a meaningful way from the standpoint of an average citizen who seldom has a brush with the law, . . . The implementation and application of the Charter should not be blunted or thwarted by technical or legalistic interpretations of ordinary words of the English language. Using this approach, our citizens will not be placed in a position of feeling that the statements in the Charter are only rights in theory. If these rights are to survive and be available on a day-to-day basis we must resist the temptation to opt in favour of a restrictive approach.

[163]       If s. 24(1) is interpreted so that persons will be denied access to the Courts until their rights have actually been violated, persons will be left without an effective means of enforcing their rights, and the rights will be rights only in theory.

[164]       Secondly, a literal reading of s. 24(1) would mean that a person must establish a violation of his rights before bringing an application for a remedy. Of course, this is not how the section is being applied. In practice, a person must establish a violation of his rights as a necessary step in his application for a remedy. In other words, the establishment of a violation of rights is pre-requisite to the obtaining of a remedy. It is not a pre-requisite to the commencing of a s. 24(1) application. There should be no doubt that in order to bring a s. 24(1) application it is necessary only that the applicant allege that his rights have been infringed or denied.

[165]       Thirdly, s. 24(1) refers to rights being “infringed or denied”. If the term “infringed” is to add anything to the term “denied” it must contemplate a situation short of an actual denial of rights. In my view, the term “infringed” should be given an independent meaning; the term is broad enough to encompass present threats to a person’s rights or freedoms .

[166]       It is not difficult to imagine situations in which a present threat of a violation of a person’s rights may lead to consequences that are as detrimental as an actual violation. A useful example of such a situation is posited by Professor Gibson in Tarnopolsky and Beaudoin, Ed., The Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms, Toronto: Carswell, 1982, at 498-99. Professor Gibson states that where the law empowers authorities to infringe rights or freedoms in some arbitrary fashion, such as randomly searching homes in the hope of finding evidence of illegal activity, the threat of an impending violation has immediate restrictive consequences. He points out that the threat of such illegal searches may cause persons to take immediate protective steps, such as the removal or destruction of innocent but suspicious or embarrassing material. Thus, although the person’s rights have not actually been violated, the threat of an impending violation constitutes an interference with the person’s enjoyment of his rights. While such a situation may not constitute a “denial” of a person’s rights, it can properly be termed an “infringement”.

[167]       I would conclude that if s.

24(1) is given a broad and liberal interpretation, a person will have standing to bring an application for an alleged impending violation of his rights, and such an interpretation does not do violence to the words actually used.

[168]       This conclusion, however, does not mean that all persons alleging an impending violation will be entitled to a remedy. Section 24(1) leaves the matter of remedies in the discretion of the court. The imminence of the impending violation of the applicant’s rights will likely be a factor in deciding whether to exercise this discretion. (In this regard see: Spry, Equitable Remedies (2d Ed.), London: Sweet & Maxwell, 1980 at p. 366-69.)

[169]       I would also note that s. 24 is a remedial section. This fact in itself imposes some restrictions on who may make application under the section. A remedy is a means by which a violation, of a right is redressed or prevented. A declaration that a statute or portion thereof is invalid, will in certain cases be a remedy. In situations where a person is convicted of an offence by operation of a statute, and that statute is subsequently declared invalid, the declaration will result in an acquittal, and is therefore a means of redress. In other cases where there is a threat that a person will become subject to a statutory provision that constitutes a violation of the person’s rights, a declaration of invalidity will be a remedy in the sense that it will prevent an impending violation of the person’s rights. Thus, in both of these situations a declaration of invalidity pursuant to s. 52 of the Constitution Act, 1982, is a remedy available under s. 24(1).

[170]       If a person is unable to show that a right personal to him has been violated or threatened, or he is unable to show that the threat of a violation is immediate enough to justify the court exercising its discretion in favour of granting a remedy, it may still be open to that person to challenge the validity of legislation by means of an action pursuant to s. 52 of the Constitution Act, 1982. In such a case the common law rules of standing as expounded by the Supreme Court of Canada in Minister of Justice of Canada et al. v. Boroweki (1982), 39 N.R. 331; 64 C.C.C.(2d) 97, would presumably apply.

[171]       Applying the foregoing analysis to the applicants’ motion for a declaration that s. 45 of the Act is invalid, I would note, that although the provisions of s. 45 may not be invoked at the present stage, there is a present threat that the applicants will become subject to the provisions of that section. Thus, the applicants are alleging an impending infringement of their rights sufficient to give them standing to apply under s. 24(1) for the declaration sought.

[172]       I now turn to the merits of the applicants’ position with respect to s. 45 of the Act.

[173]       As I have indicated, the applicants allege that s. 45 of the Act is inconsistent with their rights under s. 7 and s. 11(d) of the Charter. While s. 45 can apply to prosecutions under the Act - that is to situations in which a person is charged - it also can apply to proceedings before the Commission in which no formal charges have been layed. In light of my earlier conclusions with respect to the proper relationship between s. 7 and the other legal rights contained in ss. 8 to 14 of the Charter, it should be obvious that I would not preclude an argument being raised under s. 7 in situations in which the person has not been charged. However, on this branch of the applicants’ challenge to the Act, I believe that I can deal with the arguments raised under s. 7 rather briefly.

[174]       The applicants’ position with respect to s. 7 seems to be based on an assumption that any legislation that abrogates the common law principles of evidence is not in accordance with the principles of fundamental justice. They argue that at common law the mere production of documentary evidence was not sufficient to make the documents admissible. In the case of private documents, it was required that the court be satisfied that the document was duly executed or that the origin and authorship the document was established before the document could be introduced as evidence: see, Sopinka and Lederman, The Law of Evidence In Civil Cases, Toronto: Butterworths, 1974, at page 429.

[175]       The applicants cite R. v. Anthes Business Forms Ltd. et al. (1976), 10 O.R.(2d) 153, as authority for the proposition that s. 45 of the Act has abrogated these common law principles and rules of evidence. However, authority for that proposition is hardly needed. It is clear that s. 45 has changed the common law rules of evidence, but that fact alone does not mean that it is inconsistent with the applicants’ right to life, liberty and security of the person. The applicants do not allege any specific interest, other than their right to be presumed innocent, which is interfered with. For my part I find it difficult to conceive what other fundamental right is abrogated by this change in evidentiary rules. In this regard I wish to adopt a statement made by Cartright, J., in Howard Smith Paper Mills Limited et al. v. The Queen, [1957] S.C.R. 403 at 420:

While s. 41 (now s. 45) [of the Act] makes a revolutionary change in the law of evidence, it creates no offence, it takes away no defence, it does not render criminal any course of conduct which was not already so declared before its enactment, it does not alter the character or legal effect of any transaction already entered into; it deals with a matter of evidence only. . . .

[176]       With respect to the applicants’ submission that s. 45 constitutes a violation of the presumption of innocence, I consider it unnecessary to deal with s. 7. Presumably s. 7 would only be resorted to where a person has not been formally charged, that is at an investigatory stage. If the person alleging the infringement of his right to be presumed innocent has been charged, his interest will be squarely within the protection of s. 11(d).

[177]       I propose to examine whether s. 45 is inconsistent with a person’s rights under s. 11(d). If s. 45 is not inconsistent with the presumption of innocence in a situation in which a person has been charged, then it is difficult to see how it could be offensive in situations where a person has not been charged.

[178]       Section 11(d) has received extensive judicial consideration at appellate court level. At least four appellate courts have dealt with s. 11(d): see, R. v. Oakes (1983), 2 C.C.C.(3d) 339 (Ont. Ct. A.); R. V. Carroll (1983), 40 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 147; 115 A.P.R. 147 (P.E.I.C.A.); R. V. Cook (1983), 56 N.S.R. (2d) 449; 117 A.P.R. 449 (C.A.); R. v. Stanger; R. v. Bramwell (1983), 46 A.R. 241 (Alta. C.A.). All of these cases involves successful challenges to s. 8 of the Narcotics Control Act, R.S.C. 1970, c. N-1.

[179]       The leading case in this area is R. v. Oakes, supra. The Oakes case establishes that a statutory reverse onus clause which places the burden of disproving an essential element of a crime on the accused will be held to be inconsistent with s. 11(d) of the Charter, unless there is a rational nexus between the proven fact that gives rise to the statutory presumption and the presumed fact.

[180]       A more recent judgment of the Ontario Court of Appeal, Re Boyle and The Queen (1983), 41 O.R.(2d) 713, expands somewhat on the principles enunciated in Oakes, and is more useful for present purposes.

[181]       In Boyle, Mr. Justice Martin provides an excellent analysis of the issues that arise under s. 11(d). His Lordship points out that in addition to reverse onus clauses, such as the one considered in Oakes, which required the accused to negative an element of the offence on a balance of probability, there are certain statutory presumptions which may also be inconsistent with s. 11(d).

[182]       He divides these statutory presumptions into two groups: permissive presumptions and mandatory presumptions. The latter type is also referred to as rebuttable presumptions of law.

[183]       Martin, J., refers to a statement in Cross, Evidence London: Butterworths (5th Ed. 1979), at page 723. The essential part of this reference is as follows:

A rebuttable presumption of law is a conclusion that a fact (‘the presumed fact’) exists which must be drawn, in the absence of further evidence, if another fact (‘the basic fact’) is proved ‘or admitted.

[184]       A permissive presumption is an inference that may, not must, be drawn when a basic fact is proved.

[185]       Mr. Justice Martin then deals specifically with the term “prima facie evidence” that is used in some statutes. He indicates that this term is capable of contemplating either a permissive or a mandatory inference. At page 727 he states as follows:

Wigmore points out that the term ‘prima facie evidence’ is used in two senses. It is sometimes used as equivalent to the notion of a presumption, even in the strict sense of a ruling of the judge putting upon the opponent the duty of producing evidence. When the term prima facie is so used, it is applied to the stage of the proceedings where the party having the legal burden of proving the issue or the burden of persuasion, has not only removed by producing sufficient evidence the duty of producing evidence to get past the judge to the jury, but has gone further and, either by means of a presumption or by a general mass of strong evidence, has entitled himself to a ruling that the opponent should fail if he does nothing more in the way of producing evidence. The second and more usual sense in which the term ‘prima facie evidence’ is used is where the proponent having the first duty of producing some evidence in order to pass the judge to the jury, has fulfilled that duty, satisfied the Judge, and may properly claim the jury be allowed to consider his case: see Wigmore on Evidence, 3rd ed. (1940), volume IX, pps. 293-94.

[186]       Mr. Justice Martin finally defines a permissive presumption in these terms at page 730:

If the Crown establishes a prima facie case, in the sense that it has produced evidence which requires the case to be submitted to the Jury, the accused risks conviction if he fails to satisfy the evidential burden of adducing evidence by way of explanation or denial, but he will not necessarily be convicted. The Jury may not be satisfied beyond a reasonable doubt of the accused’s guilt, even though there is evidence upon which they could be so satisfied. Discharge of the evidential burden by the accused is not a prerequisite to an acquittal: see Phipson on Evidence, 13th ed. (1982), at pp. 48-49.

And at page 732 His Lordship defines a mandatory presumption as follows:

. .. [Upon] proof of the basic facts giving rise to the presumption, the accused is not required to disprove an essential ingredient of the offence on a balance of probabilities, it is sufficient for him to adduce evidence which raises a reasonable doubt . . . . However, when the presumption arises, if he fails to . adduce evidence (through the Crown witnesses or defence witnesses) which raises a reasonable doubt with respect to those elements, conviction will ensue.

[187]       After establishing these two types of statutory presumptions, his Lordship held that a mandatory presumption should be treated the same as a reverse onus provision for the purposes of s. 11(d). His conclusion in this regard is stated as follows at page 733:

A mandatory presumption and a ‘reverse onus’ provision with respect to an essential element of an offence have in common that when the presumption arises and it is not displaced, the trier of fact is required to find that the presumed fact has been proved. The essential difference between the two is that in the case of a mandatory presumption (rebuttable presumption of law) the presumption is displaced by evidence which is not rejected and which raises a reasonable doubt as to the existence of the presumed fact. In the case of a ‘reverse onus’ provision, the presumption is only displaced by proof to the contrary on a balance of probability.

In my view, it is their common feature, that is, the mandatory nature of the conclusion required to be drawn when they arise, which requires that they be treated alike for the purpose of determining their constitutional validity.

[188]       Mr. Justice Martin concludes that reverse onus clauses and mandatory presumptions will be held inconsistent with s. 11(d) of the Charter, unless they satisfy the rational nexus test expounded in Oakee. It is implicit in his reasons that a permissive presumption does not offend s. 11(d).

[189]       I am of the opinion that the prima facie presumptions arising under s. 45 of the Act are of the permissive type.

[190]       I am reinforced in this conclusion by the Supreme Court of Canada decision in Sunbeam Corporation (Canada) Ltd. v. The Queen, [1969] 2 C.C.C. 189, reversing a judgment of the Appeal Court of Ontario, [1967] 3 CC.C. 149.

[191]       In the Supreme Court of Canada, Ritchie, J., held at page 195 as follows:

Section 41(2)(c) [now s. 45] simply provides that documents, such as these letters, which were in the possession of the accused ‘shall be admitted in evidence without further proof thereof and shall be prima facie evidence’ that the accused had knowledge of the documents and their contents and that anything recorded in them as having been done, said or agreed upon by the accused or its agent, was done, said or agreed upon. This does not mean that the trial Judge, having accepted the letters as prima facie evidence of their contents, is precluded from assessing the weight to be attached to that evidence in considering the issue of the accused’s guilt or innocence.

[192]       Since the presumptions arising by virtue of s. 45 of the Act are permissive presumptions, I hold that they are not inconsistent with the Charter.

[193]       The result is that the applicants are entitled to a declaration that s. 17 of the Combines Investigation Act is inconsistent with s. 7 of the Charter and is therefore of no force or effect. The other relief sought by the applicants is denied for the reasons given.
