Manitoba Court of Appeal

Citation: Manitoba v. Manitoba Human Rights Commission

Date: 1983-11-08

Monnin C.J.M., Matas and Philp JJ.A.

Counsel:

R. B. Doyle, for appellants.

R. S. Perozzo, for respondent.

The judgment of the court was delivered by

[1]                      Philp J.A.:—John R. Galbraith and Samuel Lylyk are former employees of the Government of Manitoba. Both were compulsorily retired on reaching the age of 65 years. They have complained under the Human Rights Act, 1974 (Man.), c. 65 (C.C.S.M., H175) (the Act), of age discrimination.

[2]                      In these proceedings the government seeks a declaration that their complaints were not continuing contraventions of the Act and were filed out of time. That was the finding of the learned trial judge, Mr. Justice Hewak.

[3]                      Galbraith and Lylyk have appealed. They argue that the alleged acts of discrimination were continuing contraventions and were not filed out of time. Alternatively, they argue that the time limitation in the Act is directory rather than mandatory, and that, in their circumstances, there was substantial compliance with the time provision. These are the issues on this appeal.

[4]                      I have concluded that the appeal must be dismissed.

[5]                      Section 54 of the Civil Service Superannuation Act, R.S.M. 1970, c. C120, says:

54. Every employee shall be retired from the civil service on the last day of the month in which he reaches the age of sixty-five years.

[6]                      Galbraith was 65 years of age in May, 1981. He requested an extension of his employment to December 31, 1981. The request was denied and Galbraith was retired by the government on May 31, 1981.

[7]                      Lylyk was 65 years of age in February, 1981. He also requested an extension of his employment to December 31, 1981. His request was denied and Lylyk was retired by the government on February 28, 1981.

[8]                      On January 26, 1982, this court in Re Newport and Government of Manitoba (1982), 131 D L.R. (3d) 564, [1982] 2 W.W.R. 254, 13 Man. R. (2d) 292, decided that s. 6(1) of the Act prevailed over s. 54 of the Civil Service Superannuation Act. Section 6(1) of the Act says in part:

6(1) Every person has the right of equality of opportunity based upon bona fide qualifications in respect of his occupation or employment or in respect of training for employment or in respect of an intended occupation, employment, advancement or promotion, and in respect of his membership or intended membership in a trade union, employers' organization or occupational association; and, without limiting the generality of the foregoing

(a) no employer or person acting on behalf of an employer, shall refuse to employ, or to continue to employ or to train the person for employment or to advance or promote that person, or discriminate against that person in respect of employment or any term or condition of employment;

• • • • •

because of race, nationality, religion, colour, sex, age, marital status, physical or mental handicap, ethnic or national origin, or political beliefs or family status of that person.

[9]                      Following the Newport decision, by letter of January 31, 1982, Galbraith requested reinstatement to his former or an equivalent position. He was advised on July 5, 1982, that he would not be reinstated but that he could apply for future positions. On September 2, 1982, Galbraith filed a complaint with the Human Rights Commission alleging discrimination on the basis of age.

[10]                  Lylyk was reinstated to his former position on May 25, 1982, with full benefits except that the government refused to pay his wages for the period between February 28, 1981 and May 25, 1982. Lylyk filed a complaint with the commission on September 2, 1982, alleging discrimination on the basis of age.

[11]                  On March 10, 1983, the commission requested the Attorney-General of Manitoba to appoint a board of adjudication to hear and decide the Galbraith and Lylyk complaints. Section 21 of the Act provides for such an appointment. The Attorney-General has declined to make the appointment on the grounds that the complaints were not continuing contraventions of the Act and were filed out of time. Section 19(1) of the Act fixes the time for filing complaints. That section says:

19(1) Any person who has reasonable grounds for believing that any person has contravened a provision of this act, may file with the Commission a complaint in the form prescribed by the Commission, such complaint to be filed with the Commission not later than 6 months after the date of the alleged contravention or, where a continuing contravention is alleged, after the date of the last alleged contravention of this Act.

[12]                  Before considering the notion of a continuing contravention let me dispose of a related question. Galbraith and Lylyk say that there was no evidence before the court as to when the government discontinued its practice of compulsory age retirement. They argue that the government's refusal to continue the employment of employees because of age is a continuing contravention of the Act. In the absence of evidence as to when that practice ceased, Galbraith and Lylyk say that the government cannot assert that their complaints were filed out of time.

[13]                  I reject that argument.

[14]                  The individual complaints of Galbraith and of Lylyk were filed with the commission and are before the court. In their respective complaints, Galbraith and Lylyk assert their separate and distinct positions and claims. It is the time of the alleged acts of discrimination in relation to the filing of the complaints that must be considered. The government's employment practices are not on trial. The government may have contravened provisions of the Act in its dealings with other employees. However, that, in my view, is irrelevant to the issue of whether or not the complaints of Galbraith and Lylyk were filed in time.

[15]                  Were the contraventions of the Act alleged by Galbraith and Lylyk in their respective complaints "continuing contraventions?" The courts of Manitoba have not dealt squarely with the notion of a continuing contravention. In Re Manitoba Human Rights Com'n et al. and City of Winnipeg et al. (1982), 135 D.L.R. 641 at p. 643, 16 Man. R. (2d) 94 at p. 97, [1982] 4 W.W.R. 715, Hamilton J. commented:

It should be noted in any event that s. 19(1) also contemplates a continuing contravention of the Act and a complaint may be filed during that continuance. In the case at bar the alleged breach is continuing in that the employee is still being denied employment due to his age.

That comment, however, was obiter and while Re Manitoba Human Rights Com'n was upheld on appeal, 144 D.L.R. (3d) 353, [1983] 3 W.W.R. 117, 20 Man. R. (2d) 200, this court did not deal with the issue.

[16]                  In his reasons [150 D.L.R. (3d) 524, [1983] 6 W.W.R. 18], Hewak J. has trenchantly reviewed the Canadian and American authorities, which have considered the analogous concepts in labour relations of "continuing violations", "continuing grievances", and "continuous discrimination": see Re Burns and United Ass'n of Journeymen of Plumbing & Pipefitting Industry, Local 170 et al. (1977), 82 D.L.R. (3d) 488, [1978] 2 W.W.R. 22, 78 C.L.L.C. para. 14,134 (B.C.S.C.); Re United Glass & Ceramic Workers of North America (AFL-CIO-CLC), Local 246 and Dominion Glass Co. Ltd. et al. (1973), 40 D.L.R. (3d) 496, 1 O.R. (2d) 408, 5 L.A.C. (2d) 224n (Ont. C.A.); Hiscott v. General Electric Co. (1975), 521 F. 2d 632; Woodburn v. LTV Aerospace Corp. (1976), 531 F. 2d. 750; Charlier et al. v. S.C. Johnson & Son, Inc. (1977), 556 F. 2d 761; Goldman v. Sears, Roebuck & Co. (1979), 607 F. 2d 1014; Wright v. State of Tennessee et al. (1980), 628 F. 2d 949; Re Parking Authority of Toronto and C.U.P.E., Local 43 (1974), 5 L.A.C. (2d) 150.

[17]                  As well, the notions of a "continuing offence" and of a "continuing cause of action" are well known to the law. Freedman J.A. (as he then was) commented on the continuing offence in Dressier v. Tollman Gravel & Sand Supply Ltd. (1962), 36 D.L.R. (2d) 398 at p. 408, [1963] 2 C.C.C. 25, 40 W.W.R. 449 at pp. 459-60:

After all, it is not breaking new ground to say that one charge may be laid for a continuing offence which is made up of a succession of separate offences of the same character, where in their entirety they may be said to make up a single, albeit continuing, transaction.

In Hole v. Chard Union, [1894] 1 Ch. 293, the words "continuing cause of action" in the English rule (now O. 37, r. 6) respecting the assessment of damages were considered. Lindley L.J. said at pp. 295-6:

What is a continuing cause of action? Speaking accurately, there is no such thing; but what is called a continuing cause of action is a cause of action which arises from the repetition of acts or omissions of the same kind as that for which the action was brought. In my opinion, that is a continuing cause of action within the meaning of the rule.

[18]                  What emerges from all of the decisions is that a continuing violation (or a continuing grievance, discrimination, offence or cause of action) is one that arises from a succession (or repetition) of separate violations (or separate acts, omissions, discriminations, offences or actions) of the same character (or of the same kind). That reasoning, in my view, should apply to the notion of the "continuing contravention" under the Act. To be a "continuing contravention", there must be a succession or repetition of separate acts of discrimination of the same character. There must be present acts of discrimination which could be considered as separate contraventions of the Act, and not merely one act of discrimination which may have continuing effects or consequences.

[19]                  Galbraith and Lylyk were compulsorily retired pursuant to s. 54 of the Civil Service Superannuation Act when they reached the age of 65 years. Galbraith's complaint says: "I was terminated from my position of employment on May 31, 1981, in that I had reached the age of sixty-five (65) years." Lylyk's complaint is similarly worded: "I was terminated (retired) from my position of employment on February 28, 1981, because I had reached my 65th birthday." In each case, it is the termination of employment or the compulsory retirement that is complained of. Those are the acts of alleged discrimination which are the subject-matter of the complaints. In each case, employment was terminated by a single act of compulsory retirement. The specific act occurred on May 31, 1981, in the case of Galbraith, and on February 28, 1981, in the case of Lylyk. While the effects or consequences of the termination of employment were ongoing (and in the case of Galbraith are still ongoing) they flowed in each case from a single act of alleged age discrimination.

[20]                  I have concluded that, in respect of the complaints filed by Galbraith and Lylyk, there have not been continuing contraventions pursuant to s. 19(1) of the Act.

[21]                  As did Hewak J., I differ from the obiter view expressed by Hamilton J. in Re Manitoba Human Rights Com'n et al. and City of Winnipeg et al., quoted above. In my view, his comments were in error.

[22]                  I turn to the final argument of Galbraith and Lylyk, that the time limitation prescribed in s. 19(1) is directory rather than mandatory. On the time for filing a complaint the section says: "such complaint to be filed with the Commission not later than 6 months after the date of the alleged contravention ...".

[23]                  Galbraith's complaint was filed some 15 months after his compulsory retirement. In Lylyk's case, the complaint was filed some 18 months after his compulsory retirement. In both cases, the complaints were filed over seven months after the decision of this court in Newport.

[24]                  There is no general rule for determining whether statutory procedural provisions are mandatory or directory. However, in Vialoux v. Registered Psychiatric Nurses Ass'n of Manitoba (unreported, October 7, 1983 [now reported at p. 187, ante]), this court commented upon the fact that time provisions appear to be an exception.

[25]                  Maxwell on Interpretation of Statutes, 12th ed. (1969), at p. 320, says:

Enactments regulating the procedure in courts are usually construed as imperative, even where the observance of the formalities in question is not a condition exacted from the party seeking the benefit of the statute, but a duty imposed on a court or public officer when no general inconvenience or injustice seems to call for a different construction.

[26]                  Galbraith and Lylyk argue that the courts have, on occasion, considered time requirements to be directory rather than mandatory. They cite R. v. Metropolitan Toronto Board of Com'rs of Police, Ex p. Prosser (1971), 20 D.L.R. (3d) 403, [1971] 3 O.R. 353 (Ont. C.A.); and Re Int'l Beverage Dispensers' & Bartenders' Union Local 280 and Man of Aran Ltd. (1973), 41 D.L.R. (3d) 699, 2 O.R. (2d) 54 (Ont. Div. Ct.). Those decisions are, however, dependent upon their own very particular facts and lack any general precedential authority. Indeed, in Man of Aran Ltd., at p. 700 D.L.R., p. 55 O.R., Wright J. says: "We think that the point with regard to the 60 days is, in the particular circumstances of this case, a technical irregularity ...". (My emphasis.)

[27]                  Galbraith and Lylyk cite also the comments of Dewar C.J.Q.B. in Re City of Winnipeg and Torchinsky (1981), 129 D.L.R. (3d) 170, 13 Man. R. (2d) 369, in which he found a ten-day time period in the City of Winnipeg Act to be directory only. There, the statute required an assessment complaint to be left with the secretary of the Board of Revision "at least ten days before the first day of the sitting of the Board". The notice of assessment which is required to be mailed or delivered by the assessor to the person assessed was not received until the date of the first sitting of the Board of Revision. It was therefore impossible for the person assessed to comply with the time requirement on filing an assessment complaint.

[28]                  That kind of situation, in my view, properly calls into play the "different construction" referred to in the comment from Maxwell, quoted above.

[29]                  I conclude that the time requirements in s. 19(1)of the Act are mandatory and that the complaints filed with the Human Rights Commission by Galbraith and by Lylyk were filed out of time.

[30]                  The appeal is dismissed and the respondent shall have its costs.

[31]                  Appeal dismissed.
