Introduction

[1]                   This is an appeal by Alberts Construction Ltd. (“Alberts”) from a March 15, 2010, decision of Nicholas J. Stooshinoff (the “Claims Officer”), wherein the Claims Officer dismissed the claim of Alberts against Big Sky Farms Inc. (“Big Sky”).

Background

[2]                   Alberts is a Saskatchewan construction company. Its principals are Mervin Alberts, as president, and Merlin Alberts as director. For several years, Alberts built pig barns and related facilities for Big Sky at various locations throughout Saskatchewan.

[3]                   Big Sky is a producer, feeder and marketer of hogs from multiple locations throughout Saskatchewan and Manitoba. In November 2009, Big Sky applied for court protection under the Companies’ Creditors Arrangement Act (Canada), R.S.C. 1985, c. C‑36 (“CCAA”), in order to restructure its business and financial affairs. Drycast Systems Inc. (“Drycast”) is a wholly owned subsidiary of Big Sky. Big Sky Management Consulting Corp. (“BSMCC”) is an inactive company which was incorporated to facilitate management of third party hog production operations. Both Drycast and BSMCC are guarantors of the principal secured obligations owed by Big Sky to its senior secured lenders and, as a result, were parties to the CCAA application by Big Sky.

[4]                   In December 2009, Big Sky presented a plan of compromise and arrangement (the “Plan”) under the CCAA to its creditors and the Court. As part of the plan, a claims process (the “Claims Process”) was proposed. The Claims Process was approved by the Court pursuant to an order dated December 9, 2009 (the “Claims Process Order”). As part of the Claims Process, creditors of Big Sky submit a claim to a monitor (the “Monitor”), who could either accept or disallow the claim. If the claim was accepted by the Monitor, the claimant would be paid a portion of its claim as set out in the Plan. If the claim was not accepted by the Monitor and the claimant disagreed with this disposition, the claim would be referred to the Claims Officer, who would then determine the validity of the disputed claim. The Claims Process also provided for an appeal to the Court from the Claims Officer’s determination.

[5]                   During the course of the Claims Process, a claim (the “Claim”) was submitted to the Monitor by Alberts alleging that Big Sky was indebted to Alberts in the amount of $2,373,790.60 for work done for Big Sky relating to the construction of facilities and roadways at three separate sites, being the Pelly breeder barn (“Pelly One”), the Pelly nursery barn (“Pelly Two”) and the Porcupine Plain facility (“Porcupine”). The Claim by Alberts was disallowed by the Monitor on the basis that the records of Big Sky indicated that Alberts had been paid in full for the work and, alternatively, that the Claim would be statute barred by the expiration of the limitation period.

[6]                   Alberts filed a dispute note from the denial of the Claim by the Monitor and sought to have the matter determined by the Claims Officer. A summary trial was held before the Claims Officer on February 12, 2010. Affidavit evidence was submitted by both Big Sky and Alberts. Counsel were allowed to cross‑examine the opposing affiants at the hearing. In a decision dated March 15, 2010, the Claims Officer held that Alberts had failed to prove the Claim on a balance of probabilities and, further, that even if the allegations had been proven, the Claim would be statute barred except for the sum of $68,899.66.

Issues

[7]                   Although the notice of motion filed by Alberts in this appeal lists 20 grounds of appeal, the parties agree that this list could be reduced to four main issues to be determined by the Court:

1.         Did the Claims Officer err in finding that Alberts had failed to prove the Claim on a balance of probabilities?

2.         Did the Claims Officer err in finding that Exhibits “E” and “F” to the affidavit of Casey Smit were contracts by which the parties intended to govern their relationship?

3.         Did the Claims Officer err in finding that Alberts had sufficient information to prepare its invoices in a timely fashion?

4.         Did the Claims Officer err in determining that the majority of the Claim was barred by the expiry of the limitation period?

Further issues to be addressed were the Court’s jurisdiction and the standard of review that the Court should follow.

Analysis

1.         Jurisdiction and standard of review.

[8]       The parties agree that Alberts had a right to appeal the Claims Officer’s decision by virtue of paragraph 12(c) of the Claims Process Order and paragraph 24 of the Claims Process set out in Schedule “A” to the Claims Process Order. The parties also agree that the standard of review to be followed by this court is that of an appellate court. The standard of review by an appellant court was outlined by the Supreme Court of Canada in the case of Housen v. Nikolaisen, [2002] 2 S.C.R. 235, 2002 SCC 33. In that case, the Court held that deference should be given to a decision of a trial judge with respect to factual findings unless those factual findings constitute a palpable and overriding error. The Court also held that the standard of review on a pure question of law is one of correctness, where the appellate court was free to replace the opinion of the trial judge with its own.

[9]       This standard of appellate review has also been applied by courts in Ontario when hearing appeals from claims officers’ decisions in CCAA proceedings. See Re 1587930 Ontario Inc. (2006), 25 C.B.R. (5th) 264 (Ont. S.C.J.); and Triton Tubular Components Corp. v. Steelcase Inc. (2005), 14 C.B.R. (5th) 264 (Ont. S.C.J.).

[10]                 In this case, the Claims Officer’s decision was made after an adversarial proceeding in which each party tendered evidence by affidavit, which evidence was then subject to cross‑examination of the affiant. Following written submissions by the parties, the Claims Officer wrote a detailed decision outlining his findings of fact and conclusions of law. These findings and conclusions should therefore be subjected to the standard of review as set out above: correctness as to questions of law and deference as to findings of fact, except where such findings of fact would amount  to a palpable and overriding error.

2.         Did the Claims Officer err in finding that Alberts had failed to prove the Claim on a balance of probabilities?

[11]                 Alberts’ position is that the evidence submitted by Big Sky through the affidavit of Casey Smit (“Mr. Smit”), wherein he deposed that Big Sky had paid for all the work done by Alberts and that, accordingly, no money was owing, was based upon hearsay and should not have been accepted by the Claims Officer. Further, Alberts position is that the Claims Officer should have drawn a negative inference against Big Sky when it failed to call a witness, Dean Possberg (“Mr. Possberg”), who Alberts says would have given direct evidence as to the issues involved in the Claim. Alberts also submits that the Claims Officer made a palpable and overriding error when he determined that Alberts had not proven the Claim on a balance of probabilities.

[12]                 Counsel for Big Sky submitted that the Claims Officer had ample support in the evidence to make the findings and inferences of fact, which he did, and that no palpable or overriding error was made.

[13]                 The Claims Officer made the following findings in reference to the inability of Alberts to prove the Claim on a balance of probabilities:

17.   While it may be that Alberts did perform work and provided services to Big Sky for which it has not been paid, on the documentation and evidence produced by both sides I am unable to find on a balance of probabilities if that is the case and if so what that work was, when it was performed and when it was invoiced, and finally, if it was paid for in part or at all.

...

36.   Whether or not Alberts has been overpaid or is still owed money cannot be determined on the evidence before me. Big Sky submits that its account ledger indicates that Alberts has been paid in full for all work requested and approved. It indicates that the last payment to Alberts was July 16, 2007 (paragraph 24 of the affidavit of Casey Smit). On the evidence before me I can only conclude that Alberts has failed to prove on a balance of probabilities that it is owed a debt in the amounts alleged or at all.

 

[14]     In the case of F.H. v. McDougall, [2008] 3 S.C.R. 41, 2008 SCC 53, the Supreme Court of Canada referred to the standard of proof in a civil case. At paragraph 40, the Court stated:

40   Like the House of Lords, I think it is time to say, once and for all in Canada, that there is only one civil standard of proof at common law and that is proof on a balance of probabilities. Of course, context is all important and a judge should not be unmindful, where appropriate, of inherent probabilities or improbabilities or the seriousness of the allegations or consequences. However, these considerations do not change the standard of proof. ...

...

46   Similarly, evidence must always be sufficiently clear, convincing and cogent to satisfy the balance of probabilities test. But again, there is no objective standard to measure sufficiency. In serious cases, like the present, judges may be faced with evidence of events that are alleged to have occurred many years before, where there is little other evidence than that of the plaintiff and defendant. As difficult as the task may be, the judge must make a decision. If a responsible judge finds for the plaintiff, it must be accepted that the evidence was sufficiently clear, convincing and cogent to that judge that the plaintiff satisfied the balance of probabilities test.

 

[15]     The Claims Officer was therefore correct in his finding that Alberts had to prove the Claim on a balance of probabilities and that if the evidence before him did not meet this standard of proof, he must dismiss the Claim.

[16]                 In respect to the issue of hearsay, while the affidavit of Mr. Smit, the CEO of Big Sky, does refer to discussions he had had with Mr. Possberg, the Claims Officer’s decision was not based upon such evidence but, rather, the lack of evidence provided by Alberts in proof of the Claim. Big Sky’s position was that all monies owing to Alberts for work performed had been paid. This position was not based solely on the information provided by Mr. Possberg to Mr. Smit but also on the ledger which Big Sky kept regarding Alberts, which ledger was marked as Schedule “K” to the affidavit of Mr. Smit. This ledger showed a zero balance owing to Alberts. That ledger was the one referred to by the Claims Officer in paragraph 36 of his decision. Accordingly, it cannot be said that hearsay evidence led the Claims Officer to make a palpable and overriding error.

[17]                 In respect to Alberts’ position that an adverse inference should have been drawn by virtue of the failure by Big Sky to call Mr. Possberg, an adverse inference should be drawn only after a prima facie case has been established by the party bearing the burden of proof. Dwyer v. Mark II Innovations Ltd. (2006), 208 O.A.C. 305 (Ont. C.A.).

[18]                 The classic Saskatchewan case concerning situations in which an adverse inference may be drawn from a party’s failure to adduce relevant evidence is Murray v. Saskatoon, [1952] 2 D.L.R. 499 (Sask. C.A.). The Court in that case stated at paragraphs 20 and 21:

20   The subject is dealt with at length by the learned author in Wigmore on Evidence, 3rd ed., vol. II, pp. 162 et seq. On p. 162 it is stated in part:–“The failure to bring before the tribunal some circumstance, document, or witness, when either the party himself or his opponent claims that the facts would thereby be elucidated, serves to indicate, as the most natural inference, that the party fears to do so, and this fear is some evidence that the circumstance or document or witness, if brought, would have exposed facts unfavourable to the party. These inferences, to be sure, cannot fairly be made except upon certain conditions; and they are also open always to explanation by circumstances which make some other hypothesis a more natural one than the party’s fear of exposure. But the propriety of such an inference in general is not doubted.”

21   The party affected by the inference may, of course, explain it away by showing circumstances which prevent the production of the witness; but where the failure to produce the witness is not explained, the inference may be drawn that the unproduced evidence would be contrary to the party’s case or at least would not support it. In the pages in Wigmore on Evidence following the above quotation many authorities are referred to which indicates that in the Courts of the United States the rule is of wide application.

 

[19]     In the circumstances of this case, Big Sky provided evidence to explain why it did not call Mr. Possberg. At paragraph 13 of his affidavit, Mr. Smit deposes that Mr. Possberg is no longer employed by Big Sky. At Q. 567 of the cross‑examination of Mr. Smit, he confirms that Mr. Possberg stopped working for Big Sky in August 2009. As such, Big Sky has provided an explanation as to why Mr. Smit is the affiant, as he is the CEO of Big Sky. There is no property in a witness: Harmony Shopping Co. S.A. v. Davis, [1979] 3 All E.R. 177 at 180-81, and S.M. v. Canada (Attorney General), 2005 SKQB 351, 271 Sask. R. 123. If Alberts felt that the evidence of Mr. Possberg could establish its claim, Alberts has the ability to subpoena Mr. Possberg to obtain his evidence.

[20]     The finding by the Claims Officer in paragraphs 12 and 36 that Alberts has failed to prove on a balance of probabilities that it is owed a debt by Big Sky or the amount thereof are findings of fact for which deference should be given. I find that there is evidence to support the Claims Officer’s findings of fact in this regard. While there may have been evidence as to inconsistencies in Big Sky’s documentation as referred to by counsel for Alberts in this appeal that does not assist Alberts in proving its own claim. The finding of fact by the Claims Officer that Alberts did not prove its claim on a balance of probabilities cannot be said to amount to a palpable or overriding error.

3.         Did the Claims Officer err in finding that Exhibits “E” and “F” to the affidavit of Casey Smit were contracts which the parties intended to govern their relationship?

[21]                 Exhibits “E” and “F” to the affidavit of Casey Smit are documents “H” and “I” in the index of documents filed with the Court at the hearing of this appeal. Alberts submits that at paragraph 11 of his decision, the Claims Officer found that Exhibits “E” and “F” were contracts between the parties and that this finding of fact constituted a palpable and overriding error. Big Sky submits that the two documents reflect an understanding between the parties and that the Claims Officer properly made use of them in coming to the conclusions he did concerning the relationship. In any event, Big Sky submits that even if they were found by the Claims Officer to be contracts, such finding would not be a palpable and overriding error.

[22]                 In his decision, the Claims Officer recognized that Exhibits “E” and “F” were incomplete documents. His conclusion was that, although incomplete, they were contractual documents “intended by both parties to govern their relationship for the construction of Pelly Barn #1 and Pelly Barn #2”. There is evidence to support such a finding found in paragraphs 11 to 13 of Mr. Smit’s affidavit. The Claims Officer found that each side was equally at fault for failing to properly document their contractual relationship and, in my opinion, the evidence supports such a conclusion. The Claims Officer was entitled to use the said documents for the limited purpose that he did. Accordingly, I find the Claims Officer has not committed a palpable and overriding error in referring to these documents as contractual documents.

4.         Did the Claims Officer err in finding that Alberts had sufficient information to prepare its invoices in a timely fashion?

[23]                 Alberts’ position is that the Claims Officer made an error of fact in paragraph 30 of his decision when he made the following determination in the last sentence of that paragraph:

30.   ... I find that Alberts did in fact have sufficient information to prepare its invoices and at the very least could have entered into an arrangement for determining any short fall [sic] it may have claimed.

 

[24]     The Claims Officer concluded that the dirt quantity listing report dated December 7, 2006, and provided to Alberts on January 10, 2007, (document “E” in the index of documents) was sufficient to allow Alberts to submit its invoice at that time.

[25]     Once again, it is clear from his decision that the Claims Officer was aware of the position of Alberts that it could not submit an accurate invoice without additional information from Big Sky in the form of final dirt work quantities. At paragraph 30, the Claims Officer dealt with this submission and made a finding of fact that this position was not accepted for the reasons given. Deference must be given to these findings of fact. The Claims Officer’s findings of fact in this regard cannot be said to amount to a palpable or overriding error.

5.         Did the Claims Officer err in determining that the majority of the Alberts claim was barred by the expiry of the limitation period?

[26]     While the principle reason given by the Monitor for rejecting Alberts’ proofs of claim in respect to monies owing by Big Sky was that Alberts had already been paid for any goods and services provided to Big Sky, the Monitor also gave an alternative reason for disallowing the claim. In the Monitor’s notice of January 20, 2010, the Monitor stated:

... Furthermore, if any claim exists, it would be barred by the expiry of the limitation period for making such a claim.

 

[27]                 The Claims Officer dealt with this alternative reason and concluded that the Monitor had been correct in his decision that the vast majority of the claim was time barred. Although the Claims Officer found that invoices for work done in 2004 totalling $68,899.66 would not have been time barred when The Limitations Act, S.S. 2004, c. L‑16.1, was amended in 2005, the Claims Officer found Alberts had failed to establish on a balance of probabilities that the claim for 2004 work had been proven. In this appeal, counsel for Alberts raised three submissions concerning the findings by the Claims Officer that the majority of Alberts’ claims were time barred:

(a)       there is no limitation period in respect to CCAA claims;

(b)       the Alberts claim was not time barred because Alberts did not discover its claim until the spring of 2009; and

(c)       the Alberts claim was not time barred because Big Sky had concealed information from Alberts necessary to submit a claim.

Each of these submissions will be dealt with separately.

(a)       Does The Limitations Act apply to CCAA claims?

[28]                 Alberts’ position is that the CCAA does not provide for any limitation period within that Act. Further, counsel for Alberts submitted that Alberts’ claim is not subject to the limitation periods in The Limitations Act because Alberts’ claim is not commenced by statement of claim or originating notice as referred to in s. 3(1) of that Act. Counsel for Alberts submits that the application before the Claims Officer is in the nature of an appeal from the disallowance of Alberts’ claim by the Monitor and that s. 3(2)(a) of The Limitations Act clearly exempts appeals. Alberts refers to ss. 2 and 3 of The Limitations Act which provide:

Interpretation

2    In this Act:

(a)  “claim” means a claim to remedy an injury, loss or damage that occurred as a result of an act or omission;

(b)  “claimant” means a person who has a claim;

...

Application of Act

3(1)      Subject to subsections (2) to (5), this Act applies to claims pursued in court proceedings that:

(a)  are commenced by statement of claim; or

(b)  are commenced by originating notice and are not proceedings in the nature of an application.

(2)        This Act does not apply to court proceedings that are:

(a)  proceedings in the nature of an appeal;

...

 

[29]     It is the position of Big Sky that The Limitations Act does not specifically include or exclude CCAA proceedings. Counsel for Big Sky submits, however, that case law has established that a debt barred by a statute of limitations is not provable within the meaning of the CCAA and accordingly the Act applies to claims such as that brought by Alberts.

[30]     Whether The Limitations Act applies to a notice of claim filed pursuant to the CCAA is a matter of statutory interpretation. Accordingly, the standard of review is one of correctness.

[31]     When considering whether The Limitations Act applies to CCAA proceedings, the starting point is to consider the purpose of a statute of limitations. In the case of M.(K.) v. M.(H.), [1992] 3 S.C.R. 6, La Forest J. dealt with the purposes of limitation statutes. He stated at pages 29‑31:

... In order to determine the time of accrual of the cause of action in a manner consistent with the purposes of the Limitations Act, I believe it is helpful to first examine its underlying rationales. There are three, and they may be described as the certainty, evidentiary, and diligence rationales ....

Statutes of limitations have long been said to be statutes of repose; see Doe on the demise of Count Duroure v. Jones (1791), 4 T.R. 301, 100 E.R. 1031, and A’Court v. Cross (1825), 3 Bing. 329, 130 E.R. 540. The reasoning is straightforward enough. There comes a time, it is said, when a potential defendant should be secure in his reasonable expectation that he will not be held to account for ancient obligations. ...

The second rationale is evidentiary and concerns the desire to foreclose claims based on stale evidence. Once the limitation period has lapsed, the potential defendant should no longer be concerned about the preservation of evidence relevant to the claim; see Dundee Harbour Trustees v. Dougall (1852), 1 Macq. 317 (H.L.), and Deaville v. Boegeman (1984), 48 O.R. (2d) 725 (C.A.). ...

Finally, plaintiffs are expected to act diligently and not “sleep on their rights”; statutes of limitation are an incentive for plaintiffs to bring suit in a timely fashion. ...

 

[32]     The above noted rationales were referred to and adopted by the Saskatchewan Court of Appeal in the case of Josvanger v. Folk, 2005 SKCA 138, 275 Sask. R. 101.

[33]     Beginning at paragraph 45 of his decision, it is clear that the Claims Officer was aware of and considered the purposes of limitation periods as referred to above. The issue before me is whether he was correct in his determination that The Limitations Act applies in the circumstances of this case.

[34]     Although the CCAA does not have an express purpose clause, it is titled “an Act to facilitate compromises and arrangements between companies and their creditors”. This title can be taken to suggest that its purpose was to assist insolvent companies in achieving arrangements with creditors so that the company could find a way to continue operations and avoid bankruptcy. The connection between the CCAA and the Bankruptcy and Insolvency Act, R.S.C. 1985, c. B‑3 (“BIA”), as amended, can also be seen throughout the CCAA.

[35]                 Some sections of the CCAA make specific reference to the BIA. Section 2 of the CCAA defines the term “claim” and provides:

Definitions

2. (1) In this Act,

...

“claim” means any indebtedness, liability or obligation of any kind that would be a claim provable within the meaning of section 2 of the Bankruptcy and Insolvency Act;

...

 

[36]                 Section 2 of the BIA provides:

Definitions

2.   In this Act,

...

“claim provable in bankruptcy”, “provable claim” or “claim provable” includes any claim or liability provable in proceedings under this Act by a creditor;

...

 

[37]                 Section 121(1) of the BIA defines what is a “claim provable”. It provides:

Claims provable

121. (1)  All debts and liabilities, present or future, to which the bankrupt is subject on the day on which the bankrupt becomes bankrupt or to which the bankrupt may become subject before the bankrupt’s discharge by reason of any obligation incurred before the day on which the bankrupt becomes bankrupt shall be deemed to be claims provable in proceedings under this Act.

 

[38]                 Numerous cases have held that limitation periods apply to claims under the BIA. Most of these cases refer to the case of Reference re the Debt Adjustment Act, 1937 (Alb.), [1943] 2 D.L.R. 1 (P.C.), in which the Privy Council said at page 11:

... In England it has always been held that, subject to the statutory exception as to debts payable at some certain future time, the petitioning creditors’ debt and the debts provable must be debts recoverable by legal process. For example, a debt barred by the Statute of Limitations is not a debt on which a bankruptcy petition can he presented, nor is it one provable in bankruptcy.... The Dominion Act is very similar to the English Bankruptcy Acts so far as those matters are concerned, and there appears to be no reason for thinking that a similar principle would not be applied in Canada to the words “debt due.” ... [Emphasis added.]

 

[39]     In the case of Re Hamar (1921), 63 D.L.R. 241 (Sask. K.B.), MacDonald J. stated at page 241:

A debt barred by the statutes of limitation [is] not provable. Ex parte Dewdney; Ex parte Seaman (1808), 15 Ves. Jun. 479, 33 E.R. 836; Ex parte Roffey (1815), 2 Rose 245; 2 Hals. P. 202, para. 325.

 

[40]     Other cases have also held that a provable claim in bankruptcy proceedings must be one recoverable by legal process and therefore not one for which the limitation period has already run. Farm Credit Corp. v. Holowach (Trustee of) (1988), 51 D.L.R. (4th) 501 (Alta. C.A.); and Re Morton (1922), 66 D.L.R. 378 (Sask. K.B.).

[41]     As s. 2 of the CCAA requires that the claim be “provable” within the meaning of the BIA, following the above authorities, I am satisfied that the Claims Officer was correct in his determination that The Limitations Act would apply to claims brought under the CCAA. At paragraph 3 of his decision, the Claims Officer made a finding of fact that the invoices forming the Claim refer to work done prior to August 13, 2007, and that the limitation period had expired by August 13, 2009, prior to the CCAA application. My review of the invoices confirms this finding of fact and, accordingly, his conclusion that the Claim was time barred is not a palpable or overriding error.

(b)       Was the Alberts claim not time barred because Alberts did not discover its claim until the spring of 2009?

[42]                 Sections 5, 6 and 18 of The Limitations Act are relevant to this issue. They provide:

Basic limitation period

5       Unless otherwise provided in this Act, no proceedings shall be commenced with respect to a claim after two years from the day on which the claim is discovered.

Discovery of claim

6(1)   Unless otherwise provided in this Act and subject to subsection (2), a claim is discovered on the day on which the claimant first knew or in the circumstances ought to have known:

(a)     that the injury, loss or damage had occurred;

(b)     that the injury, loss or damage appeared to have been caused by or contributed to by an act or omission that is the subject of the claim;

(c)     that the act or omission that is the subject of the claim appeared to be that of the person against whom the claim is made; and

(d)     that, having regard to the nature of the injury, loss or damage, a proceeding would be an appropriate means to seek to remedy it.

(2)     A claimant is presumed to have known of the matters mentioned in clauses (1)(a) to (d) on the day on which the act or omission on which the claim is based took place, unless the contrary is proved.

...

Burden of proof

18      If, in a proceeding, a limitation period is raised against a claimant, the claimant has the burden of proving that:

(a)     the limitation period has not expired; or

(b)     there is no limitation period that applies to the claim.

 

[43]                 It is the position of Alberts that it did not discover its claim until the spring of 2009 when Mr. Possberg, who was at that time employed by Big Sky, advised Alberts that Big Sky would not pay them any more money for work done by Alberts at the Pelly  and Porcupine locations. Alberts also suggests that it did not receive the final dirt quantity numbers as prepared by the engineering technologists and submitted to Big Sky, and that, without these quantities, it has been unable to prepare and submit invoices to Big Sky. Alberts suggests that s. 6(1)(b), (c) and (d) are applicable in the circumstances of this case.

[44]                 The parties agree that s. 6 of The Limitations Act is merely a codification of the existing case law, which was set out by the Supreme Court of Canada in the cases of Kamloops (City) v. Nielsen, [1984] 2 S.C.R. 2, and Central Trustco v. Rafuse, [1986] 2 S.C.R. 147. In discussing this codification, in the case of Placzek v. Green, 2009 ONCA 83, 307 D.L.R. (4th) 441, the Ontario Court of Appeal stated at paragraph 52:

52   Whether under the former limitations regime or under the new Act, in the context of limitations law, “discovered” refers to discovering the material facts on which a cause of action or claim is based for the purpose of triggering the limitation period: see Kamloops (City of) v. Nielsen, [1984] 2 S.C.R. 2; Central Trust Co. v. Rafuse, [1986] 2 S.C.R. 147, at p. 224; and see s. 5 of the new Act.

 

[45]                 The Claims Officer made findings of fact that Alberts was aware of the work it had done and could have pursued a claim but chose not to do so. At paragraphs 49‑51 of his decision, the Claims Officer refers to evidence on which he based this determination. Alberts’ position that the limitation period would not commence until they were told by Mr. Possberg in the spring of 2009 that they would not be paid any more money, even if accepted as factual, would not be the date for discovery of the claim as required by the Act but, rather, only discovery of the non‑payment of its claim. To take the submission of counsel for Alberts to its logical conclusion, if Alberts had not heard from Big Sky for 20 years, it could still claim the limitation period did not start to run. To say that no money is owed is not an acknowledgment of a debt but, rather, an affirmation that there is no debt. I have reviewed the evidence referred to by the Claims Officer at paragraphs 49 to 51 of his decision and, in my opinion, the Claims Officer did not make a palpable and overriding error in this regard and deference must be given to his determination.


(c)        Was the Alberts claim not time barred because Big Sky concealed information from Alberts necessary to submit a claim?

[46]                 Section 17 of The Limitations Act provides that the limitation periods established by the Act will not apply if there has been a wilful concealment by the party against whom the claim is made. Section 17 provides:

Concealment

17   The limitation periods established by this Act or any other Act or regulation are suspended during any time in which the person against whom the claim is made:

(a)  wilfully conceals from the claimant the fact that injury, loss or damage has occurred, that it was caused by or contributed to by an act or omission or that the act or omission was that of the person against whom the claim is made; or

(b)  wilfully misleads the claimant as to the appropriateness of a proceeding as a means of remedying the injury, loss or damage.

 

[47]                 The position of Alberts is that Big Sky received a report from the engineering technologist as to the final dirt quantities moved by Alberts but failed to provide this report to Alberts such that Alberts was not in a position to invoice Big Sky for the work that had been done. This same submission was also made to the Claims Officer, who dealt with it in paragraphs 52‑57 of his decision. The Claims Officer specifically found that Big Sky had faxed a copy of the report to Alberts in a fax dated January 10, 2007. The fax, which is Document “E” in the index of documents, states in part: “Attached are the final excavation quantities for Site 1 and Site 2 @ Pelly.”


[48]                 While counsel for Alberts submitted that these were not the final dirt quantity reports regarding Pelly and that there were no dirt quantity reports for Porcupine Plain, there was no evidence tendered before the Claims Officer that there had been any “wilful concealment” of the dirt reports. The Claims Officer concluded that both sides bear responsibility for poor record‑keeping, but also found that sloppy record‑keeping is not wilful concealment. At question 892 of the cross‑examination of Mr. Smit, Mr. Smit indicated on behalf of Big Sky that he could not locate any other final quantity report. There was no evidence before the Claims Officer that there is any other final dirt report other than that contained in the January 10, 2007, fax. The onus of proving that Big Sky wilfully concealed such information is upon Alberts, and I conclude, as did the Claims Officer, that Alberts has not met this onus.

[49]                 In any event, the limitation period found in s. 5 of The Limitations Act commences on the day on which the claim is discovered, not when the claimant discovered the precise amount of the claim. The concept of discoverability, which was initially applicable only to actions in tort, has now been codified and incorporated into ss. 5 and 6 of The Limitations Act. See Pusch v. Freshair Enterprises Ltd., 2007 SKCA 60, [2007] 11 W.W.R. 1, and Stomp Pork Farm Ltd. v. Lombard General Insurance Co. of Canada, 2008 SKQB 405, [2009] 4 W.W.R. 483 at paras. 34‑35. The presumptions found in s. 6(2) are applicable in the circumstances of this case. The Claims Officer determined as a fact that Alberts knew it had a claim and could have pursued it but, as a result of the choice by Mervin Alberts, the president of the company, Alberts chose not to initiate proceedings. There was ample evidence on which the Claims Officer could have made such a determination and, accordingly, I find that he made no palpable or overriding error in his determination that s. 17 of the Act did not apply to this case.

Conclusion

[50]                 For the above noted reasons, the appeal is dismissed with costs to the respondent, Big Sky.
