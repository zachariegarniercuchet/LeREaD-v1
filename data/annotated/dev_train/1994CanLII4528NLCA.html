Supreme Court of Newfoundland — Court of Appeal

R. v. Simpson

Date: 19940316

Docket: 1993no.210

 

GOODRIDGE C.J.N.:— The issues in this case are:

(1) whether the police violated s. 503(l) of the Criminal Code by failing to bring the respondent, a person charged with an offence punishable by imprisonment for more than five years, before a justice within the time limits prescribed in that subsection

(2) whether such violation constituted a violation of the respon­dent's right under s. 9 of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms not to be arbitrarily detained, and

(3) if there was such a violation of s. 503(1) and if such violation constituted a breach of the s. 9 Charter right, whether a judicial stay of proceedings under s. 24(1) of the Charter was an appropriate and just remedy.

The charge

The respondent was charged with assault causing bodily harm and public mischief. The information against her contains the following counts:

Count No. 1

On or about the 8th day of August, A.D., 1992, at or near St. John's, in the province of Newfoundland, did unlawfully commit an assault that caused bodily harm to Teresa Bolt, thereby committing an indictable offence, contrary to Section 267(1)(b) of the Criminal Code of Canada.

Count No. 2

On or about the 8th day of AUGUST, A.D., 1992, at or near St. John's, in the Province of Newfoundland, did commit public mischief in that with intent to mislead a Peace Officer of the Royal Newfoundland Constabulary to enter upon or continue an investigation by making a false statement that accused Garry Allen of having committed an offence under Section 267(1)(b) of the Criminal Code of Canada (ASSAULT CAUSING BODILY HARM), thereby committing an indictable offence, contrary to section 140(î)(a)-140(2)(a) of the Criminal Code of Canada.

The facts

The respondent was arrested in relation to the charges on Sunday, March 14, 1993, at 1:30 p.m. She was detained by the police until brought before a judge of the Provincial Court on Tuesday, March 16, 1993, when she was released on an under-taking. This was just under 48 hours from the time of her arrest.

Monday, March 15, 1993, was a provincial holiday.

The respondent's appearance before a Provincial Court judge on Tuesday, March 16, 1993, was her first appearance before a justice after her arrest on Sunday, March 14, 1993. The information against her was also sworn on March 16, 1993, two days after her arrest.

On August 16, 1993, the respondent, alleging that the police had failed to comply with s. 503 of the Criminal Code and had thereby violated her s. 9 Charter rights, applied for a remedy pursuant to s. 24(1) of the Charter. The remedy which she sought was a judicial stay of proceedings; she did not request any other remedy. On October 18, 1993, Kennedy Prov. Ct. J. granted the requested stay. The trial judge concluded that the police had failed to comply with s. 503 of the Code, that this failure constituted a breach of s. 9 of the Charter and that the only appropriate and just remedy was a judicial stay as, in his opinion, it was not appropriate to permit the charges to proceed.

Relevant statutory and constitutional provisions

The provisions of the Criminal Code which the respondent was alleged to have violated are ss. 267(1)(b), 140(l)(a) and (2)(a) which provide:

267(1) Every one who, in committing an assault,

(b) causes bodily harm to the complainant,

is guilty of an indictable offence and liable to imprisonment for a term not exceeding ten years.

140(1) Every one commits public mischief who, with intent to mislead, causes a peace officer to enter on or continue an investigation by

(a) making a false statement that accuses some other person of having committed an offence;

(2) Every one who commits public mischief

(a) is guilty of an indictable offence and liable to imprisonment for a term not exceeding five years;

These sections are set forth not only to show the nature of the charges against the respondent but also to show that the first charge was punishable by imprisonment for a period not exceeding ten years. The relevance of this will become apparent.

Section 2 of the Code reads in part as follows:

2. In this Act,

"justice" means a justice of the peace or a provincial court judge, and includes two or more justices where two or more justices are, by law, required to act or, by law, act or have jurisdiction;

Section 498(1) provides:

498(1) Where a person who has been arrested without warrant by a peace officer is taken into custody, or where a person who has been arrested without warrant and delivered to a peace officer under subsection 494(3) is detained in custody under subsection 503(1) for

(a) an indictable offence mentioned in section 553,

(b) an offence for which the person may be prosecuted by indictment or for which he is punishable on summary conviction,

(c) an offence punishable on summary conviction, or

(d) any other offence that is punishable by imprisonment for five years or less,

and has not been taken before a justice or released from custody under any other provision of this Part, the officer in charge shall, as soon as practicable,

(e) release the person with the intention of compelling his appearance by way of summons,

(f) release the person on his giving his promise to appear,

(g) release the person on his entering into a recognizance before the officer in charge without sureties in such amount not exceeding five hundred dollars as the officer in charge directs, but without deposit of money or other valuable security, or

(h) if the person is not ordinarily resident in the province in which the person is in custody or does not ordinarily reside within two hundred kilometres of the place in which he is in custody, release the person on his entering into a recognizance before the officer in charge without sureties in such amount not exceeding five hundred dollars as the officer in charge directs and, if the officer in charge so directs, on his depositing with the officer in charge such sum of money or other valuable security not exceeding in amount or value five hundred dollars, as the officer in charge directs,

unless

(i) he believes on reasonable grounds that it is necessary in the public interest, having regard to all the circumstances including the need to

(i) establish the identity of the person,

(ii) secure or preserve evidence of or relating to the offence, or

(iii) prevent the continuation or repetition of the offence or the commission of another offence,

that the person be detained in custody or that the matter of his release from custody be dealt with under another provision of this Part, or

(j) he believes on reasonable grounds that, if the person is released by him from custody, the person will fail to attend court in order to be dealt with according to law.

The first charge against the respondent of assault causing bodily harm is not one of the offences described in paras. (a), (b), (c) or (d) above. More particularly it is not an offence mentioned in *s. 553; it is not a Crown option offence and therefore involves trial by indictment; it is an offence that is punishable by imprisonment for more than five years.

Section 503(1) and (2) provide:

503(1) A peace officer who arrests a person with or without warrant or to whom a person is delivered under subsection 494(3) shall cause the person to be detained in custody and, in accordance with the following provisions, to be taken before a justice to be dealt with according to law, namely,

(a) where a justice is available within a period of twenty-four hours after the person has been arrested by or delivered to the peace officer, the person shall be taken before a justice without unreasonable delay and in any event within that period, and

(b) where a justice is not available within a period of twenty-four hours after the person has been arrested by or delivered to the peace officer, the person shall be taken before a justice as soon as possible,

unless, at any time before the expiration of the time prescribed in paragraph (a) or (b) for taking the person before a justice,

(c) the peace officer or officer in charge releases the person under any other provision of this Part, or

(d) the peace officer or officer in charge is satisfied that the person should be released from custody, whether unconditionally under subsection (4) or otherwise conditionally or unconditionally, and so releases him.

(2) Where a peace officer or an officer in charge is satisfied that a person described in subsection (1) should be released from custody conditionally, he may, unless the person is detained in custody for an offence mentioned in section 522, release that person in accordance with paragraphs 498(1)0 to (h).

Subsection (2) provides that a peace officer, being satisfied that a person described in s-s. (1) should be released from custody conditionally, may release that person in accordance with s. 498(l)0 to (h). These paragraphs are set out above.

A person described in s-s. (1) is a person who has been arrested with or without a warrant. Neither of the charges against the respondent is included in the offences mentioned in s. 522.

Section 503(2) is not absolutely clear. It provides that a peace officer who is satisfied that an arrested person may be released from custody conditionally may release that person in accordance with s. 498(l)(f) to (h). These paras. (f) to (h) prescribe no conditions except a promise to appear.

As will appear, the police officer in this case wanted to release the respondent conditionally. The condition was that the respon­dent would stay away from certain persons. He saw no way of imposing such condition and therefore held the respondent until she could appear before a justice of the peace (in this case, a Provincial Court judge) who was empowered under s. 515 of the Criminal Code to impose conditions if Crown counsel established that such conditions should be ordered.

Putting all this together, it means that a person such as the respondent who has not been released under s. 503(2) of the Criminal Code shall be brought before a justice without unreasonable delay and in any event within 24 hours. When a justice is not available, the arrested person shall be brought before a justice as soon as possible.

Sections 9 and 24(1) of the Charter provide:

9. Everyone has the right not to be arbitrarily detained or imprisoned.

24(1) Anyone whose rights or freedoms, as guaranteed by this Charter, have been infringed or denied may apply to a court of competent jurisdiction to obtain such remedy as the court considers appropriate and just in the circumstances.

The hearing

There was a pre-trial application for a stay of proceedings, a remedy sought under s. 24(l) of the Charter on the basis that the s. 9 Charter right of the respondent had been denied by virtue of the failure of the police to bring the respondent before a justice without unreasonable delay.

Sergeant Roger Tinkam of the R.C.M.P testified that, in non-urban areas of Newfoundland, the R.C.M.P have an arrangement with justices of the peace to bring arrested persons before them pursuant to s. 503 as required. The need for such an arrangement is more demanding in the non-urban areas where a Provincial Court judge would not be regularly in attendance from Monday to Friday. Without having such an arrangement with justices of the peace, an arrested person might have to remain in custody for several days before being brought before a justice.

However, Sergeant Tinkam pointed out that in areas patrolled by the R.C.M.P. which are near St. John's, it is not uncommon for a person arrested on a weekend not to be brought before a justice until Monday morning when the Provincial Court opens.

Constable G.E. Crocker of the Royal Newfoundland Constabu­lary was the arresting officer. He advised that he was contacted by the complainant on February 28, 1993, about the charges against the respondent. This could not have been the first time that the subject matter of the complaints had come to the attention of the police as count 2 alleges that the respondent made a false statement to the police on August 8, 1992. No explanation was given as to what, if anything, transpired between August 8, 1992, and February 28, 1993, but evidently something was said to Constable Crocker on February 28, 1993, that led him to the conclusion that the arrest of the respondent was desirable.

Nothing transpired for two weeks. Constable Crocker himself was away for a portion of that period and, apparently, no other police officer was assigned to the case. Constable Crocker indicated that he made two calls at the home of the respondent and received no response. He did find her there on Sunday, March 14, 1993, at which time he arrested her.

The arrest was, apparently, based on Constable Crocker's belief that the respondent had committed an indictable offence. The information had not been sworn out at that time.

There were other procedures that might have been employed by Constable Crocker in accordance with the Criminal Code, such as a summons which could have been issued by a justice following the laying of an information.

Constable Crocker, however, felt that the respondent should be detained and released only upon the condition that she remain away from and not communicate with certain identified persons.

Sergeant J.H. Baggs of the Royal Newfoundland Constabulary, the respondent herself and Deputy Chief Leonard Power of the Royal Newfoundland Constabulary also testified. The evidence of the respondent herself did not really assist in the determination of the issue.

Sergeant Baggs and Deputy Chief Power spoke of the practice of the Royal Newfoundland Constabulary with respect to week-end arrests. They mentioned a manual of instructions provided to the police officers and presumably prepared by the constabulary or, for its benefit, by the department of justice.

This manual does not apparently contain any direction with respect to persons who are arrested on a week-end and whom the police are either unwilling or unable to release. If not released, such persons are simply held in custody until they can be brought before a Provincial Court judge on Monday morning or, if Monday is a holiday, on Tuesday morning.

In Corner Brook, the constabulary does bring weekend arrests before a justice without waiting for Monday morning to arrive.

The judgment

The judgment of the Provincial Court was given by Kennedy Prov. Ct. J. He gave careful thought to the issues that had been presented to him. He noted that the Provincial Court judges sit on a regular basis from Monday to Friday in St. John's and are available on the week-ends for limited purposes, such as dealing with requests for autopsy orders and for search warrants. These are matters that generally do not require a formal hearing and can be dealt with from the home of the judge.

He noted that the St. John's Commission of Justices of the Peace consists of 55 persons but none of these have been trained to deal with hearings for judicial interim release.

He held that the failure of the police to bring the respondent before a justice within the times limited by s. 503 was a violation of that section which resulted in an arbitrary detention of the respondent. He went on to hold that a stay of proceedings was the only remedy available and he so ordered.

Section 503(1)

This section requires the arresting officer to bring an arrested person, who is not released under any other provision of the Criminal Code, before a justice without unreasonable delay to be dealt with according to law. It is sometimes thought that the police may detain such a person for 24 hours. The dominant time factor is "without unreasonable delay" and the limit placed on this period is 24 hours. An unreasonable delay could nevertheless occur in less than 24 hours. It should not be assumed therefore that a violation of s. 503(1) is not taking place notwithstanding that 24 hours has not expired.

Section 503(l)(b) provides that, where a justice is not available within 24 hours after the person has been arrested, the person shall be taken before a justice as soon as possible. It seems that this is being relied upon by the police to justify the continuation of detention on a week-end until the Provincial Court opens on Monday morning. While the purpose of this enactment is not absolutely clear, it probably applies to situations where a person is arrested in a remote area where a justice is not available. It cannot be said to apply to an arrest in the City of St. John's where there are a large number of Provincial Court judges and 55 justices of the peace. It is ludicrous to say in those circumstances that a justice is not available within a 24-hour period. It is difficult to conceive of any circumstances in St. John's (and probably in most parts of Newfoundland although this judgment only puts in issue police practices in St. John's) where the police could rely on s. 503(1)(b) to justify detaining an arrested person until the Provincial Court opened on Monday morning or Tuesday morning as the case might be.

Section 503 may be one of the most important procedural provisions of the Criminal Code. The liberty of the subject is dominant. A person not convicted of an offence should never be held in custody except in accordance with constitutionally valid provisions of the Criminal Code or other legislation. In some jurisdictions, even during the sitting week, the police will bring an arrested person before a justice, as soon as the various tasks that generally follow an arrest are completed, for hearing as to whether the person should be released or detained. If necessary, a trial will be interrupted for this purpose.

The paramountcy of the liberty of the subject has been recog­nized in English law from the earliest times. Freedom is a fundamental right. It is not to be taken away except in strict accordance with the law.

The prerogative writ of habeas corpus was developed as a remedy for unlawful detention. The superior courts are entrusted with the function of ensuring that any person improperly detained shall be released.

Where a person is arrested with or without a warrant, it is the duty of the arresting officer to ensure that that person is not detained any longer than is absolutely necessary and that, if he or she is not authorized by law to bring about the release, the person is brought before a justice of the peace who may determine whether the detention should continue or not and, if it is not to continue, what lawful conditions should be attached to the release.

The peace officer, of course, has a problem. In this case Constable Crocker was required to bring the respondent before a justice without unreasonable delay. The particular delay in this case appears to have been about 90 minutes which would have brought the time to about mid-afternoon on Sunday. How was he to go about finding a justice of the peace ready, willing and able to preside at a hearing at that time?

There was a practical problem. The Provincial Court judges did not, at that time, sit on week-ends. There were duty judges but their function as duty judges was, apparently, to deal with ex parte process. There were 55 justices of the peace other than Provincial Court judges in the city area. The constabulary does not appear to have been particularly aware of this fact and, in any event, there is no indication that any of the justices were trained in the judicial process with respect to hearings for judicial interim release.

There was no indication before the court as to what the practice in this respect in other provinces is. The problem in St. John's is currently being taken care of by Provincial Court judges who hold hearings in the court-house near the lock-up on Saturday and Sunday mornings.

That there is a practical problem is clear. It is not, however, for the Court of Appeal to determine what the solution to the problem is. All the court can say is that if s. 503 is to be complied with (and, of course, there must be compliance), there must be justices as defined available before whom people arrested on week-ends who cannot be released except by order of a justice may be brought without unreasonable delay. What has happened in this case should never be allowed to happen again.

Charter, s. 9

Crown counsel argued before this court that the detention in this case was not an arbitrary detention. The discussion revolved around the meaning of the word "arbitrary". There is a tendency to resort to dictionary definitions. This resort generally leads to other words which themselves need defining. Constable Crocker himself had probably given no thought as to whether his decision to hold the respondent until conditions could be placed upon her release was arbitrary. If he had been fully aware of and understood the provisions of s. 503(1) and knew the responsibilities which lay upon him as set forth above, and nevertheless decided to hold the respondent until she could appear in the Provincial Court on Tuesday morning, there would be little doubt that the detention was arbitrary.

This raises the question as to whether it is any less arbitrary because Constable Crocker appears not to have been fully aware of his legal obligations in this respect. To take this position is to define arbitrary from a subjective point of view, from the point of view of the detainer and not the detainee.

It is true, of course, that the respondent, when brought before a justice, might not have been released. She was in fact never given the opportunity to seek her own release (except technically by habeas corpus which was not a practical alternative in the circumstances of this case). If she had been given that opportunity and was denied her freedom, her detention would then have been lawful. Unless and until judicial interim release was denied, however, once the period that is denoted by the words "without unreasonable delay" had expired and until she was thereafter brought before a justice there was certainly an unlawful detention.

Counsel argued and it is acknowledged that an unlawful deten­tion is not necessarily an arbitrary detention. An unlawful deten­tion may result from a technical error of process. Here there was a major violation of a statutory provision protecting the fundamental right of the respondent to be free unless properly detained by law. It is an understatement to describe such detention as unlawful. It must be viewed as arbitrary. There was a failure of a policeman to carry out his duty under the law which, whether deliberate or simply neglectful, resulted in the respondent being detained. By act or neglect, the administrative or judicial system, or both, failed the respondent. A neglect to follow s. 503(1) or to provide the mechanism by which s. 503(1) may be followed is just as arbitrary as a positive decision not to follow s. 503(l).

The respondent must be considered to have been arbitrarily detained and to be entitled to apply for a remedy under s. 24(1) of the Charter.

Charter, s. 24(1)

Section 24(1) of the Charter provides that anyone whose rights or freedoms have been infringed or denied may apply to a court of competent jurisdiction to apply such remedy as the court considers appropriate and just in the circumstances.

The question arises as to what is a court of competent jurisdic­tion. This is a question that involves many considerations. It is not proposed to examine the question in its broadest context but rather to consider it within the confines of a case such as the one before court. The decision of the Supreme Court of Canada in R. v. Mills (1986), 26 C.C.C. (3d) 481, 29 D.L.R. (4th) 161, [1986] 1 S.C.R. 863 (S.C.C.), although given by a divided court, has offered some light on the issue of what is a court of competent jurisdiction. The court was in agreement that a judge presiding at a preliminary inquiry is not a court of competent jurisdiction for the purpose of granting a remedy under s. 24(l). The court before whom an accused person is being tried is a court of competent jurisdiction.

For the purposes of this case, there is no dispute as to the right of the trial judge to consider a s. 24(1) application. For reasons already stated there should likewise be no dispute that the respondent was entitled to obtain such remedy as the trial judge considered "appropriate and just in the circumstances". In Mills, McIntyre J. said at p. 493:

These courts will be courts of competent jurisdiction, where they have jurisdiction conferred by statute over the offences and persons and power to make the orders sought. It is to be hoped that trial judges will devise, as the circumstances arise, imaginative remedies to serve the needs of individual cases. Such remedies must remain, however, subject to constitutional restraint, that is, they must remain within the ambit of criminal powers. A claim for a remedy under s. 24(1) arising in the course of the trial will fall within the jurisdiction of these courts as a necessary incident of the trial process.

He continued at pp. 501:

What remedies are available when an application under s. 24(1) of the Charter succeeds? Section 24(1) again is silent on the question. It merely provides that the appellant may obtain such remedy as the court considers "appropriate and just in the circumstances". It is difficult to imagine language which could give the court a wider and less fettered discretion. It is impossible to reduce this wide discretion to some sort of binding formula for general application in all cases, and it is not for appellate courts to pre-empt or cut down this wide discretion. No court may say, for example, that a stay of proceedings will always be appropriate in a given type of case. Although there will be cases where a trial judge may well conclude that a stay would be the appropriate remedy, the circumstances will be infinitely variable from case to case and the remedy will vary with the circumstances.

Mills makes it clear that a criminal trial court is a court of competent jurisdiction to grant s. 24(1) remedies while the matter in which the question of a Charter violation rises is sub judice. What is less clear, however, is what type of remedies may be given. McIntyre J., in Mills, suggested that the remedy must be within the ambit of the criminal powers of the trial court. This would seem to exclude civil remedies such as damages. If damages were considered to be the just and appropriate remedy, the persons whose Charter rights have been violated might have to proceed in a court of civil jurisdiction.

A criminal trial court may not always be the proper place to seek a s. 24(1) remedy. A person whose rights have been violated may not be put on trial; the proceedings initiating the trial process may not have yet been launched; the trial judge may have been involved in the Charter violation: see R. v. Rahey (1987), 33 C.C.C. (3d) 289, 39 D.L.R. (4th) 481, [1987] 1 S.C.R. 588 (S.C.C.). The appropriate and just remedy may be damages which a criminal trial court may not be deemed to have power to award or, having such power, may not have monetary jurisdiction to make an award in the amount warranted.

In the publication entitled "The Canadian Charter of Rights" subtitled, "The Prosecution and Defence of Criminal and other Statutory Offences", vol. 3 by McLeod, Takach, Morton and Segal published by Carswell in 1989, a number of potential remedies are discussed. These are:

(a) Dismissal

(b) Stay of proceedings

(c) Quashing of search warrant

(d) Quashing of information

(e) Quashing of committal for trial

(f) Order expediting trial

(g) Order for new trial

(h) Mistrial

(i) Exclusion of evidence

(j) Imposition of minimum sentence

(k) Declaratory relief

(l) Release from custody

(m) Relief related to parole

(n) Injunctive relief

(o) Cost and monetary compensation

(p) Destruction of evidence

(q) Other cases

For the purposes of this case we need really only consider a stay of proceeding, a lesser sentence and monetary compensation.

Within the context of a criminal trial a stay of proceedings is the ultimate remedy. In R. v. Erickson (1984), 13 C.C.C. (3d) 269 at p. 275, 12 C.R.R. 193, [1984] 5 W.W.R. 577 (B.C.C.A.), Esson J.A. said:

That brings me to the issue upon which, in my view, the appeal must succeed. That is the question whether, assuming everything else in favour of the accused, there was any ground in law for making an order under s. 24(1) that the indictment be quashed.

Assuming everything else in favour of the accused, the question is whether the trial judge erred in law on the facts found by him in granting such a remedy. In my view, he clearly did. He granted the most sweeping and drastic remedy in the arsenal of remedies without any factual basis that could possibly permit the conclusion that it would be either just or appropriate to do so. He concluded as a fact that there was no malice or negligence by either the Crown or the police. In the light of that conclusion, the point as to whether the remedy could be granted is really not arguable. The need to impress upon all parties the requirement that the law be obeyed is not enough to justify granting a remedy which is not otherwise just and appropriate. I say that with full recognition of the fundamental nature of the right created by s. 454 and the importance of it being complied with by those who are obligated to do so; but a breach does not in itself justify turning the system on its head.

The source of the error may be the view of the trial judge, which is implicit in his decision, that having found that, in connection with the charges before him there had been a breach of the rights of the accused, he must grant some remedy. I will assume that for every breach of a Charter right there is some remedy. It simply does not follow that every breach must lead to some remedy being granted at trial. The purpose of the trial is, as it was before the Charter, to decide whether the accused is guilty. Breaches of Charter rights do not become a proper subject of inquiry at trial simply because they occurred in relation to the charge being tried.

That case involved an application for a remedy for a breach of s. 9 of the Charter. So too did the case of R. v. Sullivan (1991, Nfld. C.A. unreported) [since reported 96 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 7, 13 W.C.B. (2d) 547], where the respondent had been held in custody for 28 hours after his arrest, at least four hours more than the maximum time prescribed by s. 503(1).

In that case Gushue J.A., in setting aside the stay ordered by the trial court, said [at p. 9]:

It is certainly arguable that where a maximum period of imprisonment or detention is prescribed by statute, any detention in excess of that period, without explanation, is unreasonable or unjustified and thus arbitrary within the meaning of s. 9 of the Charter. Indeed, we are prepared to accept, for the purposes of this case, that the respondent was arbitrarily imprisoned. We find it unnecessary to enter upon an analysis of the judge's finding in that regard when, in our view, the remedy of entering stays of proceedings was totally unjustified.

The respondent's imprisonment past the 24 hour period was of a very short duration. He had been charged with three serious offences and, unquestiona­bly, had he appeared before the court within the statutory period, he would have been remanded in custody pending a bail application, as he indeed was when he did appear. While his rights had been breached and he was entitled to apply for a remedy under s. 24(1) of the Charter, it is clear that the breach was not of a serious nature.

The staying of criminal charges against an accused person is obviously the ultimate response by the courts to infringements of that person's constitu­tional rights or an abuse of court process and should only be utilized in the most serious and clearest of cases. While a judge is given the discretion under s. 24(1) to grant a remedy which "the court considers just and appropriate in the circumstances", it does not follow that the court is thereby given an unqualified right to deal with a breach as it sees fit. Any remedy granted must be for good reason in the circumstances of the particular case.

It is unnecessary to examine all of the suggested remedies to determine whether they are remedies which may be granted and, of those which may be granted, the circumstances when they are appropriate and just. The remedy sought in this case was a stay of proceedings. This has generally been recognized as a remedy that a trial judge may grant. What is seldom addressed, however, are the circumstances in which it is an appropriate and just remedy.

A stay of proceedings is a very special remedy reserved for very special occasions.

Dubin J. in R. v. Young (1984), 13 C.C.C. (3d) 1 at p. 31, 40 C.R. (3d) 289, 10 C.R.R. 307 (Ont. CA.), said:

I am satisfied on the basis of the authorities that I have set forth above that there is a residual discretion in a trial court judge to stay proceedings where compelling an accused to stand trial would violate those fundamental principles of justice which underlie the community's sense of fair play and decency and to prevent the abuse of a court's process through oppressive or vexatious proceedings. It is a power, however, of special application which can only be exercised in the clearest of cases.

This statement was approved by the Supreme Court of Canada in R. v. Jewitt (1985), 21 C.C.C. (3d) 7, 20 D.L.R. (4th) 651, [1985] 2 S.C.R. 128 (S.C.C.), and in R. v. Keyowski (1988), 40 C.C.C. (3d) 481, [1988] 1 S.C.R. 657, 62 C.R. (3d) 349 (S.C.C.).

The Young case dealt with abuse of process. It may be that the jurisprudence with regard to granting a stay with respect to Charter breaches could be even more stringent than that sug­gested by Dubin J. in Young because, in the case of Charter breaches, there is usually a wider, range of remedies available and, moreover, the victim of the breach has the further remedy of suing in civil court for what has become known as a constitutional tort — a breach of a constitutional right.

The public has a vital interest in seeing that persons who are accused of a crime are tried. A stay of proceedings should not be allowed to interfere with the prosecution of an accused person to conclusion. That may even be true of any s. 24(1) remedy except where the breach in some way affects the trial itself.

There are of course circumstances where a stay is the only remedy. One of these, not necessarily concerned with a Charter breach, is where there is an abuse of process to such a degree that a stay is demanded: see Young, supra. Another circumstance is where it would be unfair to allow the trial to proceed. That would generally involve a situation where the accused person would be unable to make a full answer and defence.

Another circumstance is where a stay is warranted to prevent the continuation of an improper police practice. This present case, or a fact situation corresponding with the present case, could be illustrative of such a situation. In this particular case, the police practice in respect of s. 503(1) had been relatively constant beforehand but the issue that comes up in this case had, according to the evidence, not been raised before. A stay may not be necessary in this case but, if it appeared that the police were to revert to their former practice, the ordering of stays might be the only way to end that practice.

A stay at trial may be appropriate where there is a pattern of unreasonable delay in bringing matters on for trial which may be attributed to the Crown.

Stays should not be given as s. 24(1) remedies except in special circumstances such as those illustrated above. The Charter is not intended to defeat the criminal process but to protect citizens from conduct that is contrary to those rights therein described. It is only when interference with the criminal process is the only way in which the appropriate remedy may be given — such as to prevent an unfair trial or to bring a stop to improper practices — that a stay should be ordered.

McIntyre J. in Mills, supra, expressed the hope that trial judges would devise imaginative remedies but expressed the reservations

that such remedies should remain subject to constitutional restraint. They must remain within the ambit of criminal powers.

The powers of a trial judge in the Provincial Court under s. 24(l) are relatively limited and almost appear in a case such as this to require that the judge either stay the proceeding or leave the accused to seek redress in a civil process in the Trial Division of the Supreme Court of Newfoundland.

Usually, the remedy that is sought where there is a Charter breach is a stay of proceedings. If such is sought but not justified in a particular case, the fact that an alternate remedy may not be within the ambit of the powers of the trial court should be of no concern to the trial judge. Such alternate remedy may be pursued elsewhere. It is true that this makes it inconvenient for the person whose Charter rights have been violated but inconvenience alone does not justify the granting of the extreme remedy of a stay of proceedings.

There can be no doubt that the violation of the respondent's s. 9 Charter right in this case was serious. The police had several months in which to begin proceedings against her and for reasons unknown to the court failed to do so. The matter was energized at the end of February, 1993, but even then no action was taken that gave the impression that the police had any sense of urgency whatsoever. Moreover, the police failed to take any of the several alternative processes and in fact failed to lay any charges until two days after she was arrested. Defence counsel had alerted the police to the requirement of s. 501(3) during the detention.

While the court does not receive the impression that the police acted in bad faith, it seems that the police were totally unaware of the civil rights of the respondent and seemed to have taken a rather casual attitude towards her detention.

However, the violation of her rights, while serious, bears no relation to the charges laid against her. The respondent was unlawfully imprisoned. This is a civil tort and a constitutional tort. The respondent may seek her remedy in the Trial Division by another proceeding. The charges against her should proceed because the trial would not be unfair and there is no justification at this stage in ordering a stay for the purpose of bringing to a halt improper administrative or judicial practices.

The appeal is allowed. The matter is remitted to the Provincial Court for trial.

GUSHUE J.A. concurs with GOODRIDGE C.J.N.

O'NEILL J.A. (dissenting):—This is an appeal from Kennedy Prov. Ct. J., who entered a stay of proceedings, pursuant to s. 24(1) of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms, prior to the scheduled trial of the respondent in Provincial Court, in which court she had elected to be tried, and had pleaded not guilty to charges of assault causing bodily harm, contrary to s. 267(l)(b) of the Criminal Code and of public mischief, contrary to s. 140(l)(a) of the Criminal Code.

The incidents with respect to which the charges were laid are alleged to have occurred on August 8, 1992. It appears that no action was taken by the police until February 28, 1993, when Constable Crocker of the Royal Newfoundland Constabulary became involved, although the public mischief charge indicates that it arose out of an alleged false statement made with intent to mislead an officer of the constabulary. Be that as it may, it only came to the attention of Constable Crocker on that date and no real action was taken by him with respect to questioning or apprehending the respondent until Sunday, March 14, 1993, when he went to the respondent's residence in St. John's and arrested her. There was evidence that the constable had previously gone to the respondent's residence a couple of times, had knocked on the door, but had gotten no response. There was also evidence that the constable had been away from duty for 10 days during that period. Clearly, there seems to have been no great urgency, at least on the constable's part, to take the respondent into custody.

Following her arrest, which occurred at approximately 1:30 p.m. on Sunday, the respondent was taken to a lock-up or holding facility in St. John's and was not brought before a justice until late in the morning of Tuesday, March 16, 1993, when she was released on conditions that she avoid any contact with the complainant and members of the complainant's family.

The conditions attached to the respondent's release, which were ones the constable had requested and to which the respondent consented, were the very reasons why the respondent was held in custody, since the constable wished to have these conditions made a prerequisite to the respondent's release. The release, on conditions, could only be implemented by a justice, or, as the constable believed, by a Provincial Court judge. The respondent was not kept in custody for any other reason, such as being required for questioning or to prevent the commission of any offence.

The constable, in making the arrest was aware that, as the system operated in St. John's, the respondent would remain in custody until the first court day in Provincial Court following the arrest, which, in this case, happened to be Tuesday because Monday was a provincial holiday.

While at the holding facility, the respondent contacted a lawyer. The lawyer was told by the arresting officer that the respondent would not be released since the police were not prepared to release her without the conditions already referred to. When asked by counsel if the respondent would be brought before a justice in accordance with s. 503(1) of the Criminal Code, the police officer replied that she would not be brought before a justice until Tuesday, March 16, 1993, when the Provincial Court was sitting.

The application for a stay of proceedings was based on alleged violations of ss. 9 and 15 of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms.

The argument with respect to a breach of s. 15 of the Charter was that availability of justices before whom arrested persons could be brought, was not constant in all parts of the province and therefore, there was an inequality in the rights of citizens to "equal protection and equal benefit of the law". That argument was rejected by the trial judge and was not pursued here.

The real issue is with respect to s. 9 of the Charter. The basis for the application was that her continued detention following her arrest was contrary to s. 503(1) of the Criminal Code and hence, that she had been arbitrarily detained or imprisoned contrary to s. 9 of the Charter.

Although the application specifically requested a stay of proceedings under s. 24(l),' the trial judge was not thereby restricted to dealing solely with that particular remedy. Indeed, in his very thorough decision, the trial judge canvassed the various remedies which might be appropriate arising out of the application and concluded that the only just and appropriate remedy was a stay of proceedings.

The Crown appealed.

The appeal

(1) The factum of the appellant raises three issues:

(2) Whether there was a violation of s. 503(1) of the Criminal Code.

(3) Whether there was a breach of s. 9 of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms.

Whether a judicial stay of proceedings was a just and appropriate remedy.

The first two issues were not really seriously pursued by the appellant and can be disposed of fairly readily.

Was there a violation of s. 503(1)?

The relevant part of s. 503 is as follows:

503(1) A peace officer who arrests a person with or without warrant or to whom a person is delivered under subsection 494(3) shall cause the person to be detained in custody and, in accordance with the following provisions, to be taken before a justice to be dealt with according to law, namely,

(a) where a justice is available within a period of twenty-four hours after the person has been arrested by or delivered to the peace officer, the person shall be taken before a justice without unreasonable delay and in any event within that period, and

(b) where a justice is not available within a period of twenty-four hours after the person has been arrested by or delivered to the peace officer, the person shall be taken before a justice as soon as possible,

unless, at any time before the expiration of the time prescribed in paragraph (a) or (b) for taking the person before a justice,

(c) the peace officer or officer in charge releases the person under any other provision of this Part, or

(d) the peace officer or officer in charge is satisfied that the person should be released from custody, whether unconditionally under subsection (4) or otherwise conditionally or unconditionally, and so releases him.

The section requires that a person such as the respondent, arrested without warrant, which was the case here, and not released under any other provision, be brought before a justice without unreasonable delay, and, in any event, within a period of 24 hours following the arrest. Section 503(1)(b), in my view, contemplates a situation which might arise in remote areas of the country where because of problems such as geography or trans­portation, a justice, before whom an arrested person should be brought, would not be readily available.

As noted earlier, Constable Crocker was aware when he arrested the respondent on Sunday that she would not be brought before a justice until Tuesday, some 48 hours later. Even when reminded of his obligation under s. 503 by duty counsel on behalf of the respondent, he did not, nor in fairness to him, could he, by following police practice, bring the respondent before a justice before Tuesday morning.

There was evidence that in the St. John's area there are some 10 Provincial Court judges and approximately 50 justices of the peace, all of whom would satisfy the definition of justice as it appears in s. 503.

Clearly, there was a breach of s. 503 of the Criminal Code.

Was there a breach of s. 9 of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms?

We must now consider whether the detention of the respondent was an arbitrary detention as contemplated by s. 9 of the Charter:

9. Everyone has the right not to be arbitrarily detained or imprisoned.

There was no system in place in St. John's whereby the obligation placed on a peace officer by s. 503 of the Criminal Code could be observed. However, in the rural parts of this province, which for the most part are policed by the R.C.M.P., a procedure is in place whereby justices are available to permit the police officers serving in these areas to comply with s. 503. What is even more surprising is that the constabulary, which also polices the City of Corner Brook, had implemented a policy of having justices availa­ble as required under s. 503. .

In the situation here, while it cannot be said that there was a deliberate effort on the part of the police constable not to comply with the provisions of s. 503(1), no effort was made to comply with the section either by the police officer, by those in authority in the constabulary, or by those involved in the administration of justice and in a position to institute and implement a system whereby the section could be complied with. Neither the arresting officer nor his superior officers who were called as witnesses seemed aware of the obligation of a peace officer to comply with s. 503. Even if one could argue that the 24-hour period referred to in s. 503(1)(b) were the applicable period, and not "without unreasonable delay", as is the real obligation, there was not even any effort to comply with that.

This was not a question whether there was a failure to comply with the system for some technical reasons or an oversight — a falling through the cracks. The respondent's detention occurred because of what could be termed a deliberate policy on the part of those charged with the responsibility for the administration of justice to ignore the very clear direction embodied in s. 503. However, whether it occurred by neglect or deliberate policy, where there was a failure to provide a system whereby justices are made available, and a detention occurs because of that failure, then that detention is arbitrary within the meaning of s. 9 of the Charter.

In the factum of the appellant, there is a footnote indicating that: "a system of having Provincial Court judges available to conduct judicial interim release hearings on weekends is now in place".

The remedy under s. 24(1)

Section 24 of the Charter is as follows:

24(1) Anyone whose rights or freedoms, as guaranteed by this Charter, have been infringed or denied may apply to a court of competent jurisdiction to obtain such remedy as the court considers appropriate and just in the circumstances.

(2) Where, in proceedings under subsection (1), a court concludes that evidence was obtained in a manner that infringed or denied any rights or freedoms guaranteed by this Charter, the evidence shall be excluded if it is established that, having regard to all the circumstances, the admission of it in the proceedings would bring the administration of justice into disrepute.

In his argument, Crown counsel argued that the trial judge did not sufficiently inquire into alternative remedies available under s. 24(1) of the Charter such as a reduction in sentence or an award of damages. He also argued that the remedy of habeas corpus was open to the respondent.

The trial judge considered the remedy of a reduction in sentence and concluded, in my view, correctly, that such was not an appropriate remedy. His reasons for so concluding are set out later in this decision; I agree with his reasons and conclusion.

With respect to an award of damages, I have very grave doubts as to whether a Provincial Court judge, sitting on what might be called the criminal side, would have jurisdiction to make an award of damages. There is also the problem that whereas the Provincial Court, on its civil side, has jurisdiction to award damages, the amount which may be awarded in that court is limited. Assuming then that a Provincial Court judge were to have that jurisdiction, (and I make no decision as to his right to do so in a criminal matter), and were to conclude that the appropriate damages to be awarded as a remedy were in excess of the civil jurisdiction of the Provincial Court, the applicant would then have to make an application in the Supreme Court where the matter would presum­ably be argued with the attendant increased legal fees, delay and inconvenience.

The submission of Crown counsel with respect to habeas corpus was that counsel for the respondent, having been advised that the respondent would not be brought before a justice before Tuesday morning, could have sought her release through habeas corpus or by a s. 24(1) application in the Supreme Court of Newfoundland. For very obvious reasons, neither of these steps was a reasonable alternative. Nothing further need be said with respect to these submissions.

In R. v. Sullivan (1991), unreported [reported 96 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 7, 13 W.C.B. (2d) 547], this court dealt with an application for a remedy for a breach of s. 9 of the Charter where an accused person had been detained for some 28 hours following his arrest. Gushue J.A., having stated that the court was prepared to accept, for the purposes of the case, that the accused had been arbitrarily imprisoned, went on to state [at p. 9]:

[The accused] had been charged with three serious offences and, unquestiona­bly, had he appeared before the court within the statutory period, he would have been remanded in custody pending a bail application, as he indeed was when he did appear. While his rights had been breached and he was entitled to apply for a remedy under s. 24(1) of the Charter, it is clear that the breach was not of a serious nature.

The staying of criminal charges against an accused person is obviously the ultimate response by the courts to infringements of that person's constitu­tional rights or an abuse of court process and should only be utilized in the most serious and clearest cases. While a judge is given the discretion under s. 24(1) to grant a remedy which "the court considers just and appropriate in the circumstances", it does not follow that the court is thereby given an unqualified right to deal with a breach as it sees fit. Any remedy granted must be for good reason in the circumstances of the particular case.

Clearly, Sullivan can be distinguished from the matter before us. The respondent here was just 21 years of age at the time of her arrest, she was a resident of St. John's, there was no evidence of any criminal record, and the alleged incidents giving rise to the charge had occurred some seven or eight months prior to her arrest. Further, the respondent had denied to the arresting officer her involvement with respect to at least one of the charges and the police officer had indicated that he had no reason to keep her for more than an hour or so and that he was prepared to have her released provided that she entered into the conditions already referred to.

Although considerable evidence was adduced at the hearing with respect to the practices of the Royal Newfoundland Constabulary and the R.C.M.P. with respect to the availability or non-availability of Provincial Court judges, and the availability and ability of justices to deal with the release of persons arrested, no evidence whatsoever was introduced or sought to be introduced with respect to the seriousness of the matters or incidents giving rise to the charges brought against the respondent, or as to why she was arrested on a Sunday during a long weekend, as opposed to being served with a summons to appear following the laying of an information. I might add that the actual information setting forth the charges against the respondent was not actually sworn to until March 14th, just shortly before the release of the respondent.

In his decision the trial judge said at p. 87:

While the court has found that there was no police policy to detain all persons arrested outside the periods of time when the Provincial Court in St. John's is sitting, the court did find that this was the practice. Further the court found that the practice operates in all but exceptional cases.

The practice operates to deprive individuals who have been detained of their liberty in an arbitrary manner. Everyone familiar with the justice system in St. John's is aware of the practice. It does operate in a manner wherein people are arbitrarily detained. To equate the availability of a justice under s. 503(1) of the Code to the hours when court is in session is not an acceptable practice.

In considering the remedies which may be available to a court on such an application, the trial judge reviewed cases where courts had afforded, as a remedy, a substantial reduction in the penalty which might otherwise have been imposed. He went on to state that in his opinion, that particular remedy would not be appropri­ate in the manner before him. He said:

First, the application has been brought pre-trial. The accused is of course at this stage presumed innocent. The remedy would have to be held in abeyance until the end of the trial and applied only if the accused were found guilty. It is a remedy that can only be applied after a trial and a conviction and is not a remedy which could apply in the event of an acquittal. It is therefore only a potential remedy contingent upon a conviction and cannot be applied in event of an acquittal. It would be no remedy if that result were attained.

And later:

A reduced sentence, even a substantially reduced one, would not adequately address the deprivation of liberty involved. The deprivation of liberty in an arbitrary manner is a serious violation of the Charter. The deprivation of liberty commenced with the refusal of the police to act under s. 503 of the Code. After having been asked by duty counsel if they would arrange for the appearance of the accused before a justice, they failed to act. The officer simply indicated that the accused would be held until Court was open on Tuesday morning. The failure to act when asked to do so made the violation of the right all the more serious and flagrant.

In arriving at his conclusion to enter a stay of proceedings, the trial judge stated:

There is no doubt that the offences before the court are serious ones. In the situation when the court confronts serious charges and a serious rights violation it must consider what remedy is appropriate. I have delineated each of the remedies and offered my opinion on the appropriateness of them in regard to the circumstances of this case.

The failure to act in accordance with the Criminal Code provisions relating to bringing the accused before a justice resulted in a substantial deprivation of liberty in an arbitrary manner. The liberty of individuals is a cherished societal value. An arbitrary deprivation of liberty is not acceptable to society. To afford no remedy at all would be to condone the deprivation of liberty.

I have concluded that even in the face of serious charges a remedy must be granted. So serious and flagrant was the violation, I feel that it is not appropriate to permit the charges to proceed — all other remedies are inadequate.

He concluded:

I feel that no remedy short of a stay of proceedings would address in an appropriate and just manner the arbitrary detention in the circumstances of this case.

The matter of the appropriateness of the remedy here is one vested in the Provincial Court judge, sitting as a court of competent jurisdiction. The trial judge in exercising the discretion granted to him by s. 24(l) of the Charter, thoroughly canvassed the various remedies which might be applicable and concluded that no remedy short of a stay of proceedings would be appropriate and just.

I cannot say that in the circumstances of this case the trial judge was in error in exercising his discretion under s. 24(1) of the Charter by entering a stay of proceedings.

I would dismiss the appeal.

Appeal allowed.
