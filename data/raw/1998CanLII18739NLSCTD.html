[1]                      Green, J.: This matter arises out of what was one of the longest civil trials in Newfoundland history. It involves an application for special costs under Rule 55 in favour of a defendant, CSE Marine Services Inc. (CSE), who was successful on a non-suit motion. The applicant claims that the costs should be calculated on a solicitor and own client basis and should be awarded against the plaintiffs as well as against the plaintiffs' lawyer personally.

[2]                      It raises issues concerning the standards expected of a lawyer in the conduct of litigation and the nature of the relationship between that lawyer and his client as it impacts on decisions made with respect to the manner in which the litigation is carried on.

[3]                      Although advanced on a number of different bases, the main thrust of CSE's claim to special costs is that, on the facts, it was "impossible" for CSE to have been found liable to any of the plaintiffs and that the plaintiffs knew this or at the least could have satisfied themselves that such was the case if they had taken the opportunities presented to examine the information available. By not doing so, it was submitted, the plaintiffs improperly dragged CSE through an unnecessary and extremely expensive trial. CSE's position was vindicated, it is said, by the fact that the plaintiffs were ultimately non-suited.

Background

(a) The Non-suit

[4]                      On May 18, 1995, I granted a non-suit motion in favour of CSE following the conclusion of the plaintiffs' case after over 90 days of trial. At the request of the parties, I reserved on the issue of costs flowing from the non-suit pending further application and argument. The decision on the merits of the non-suit motion has been reported at (1995), 131 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 351. The interrelationship of the parties and the factual background of the case, as well as an outline of the pleadings, is set out in another previous decision of mine reported at (1994), 120 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 313 as well as in the final judgment on the merits of the claim filed on July 31, 1998, [[1999] Nfld. & P.E.I.R. TBEd. JN.001], with addendum dealing with costs on September 2, 1998 (as yet unreported).

[5]                      The trial of the claims against the other defendants proceeded after some delay due, amongst other things, to the withdrawal of plaintiffs' counsel and an appeal against the non-suit decision which did not proceed because of the plaintiffs' failure or inability to put up security for costs which had been ordered by the appeal court. As indicated, the trial has now been concluded and judgment rendered.

[6]                      In order to appreciate the basis of the current application, it is necessary to review parts of the convoluted history of the conduct of the case from the time of its inception, which was long before I became involved as the trial judge. A number of pretrial applications had been made, some of which have a bearing on the current application, particularly as they relate to alleged representations that were made by counsel in the course of argument. I have reviewed the court files with respect to these matters. In some cases, I have been provided with transcripts of what was said.

(b) The Claims

[7]                      The present proceeding, as ultimately tried, resulted from the consolidation of four separate actions that had been commenced between 1989 and 1991. The first action, commenced by Harold Efford on May 16, 1989, ([1989] St. J. No. 1515; hereinafter, the "Delay Claim") claimed damages flowing from an alleged delay in the delivery by Eastern Shipbuilders Limited (ESB) of the M.V. Shelby Ann, a 64' 11" fishing vessel which ESB had agreed to construct for Efford. The second action, also commenced by Efford, this time on August 23, 1989, ([1989] St. J. No. 2516; hereinafter the "Unseaworthiness Claim") claimed damages from ESB resulting from alleged improper construction of the vessel. In relation to that claim, Efford had commissioned a marine architect, Clarence Carroll, to inquire into and prepare a report concerning what were perceived by Efford to be potential seaworthiness and stability problems with the vessel.

[8]                      The third action was commenced on November 16, 1990 by Arthur Petten and Joyce Petten personally ([1990 B. No. 290; hereinafter the "Business Loss Claim") claiming financial loss suffered by the Pettens, directly and through their company ESB, allegedly resulting from the negligent preparation and dissemination of the Carroll Report. At the time of the preparation of the report, Carroll had been working with Evans, Yeatman & Endall (Associates) Ltd. and its subsidiary, Evans, Yeatman & Endall (Newfoundland) Ltd. At the time of the commencement of the action, however Carroll was working with CSE, which had bought out the assets of the Evans companies on May 31, 1989. While employed by CSE, Carroll had continued to undertake some follow-up work in connection with the report which had been previously prepared by him.

[9]                      The Pettens alleged that a number of statements and recommendations contained in Carroll's report were inaccurate and based on wrong information and that certain governmental agencies, including the Canadian Coast Guard and the Fisheries Loan Board, acted on the report and took steps that effectively resulted in the plaintiffs' shipbuilding enterprise being shut down and the plaintiffs' bank calling their loans, including the Pettens' personal guarantees. It was also alleged that Efford took steps to publish the report and other false and misleading statements about the Pettens and their business that compounded the problem. Carroll and Efford were named as defendants, as was CSE, Carroll's then current employer. The Evans companies, as the former employers of Carroll, were not named as parties. Indeed, it is not clear that the Pettens would have known at that time sufficient of the details of the corporate arrangements which had taken place between the Evans companies and CSE to have appreciated the significance of the changeover. With Carroll as the named defendant, however, the details were not important because they had before the court the person who had been involved throughout, in whatever different employment guise.

[10]                   The same allegations concerning Efford's use of the Carroll report were also made by way of Defence and Counterclaim to the Unseaworthiness Claim, except that they were asserted by ESB, rather than the Pettens personally, and they were made only against Efford.

[11]                   In response to the Business Loss Claim, Efford asserted by way of Counterclaim against the Pettens personally essentially the same claims as he had made against ESB in the Unseaworthiness Claim.

[12]                   The fourth action ([1991]) B. No. 205; hereinafter, the "Administrative Negligence Claim") was launched by the Pettens on August 8, 1991 against the Fisheries Loan Board alleging negligence in the way the Board participated in the provision of information that found its way into the Carroll Report and in its response to that report, which allegedly contributed to the ultimate shutdown of ESB's shipbuilding business.

[13]                   The Business Loss Claim, with its attendant Counterclaim, and the Administrative Negligence Claim were ultimately consolidated and set down for trial. During the course of trial, the Pettens applied for, and were granted, an order consolidating the Delay and Unseaworthiness Claims as well, and adding ESB and Eastern Shipbuilders Enterprises Ltd., (ESEL) another company controlled by the Pettens, as additional plaintiffs.

[14]                   A fifth action was started by Efford alleging amongst other things negligence by the Fisheries Loan Board in its inspection process which allegedly led to the construction of an unseaworthy vessel. This action was not tried with the others and still remains outstanding. It is significant, however, in that it demonstrates the degree of litigation - involving almost every possible combination of plaintiff and defendant - that was spawned by the events surrounding the Shelby Ann. This atmosphere of "litigation fever" in my view affected the actions and positions taken by various of the participants throughout the remainder of the proceedings.

[15]                   As noted, a number of pre-trial applications were made virtually all of which were vigorously contested. At the time of most of those applications, the Pettens and their companies were represented by Douglas Moores, Q.C. Mr. Moores withdrew as counsel about seven months prior to the commencement of trial and was replaced by Mr. Owen Myers, one of the Intervenors in the current application. Mr. Myers represented the plaintiff at trial during the presentation of their case. Thereafter he retired from the case because of ill-health and other reasons. The Pettens represented themselves at the remainder of the trial.

[16]                   The claims in the current application, though made in respect of Mr. Myers' role as solicitor and counsel, and not against Mr. Moores, have nevertheless been advanced in reliance on a number of events that occurred during the period of Mr. Moores' carriage of the case. This is because it is argued that those events, while not implicating Mr. Moores, are nevertheless relevant to an award of costs against the plaintiffs directly. It is necessary, therefore, to examine the details of certain of the applications and other events that occurred before the trial commenced.

(c) Pre-Trial Applications and Related Matters

(i) The First Pre-Trial Application to Strike

[17]                   The statement of claim issued in the Business Loss Claim named Carroll and CSE, as his current employer, as defendants. It alleged, amongst other things, that Efford, another defendant, engaged Carroll and CSE to prepare the Carroll Report which was allegedly negligently made and distributed. No distinction was drawn between Carroll's employment by CSE and his employment by predecessor companies.

[18]                   Three weeks after the commencement of the Business Loss Claim, counsel for CSE and Carroll wrote the solicitors for the Pettens as follows:

"We have contacted the corporate First Defendant [CSE] and we have confirmed that the actions involving the Second Defendant [Carroll] in the matters which are in issue taken by the Second Defendant as an employee of the First Defendant. We have also been informed by the First Defendant that they are willing to confirm this status of the Second Defendant in these proceedings they are willing to provide an assurance to the affect [sic] that should any liability be attributed to the actions of the Second Defendant that the First Defendant would be responsible for such liability. We are in the process of preparing an affidavit providing confirmation of this information and upon the receipt of same we would ask you to please consider dropping the Second Defendant as a party in the action."

[19]                   This letter was followed by another dated January 30, 1991 in which CSE's counsel enclosed a draft of the affidavit previously referred to and repeated his request that Carroll be removed as a party to the action. He also stated that Evans, Yeatman and Endal (Newfoundland) Ltd.:

"... was the company that employed Clarence Carroll up to July or August of 1989 while Mr. Carroll was involved in this matter involving your clients. In the summer of 1989, this company was bought by CSE Marine Services, a division of Canadian Shipbuilding and Engineering Limited and from that time became known as E.Y.E. Marine Consultants a division of CSE Marine Services Inc. We expect therefore that you shall have to amend your pleadings to recite these facts.

"... if you are not inclined to presently cooperate to clarify these matters, please contact the undersigned immediately where upon we shall proceed with the filing of a defence or we shall commence the proceeding in court to apply to have the Second Defendant removed from the record."

[20]                   Again, on March 8, 1991 counsel for CSE and Carroll wrote the solicitors for the Pettens enclosing a copy of the sworn affidavit previously referred to, as well as a copy of the closing agenda for the sale of the assets of Evans, Yeatman and Endal (Associates) Ltd. to CSE, together with related documents, and repeated its previous requests:

"We ask you to please consider these documents with a view to determining if you shall accede to our request to have Clarence Carroll remove [sic] his [sic] party to the action.

"We look forward to hearing from you in this regard and also with respect to the possibility that you may have to amend the statement of claim to accurately reflect the involvement of the First Defendant and its predecessor."

[21]                   The affidavit in question was sworn by Martin Yeatman who described himself as the manager of CSE's division known as "E.Y.E. Marine Consultants" and stating that he had "authority to bind" CSE to contracts, obligations and other legal commitments. In the affidavit, Mr. Yeatman stated that Carroll was and had been an employee of CSE since June of 1989, that prior to June Carroll had been employed by Evans, Yeatman and Endal (Associates) Ltd., that all of the actions referred to in the statement of claim as having been done before June of 1989 were done by him as an employee of that firm, and that all actions attributed to Carroll since June of 1989 were done by him as an employee of CSE. He then made the following acknowledgement:

"6. THAT should liability be found to exist in this action, then any liability attributed to the actions or decisions of the Second Defendant [Carroll] would be acknowledged by the appropriate company as having been done while the Second Defendant carried out his duties as an employee of the respective companies and would result in liability for the appropriate company depending on the timing of the event involved." (Emphasis added)

[22]                   On its face, this amounted to a representation made by an officer of CSE on behalf of CSE that Carroll's corporate employers would accept responsibility for and stand behind the actions of Carroll if he was ultimately held to be negligent. As to which company would assume responsibility, that would depend on who was the employer at the time of any negligent act.

[23]                   Although there were discussions between counsel concerning the request to remove Carroll as a party, the plaintiffs refused the request and, as a result, CSE and Carroll made an interlocutory application before Bartlett, J., on June 10, 1991 requesting that Carroll be removed as a party. The essence of the application was that to the extent to which any actions of Carroll could be said to be negligent, they would have been acts that occurred within the scope of his employment and not in any personal capacity and it was not therefore necessary to have Carroll remain as an individual party. The application referred to the Yeatman affidavit and asserted:

"... all of the actions of the Second Defendant throughout the events of this matter were done as an employee of the First Defendant and its predecessor company. The affidavit provides confirmation of the First Defendant's acceptance of any liability attributed to the Second Defendant resulting from those actions while the Second Defendant acted as an employee of the First Defendant." (Emphasis added)

[24]                   During the argument before Bartlett, J., counsel for CSE and Carroll stated, while referring to the Yeatman affidavit:

"... he [Yeatman] is simply confirming in his affidavit that he is the person that has full authority to speak on behalf of the first defendant [CSE] and that Clarence Carroll, if Clarence Carroll were found to be negligent as a result of the allegations in the statement of claim, that he would be obliged to have the first defendant stand in Clarence Carroll's place for the purpose of paying any damage award or for the purpose of being responsible for any liability because he is confirming there that Carroll participated the whole time as an employee. I think the affidavit very clearly shows that the company would be the responsible party and that he is supporting the application and is representing from the first defendant that he really believes that Clarence Carroll, the second defendant, should not be a party to the application."

[25]                   Counsel for the intervenor Myers argues that this representation to the court amounted to an undertaking on behalf of CSE to stand behind the actions of Carroll and to accept responsibility for any liability for which Carroll might ultimately have been held responsible regardless of when that liability was incurred. He puts this alleged undertaking forward, therefore, as a justification for the plaintiffs' and Myers' steadfast refusal to countenance the possibility of agreeing to subsequent demands by CSE that it also should also be removed as a party on the ground that it only became Carroll's employer after the actions of Carroll giving rise to the alleged liability occurred.

[26]                   The language used by counsel for CSE, as quoted above, is, perhaps, unfortunate. Viewed in isolation it could be said to give the impression that CSE was giving a blanket indemnity for any liability for which Carroll might be ultimately held responsible. One must, however, view those comments in context. The Yeatman affidavit makes it clear that liability for Carroll's actions was being acknowledged "by the appropriate company ... depending on the timing of the event involved". Furthermore, the application itself states the position of CSE that Carroll was acting as an employee, at different times, of CSE "and its predecessor company" and that CSE's representation of acceptance of liability was intended to be in respect of the actions of Carroll while he acted as an employee of CSE. Finally, at other places in the transcript counsel for CSE does in fact refer to the role of the Evans' companies as the employer of Carroll at an earlier point in time. For example, he is also reported as stating:

"... prior to June of 1989 he [Yeatman] was an officer of Evans, Yeatman and Endal Associates Limited who is not a party to this action which employed the second defendant Clarence Carroll up until the company sold its goodwill and physical assets to CSE Marine. Now, very early in this matter when the defendants were served, our law firm contacted the plaintiffs' lawyers and indicated to them that they should amend their statement of claim. We recommended they should amend their statement of claim because, in fact, the third defendant [sic; should read 'second defendant' was involved not with the present first defendant [CSE], he was involved initially with the company called Evans, Yeatman and Endal Associates Limited, and in June of 1989 Evans, Yeatman and Endal Associates Limited became dissolved and transferred its assets to EYE Marine Consultants. So, for the purpose of clarifying the record, we were just recommending to the plaintiff that they - the cause of action should really be against the present first defendant and as well including this prior company, Evans, Yeatman and Endal and Associates Limited ... So to eliminate all doubt, my lord, Clarence Carroll carried on his operations as an employee of both of those companies throughout his time involved in this report which is the subject of this cause of action."

[27]                   After some discussion in the courtroom, counsel for the plaintiffs, in response to questions from the judge, agreed with the application to strike Carroll as a party and stated:

"With Mr. Templeton's comments and with the affidavits as filed, we really have no difficulty with his application."

[28]                   It is clear from this exchange that in conceding the matter, counsel for the plaintiffs was not relying solely upon the alleged blanket indemnity on behalf of CSE but upon the whole of the comments made by counsel for CSE and the affidavit material which was filed. Indeed, shortly following the application, and the removal of Carroll as a party, the plaintiffs amended their statement of claim so as to name the Evans companies as well as CSE as defendants, thereby inferentially recognizing the potential of liability by the Evans' companies, and not CSE, for the acts of Carroll undertaken while their employee. Furthermore, if the plaintiffs were relying on the indemnity argued for, one would have expected that it would have been put forward as any absolute bar to CSE's subsequent non- suit motion and in affidavits filed in opposition to other applications by CSE, as hereinafter described, to be removed as a party. The plaintiffs did not do so.

[29]                   Accordingly, I do not regard the statements of counsel for CSE as amounting to a blanket indemnity, as alleged, for any liability for which Carroll might ultimately be held responsible and as a justification, in itself, for the refusal of the plaintiffs or their counsel to countenance the possible subsequent removal of CSE as a party.

[30]                   Having said that, however, it can be said that what was represented to the court and to the plaintiffs was that there was no need for Carroll, the employee, to remain as a defendant because: (i) CSE would be responsible for any negligence which occurred while Carroll was CSE's employee; and (ii) the Evans companies would be responsible for any negligence which occurred while he was their employee. Yeatman was in a peculiarly appropriate position to give these assurances because he was a former owner of the Evans companies and was a current manager of CSE.

[31]                   There can be no doubt that the existence of these representations (although in a more attenuated form than argued for by counsel for the intervenors) had an effect on the positions taken by the plaintiffs in response to the subsequent efforts by CSE to extricate itself from the litigation.

[32]                   What I find disturbing about this situation is that at the time of the application before Bartlett, J., the Evans companies were no longer operating entities with any assets and were acknowledged by Brian Waring as being "judgment proof." That would have made the representation that the Evans companies would be responsible for Carroll's negligence while he was their employee virtually meaningless.

[33]                   I am not satisfied that this point was sufficiently made to Bartlett, J., or was sufficiently appreciated by the plaintiffs at that time. Yet, counsel for CSE (who was also counsel for the Evans companies and Carroll) was suggesting that the claim would have to be amended to reference the Evans companies as Carroll's employer for the period prior to the purchase by CSE and, indeed as noted, the plaintiffs did subsequently add the Evans companies as defendants. The reality was that as a result of the order, the plaintiffs lost the actual author of the alleged negligence as a party and gained a shell company as a defendant instead. In terms of having anyone of substance to answer for the negligence of Carroll, therefore, it is easy to see how the plaintiffs, upon becoming aware of the status of the Evans companies, would have regarded the continued presence of CSE in the litigation as important.

(ii) Subsequent Pre-trial Applications to Strike

[34]                   Following the filing and service of the plaintiffs' amended statement of claim naming the Evans companies, as well as CSE, as defendants, a joint defence was filed by solicitor Kenneth Templeton on behalf of both Evans companies and CSE. In it, the defendants alleged that the plaintiffs' claims were frivolous and vexatious. In particular, it alleged that CSE could not be held liable for the preparation and dissemination of the Carroll report because the report had been prepared before CSE's involvement by acquisition of the assets of the Evans companies. As well, Evans, Yeatman and Endal (Associates) Ltd. claimed that it could not be liable because it was not carrying on business in Newfoundland at the time of the preparation of the report. The defence concluded that both CSE and Evans (Associates) would apply, prior to trial, to be removed as parties to the action.

[35]                   These were not insignificant statements. Shortly before, representations had been made that Carroll was not necessary as a party because the "appropriate company" would acknowledge responsibility for Carroll's liability "depending on the timing of the event involved." Now, the position was advanced that CSE and one of the Evans companies should also be removed, leaving no real defendant to answer for Carroll. Mr. Petten testified that he became very concerned that CSE was now trying to avoid any involvement. He became suspicious of CSE's motives. Of course, no plaintiff is entitled to have a particular defendant before the court simply because that defendant is one of substance. Nevertheless had the full implications of removing Carroll as a party been appreciated, it may well be that Bartlett, J., would not have been as disposed to granting the application and the plaintiffs may well have not ultimately acceded to the request.

[36]                   As the subsequent discussion at para. indicates, it is not an immutable legal principle that an employee who acts negligently, in the course of his employment, performing the very essence of his employer's contractual obligations, is necessarily able to avoid personal liability. As the matter had developed, therefore, one can appreciate why the plaintiffs would be reluctant to concede the removal of any of the corporate defendants, including CSE, unless there was no possibility of any of them being found liable.

[37]                   Of course, that is the very position CSE was taking. It was arguing that it came on the scene after the negligent act of preparing the report had been completed and that if any corporate defendant was to be held vicariously liable for any negligence of Carroll, it could only be one of the Evans companies no matter how insolvent they might be. The success of that argument depended on there being no connection between CSE and the preparation of the report and no evidence of any other actions by Carroll, after he became an employee of CSE, that could be characterized as negligent. It is clear that CSE and its counsel firmly believed this to be the case; however, it is not CSE's belief that is important, nor is it significant that CSE was ultimately successful in its non-suit motion. What is important for this application is whether the plaintiffs acted appropriately in pursuing the claim and resisting attempts by CSE to remove itself given the plaintiff's knowledge base and their opportunities to satisfy themselves as to the role, if any, which CSE played. Clearly, if the plaintiffs knew or ought reasonably to have concluded that their claim against CSE was baseless, then the inference can be drawn that they must have acted for improper motives or that their behaviour in improperly invoking the judicial system against CSE, is of the type that warrants an order for special costs.

[38]                   Mr. Petten had in his possession a document which, he felt, showed a potential involvement of CSE prior to the purchase of the Evans assets. It consisted of a letter dated November 23, 1989, on CSE letterhead, addressed to the Canadian Coast Guard and signed by Carroll, in which Carroll (who at that time was an employee of CSE) stated that "initially our company became involved with the M/V Shelby Ann December 1988 - January 1989. ..." Mr. Petten believed that this indicated that CSE was involved before the report was written.

[39]                   In addition, there were other documents indicating that after the purchase of the Evans assets, Carroll continued to do work for Mr. Efford respecting the Shelby Ann, including correspondence with the Coast Guard respecting some of the positions taken by the Coast Guard in response to that report. His view of this material, as ultimately explained in argument, was that these actions by Carroll, now on behalf of CSE, involved a republication and reaffirmation of the negligent misstatements in the original report or a negligent failure to correct those statements. Could these peripheral indications of possible CSE involvement withstand scrutiny in light of the detailed information being supplied by CSE indicating that it had in fact purchased the Evans assets on May 31, 1989 and had not been involved previously?

[40]                   In response to the plaintiffs' filing of a request for trial on December 3, 1991, CSE and Associates applied to the court on December 10, 1991 to be removed as parties. A subsequent application made on October 16, 1992 by CSE alone also sought the same relief. The applications were accompanied by affidavits from Martin Yeatman and Brian Waring of CSE in which they asserted that CSE had no involvement prior to its take over of the Evans companies as of the end of May, 1989, when those companies "ceased to exist." Waring deposed that CSE had "absolutely nothing to do" with the preparation of the Carroll report. Attached to the affidavit material were extracts from the Agreement of Purchase and Sale and other related documents designed to verify the assertions that were made.

[41]                   Waring asserted that it was "totally inappropriate" to require CSE to participate in the proceeding since "it was not possible" for Harold Efford to have engaged CSE to prepare the report.

[42]                   Arthur Petten filed an affidavit in response to the application in which he referred to the Carroll letter of November 23, 1989 which implied that CSE had in fact been involved in the preparation of the report prior to its take over of the Evans companies. As well, he highlighted the contrast, as noted above, between the assertions given before Bartlett, J., that responsibility for Carroll's actions would be assumed by the "appropriate company" and the position, now being taken, that CSE should not have to be further involved.

[43]                   An affidavit in response to Petten was filed by Yeatman. In it he asserted that "the impression" left by the November 23 letter that CSE was involved in the preparation of the report was "not accurate" because, as asserted previously, CSE only came on the scene with the asset purchase of May 31, 1989.

[44]                   These applications to strike were dismissed, without written reasons, with costs to the plaintiffs.

(iii) Withdrawal of Counsel

[45]                   On December 3, 1992 Mr. Templeton withdrew as counsel for the Evans companies and was given leave to file a separate defence on behalf of CSE. Shortly thereafter, an application by CSE against the plaintiffs for security for costs of the trial was made but denied. In the course of giving his oral reasons, the Chief Justice of the Trial Division is reported as observing that if the evidence submitted at trial confirmed the submissions of counsel for CSE relative to CSE's non-involvement in the matter, there was a risk that the plaintiffs would be exposed to considerable costs.

[46]                   With a scheduled trial date looming on January 16, 1993, CSE applied, on January 7, 1993, for, and obtained, an order that the plaintiffs answer certain interrogatories which had previously been delivered and that documentary disclosure be made within ten days. At that time the commencement of trial was rescheduled for March 23, 1993.

[47]                   Shortly after the making of the production order, Mr. Moores, plaintiffs' counsel, applied on January 27, 1993, for and obtained an order entitling him to withdraw as counsel. The intervenor Owen Myers deposed that he had been retained by the plaintiffs as their solicitor on January 16, 1993, and that at that point he knew there was an outstanding order requiring the plaintiffs to make document production. He said that he worked for the next six weeks "almost exclusively" in reviewing the mass of documents and exhibits with a view to filing a list of documents in compliance with the previous court order. He succeeded in filing a list of documents on March 4, 1993, the day another application by CSE to have the proceeding dismissed for non-compliance with the production order was to be heard. Myers deposed that in his opinion, "it was not possible to comply with the order of the Chief Justice more expeditiously ... given the condition in which the file documents were received" from previous counsel and the fact that he had become involved in the matter at a late date.

[48]                   As a result of the plaintiffs' change of solicitors and the late document production, as well as other matters, the trial date was again pushed back to September 13, 1993. The trial did in fact start on that date.

(d) Applications During Trial

[49]                   Following Mr. Myers' retention as solicitor for the plaintiffs but before the trial actually started, counsel for CSE repeated his request that the involvement of CSE in the matter be reviewed and that the plaintiffs voluntarily agree to allow CSE to be removed as a party. The plaintiffs would not agree.

[50]                   Subsequently, during trial, the question of CSE's further involvement was dealt with on a number of occasions by way of application or was commented on in the course of other applications.

(i) Application to Strike

[51]                   Twenty-seven days into the trial, the plaintiffs made application to, amongst other things, amend their pleadings to add ESB and ESEL as plaintiffs and to allege additional causes of action, including defamation, injurious falsehood, conspiracy, intimidation, inducement of breach of contract, interference with economic relations and breach of fiduciary duty. CSE took issue with the way in which this application was made, in particular, with the fact that no draft of the proposed amended statement of claim accompanied the application and that the matter had to be delayed to enable the plaintiffs to provide such a draft.

[52]                   CSE responded to the application with a further application to be removed as a party to the proceedings or, in the alternative, for an order for security for costs. These applications were supported by an affidavit from Brian Waring which set out in considerable detail the relationship between the Evans' companies and CSE and the nature of the transaction that involved the transfer of assets to CSE. No cross-examination on this affidavit was made.

[53]                   In a decision reported at (1994), 120 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 313, I made an order adding the Pettens' companies as plaintiffs, and allowed some, but not all, of the requested amendments. In particular, I refused to allow new causes of action based on intimidation, unlawful interference with economic interests, conspiracy, inducement of breach of contract and breach of fiduciary duty. I allowed amendments alleging injurious falsehood, some additional acts of negligence and an allegation that CSE assumed the liabilities of the Evans' companies when the assets were purchased. I also ordered that a composite amended statement of claim be filed in which the plaintiffs were required to state with particularity "which defendants are alleged to be responsible for each cause of action and identify the factual bases upon which the liability of each is alleged to be founded." Although an amended statement of claim was filed, the plaintiffs did not, as required, segregate the causes of action and their factual bases as they applied to each separate defendant. Instead, composite pleas were maintained against CSE and both Evans companies and, with the exception of the new allegation of assumption of Evans liabilities by CSE, the factual allegations, including the preparation of the Carroll report, were equally attributed to CSE.

[54]                   With respect to CSE's claim to be removed as a defendant, I denied that application. I relied on the allowed amendment with respect to the alleged assumption of liabilities as well as on the limited documentary evidence available which suggested that Carroll, whilst an employee of CSE, did spend some time on follow-up on the report and other related matters after the assets were acquired by CSE. I also relied on the letter of November 23, 1989 which on its face (and without further explanation) could be said to suggest that CSE might have had an involvement prior to acquisition of the Evans' assets. I noted, however, that the letter, considered in context, might well be susceptible of a different interpretation but "if so, it would likely have to await the evidence of Carroll who has yet to testify." Finally, I observed that there was a lack of any temporal limitation in the allegations in the statement of claim respecting dissemination of the report upon which counsel for the plaintiff indicated he intended "to argue that there were actions of dissemination on behalf of CSE after June 1, 1989 that involve CSE directly in liability". I concluded that:

"... while the claim against CSE appears to be very tenuous, it cannot be said that it is without hope of success. Accordingly, I conclude that CSE has failed to satisfy me that it should be removed as a party at this stage.

"It may be felt that, because of the very limited (if any) potential connection of CSE to the matters in issue it would be unfair to force CSE to continue to participate in the trial, especially where its prospect of recovery of costs against the plaintiffs, if it were successful, would be minimal. Nevertheless, the plaintiffs are entitled to make claims against any person they choose provided they comply with the rules of pleading and allege a cause of action known to the law. In the circumstances of this case, a balancing of these two competing interests can best be achieved by application of the rules respecting security for costs."

[55]                   Because I felt that "the plaintiffs' claims against CSE as they presently appear seem tenuous at best" I made an order for security for costs in favour of CSE. I observed:

"CSE's position had been made known to the plaintiffs at an early date; yet, as was pointed out by CSE, no effort was made by the plaintiffs to pursue either oral or documentary discovery of CSE to determine whether CSE's position is well founded. The plaintiffs therefore are assuming a significant risk that they will be unsuccessful at the end of trial against CSE. It is, of course, their choice to take such risk. However, it is manifest that the costs of this trial will not be insignificant. It would be not consonant with justice, given the apparent weakness in the plaintiffs' case in this regard to place CSE in the position of having to continue to participate in the trial in these circumstances without some assurance that they will be able to recover at least some of their costs at the end if their predictions of success on this issue are well founded.

"This is especially so where, because of the apparent weakness of the claims, CSE might well be able, if it is successful at the end of trial, to claim extra costs against the plaintiffs pursuant to Rule 14.20 on the basis that the plaintiffs, having been presented with information as to CSE's position at an early date, ought to have admitted those facts and not forced CSE to litigate those issues."

[56]                   The foregoing observations were intended by me as a "wake-up call" to the plaintiffs, with a view to indicating that while they escaped having CSE removed as a party at that stage, the court was certainly going to be looking with care at the evidence to determine whether the plaintiffs could make out a case against CSE at the end of the day. It has been said that an application for security for costs "acts as a deterrent to frivolous litigation and reminds the plaintiff that a lawsuit is not to be undertaken lightly". (See Rochon, Terry & Gray, Interlocutory Proceedings, Strategy and Practice (Toronto: Butterworths, 1996) p. 121) The order for security was in effect a test of the plaintiffs' resolve to proceed against CSE as well as a reminder that lack of success usually attracts costs. It further encouraged them to analyse carefully the case they had specifically against CSE. It also effectively invited the plaintiffs to address the adduction of further evidence tying CSE to the role of Carroll in preparation and dissemination of the report.

(ii) Delays in Provision of Security for Costs

[57]                   Counsel for the plaintiffs and CSE engaged in some discussions as to the possible variation of the form which the security of costs which I ordered could be made. Because the deadline for provision of the security passed, an additional application was made to deal with the question of the form of security and whether an extension of time ought to be granted for its provision. I ruled, in a decision reported at (1994), 121 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 338 that it would be fair to extend the time for provision of security because of the discussions which had been ongoing relative to the possibility of providing alternative forms of security due to the apparent difficulty which the plaintiffs were having in arranging security in the form originally envisaged. On the issue of what was an appropriate form of security, the plaintiffs ultimately provided a cash deposit with the court even though it was they who had initiated the discussions respecting the provision of alternative security. CSE was awarded costs with respect to these matters.

(iii) Application for Further Security

[58]                   Following the failure of the plaintiffs to satisfy the order for payment of costs, CSE made a further application later in the trial for additional security. A hearing was held with respect to this matter in which the financial and other information provided by the plaintiffs and the manner in which they dealt with the application was far from satisfactory. Negotiations had also been undertaken between the parties with respect to the provision of additional security by consent but ultimately those negotiations failed because Mrs. Petten disagreed. In yet another decision, reported at (1995), 130 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 205, I issued a restraining order preventing the disposition by the Pettens of certain property owned by them pending resolution of trial issues involving CSE.

(iv) The Allegation of an Undertaking to Call Witnesses

[59]                   At the conclusion of the plaintiffs' case, CSE made its non-suit motion. In response, Mr. Myers asserted, amongst other things, that a non-suit motion was inappropriate because counsel for CSE had undertaken to call Brian Waring as a witness at the trial so that the plaintiffs would have an opportunity to cross- examine him.

[60]                   Counsel for CSE took strong exception to this suggestion, regarding it as an unfounded imputation on his professional character. After some discussion, during which it became clear that the plaintiffs may have made certain assumptions based on earlier discussions between counsel respecting the availability of witnesses for certain purposes relating to earlier applications, and statements made early in the trial about contemplated witnesses, Mr. Myers, quite properly and professionally, withdrew the suggestion that an undertaking had been given.

[61]                   Although the suggestion of an undertaking was withdrawn and the plaintiff proceeded to deal with the non-suit motion without the benefit of Mr. Waring's evidence, the occurrence of this incident has another dimension. It demonstrates that the plaintiffs were interested in exploring with Mr. Waring, by way of examination in court, the involvement of CSE in the preparation and dissemination of the Carroll report both before and after the acquisition of the Evans assets. I am satisfied that, though the existence of an undertaking was not established, the plaintiffs honestly, though mistakenly, believed that they would, at some point have an opportunity to question Mr. Waring during the trial process.

[62]                   As matters turned out their assumption in that regard was mistaken, and, inasmuch as they had not called Mr. Waring as their own witness they were, as a matter of tactics, deprived of that evidence. Of course, Mr. Waring's evidence may or may not ultimately have been of assistance but one cannot speculate on what impression may have been presented following cross-examination and the possible eliciting of new information. Nevertheless, a belief in the need to cross- examine Mr. Waring suggests a bona fide intention to attempt to build on the information that the plaintiffs had by further linking CSE, through a cross- examination of Mr. Waring, to the case.

(v) The Non-Suit Application

[63]                   The non-suit application proceeded and, as I have already indicated, it was successful. I concluded that the "uncontradicted evidence" from the plaintiffs' witnesses and the consent exhibits was that CSE purchased the assets of the Evans' companies on May 31, 1989 and that there was "no evidence" that prior to that date Carroll had any employment connection with CSE. The evidence and argument relied upon by the plaintiffs in support of the argument of an involvement by CSE in the preparation of the Carroll report prior to acquisition of the evidence assets was essentially the same material that the plaintiffs pointed to and relied upon in resisting the previous applications for dismissal as a party. In essence, the plaintiffs continued to rely on the letter of November 23, 1989 as indicating prior involvement by CSE. By the time of the non-suit application, however, Carroll had testified and given an explanation, which was uncontradicted, that his language was poorly chosen and was not in fact intended to suggest that CSE had any connection with the preparation of the report; rather, what Carroll was trying to convey was the notion that he, as an employee of different companies (but under the same trade name), had had a continuous involvement. I ruled that, considered in the context of all the other evidence, no reasonable trier of fact properly instructed could conclude that the letter could support the inference that CSE was involved in the preparation of the report and that, accordingly, it did not meet the test of probative sufficiency (described in Campbell v. Campbell (No. 1) (1989), 83 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 332; 260 A.P.R. 332 (U.F.C.)), necessary to resist a non-suit application.

[64]                   I further held that any involvement of Carroll, as an employee of CSE, in the subsequent dealings with his report could not, as a matter of law, amount to negligence, negligent mis-statement or injurious falsehood as alleged in the statement of claim and that there was no evidence that any of such activities led to the actions of the Coast Guard and Fisheries Loan Board in the making of their decisions which were detrimental to the Shelby Ann and the plaintiffs' business. Finally, I concluded that there was no evidence from which a reasonable trier of fact could infer liability on the part of CSE on the basis of either the voluntary assumption by CSE of prior Evans liabilities for the preparation of the Carroll report or the imposition, by law, of such liabilities.

[65]                   In short, I concluded that there was nothing presented by way of evidence called by the plaintiffs to advance their case to the level of probative sufficiency necessary to resist the non-suit application.

Analysis

[66]                   Based upon events which occurred, both pre-trial and during trial, CSE claims entitlement to costs on a solicitor and own client basis both against the plaintiffs and against their trial counsel, Owen Myers, personally. Regardless of what costs order is made, they further request an order entitling them to realize upon the security which had previously been given for payment of their costs.

[67]                   In support of his submission that costs on a solicitor and own client basis ought to be awarded, counsel for CSE cites the following events of allegedly contumacious conduct on the part of the plaintiffs and their counsel:

1. Insisting upon pursuing a claim without merit against Clarence Carroll personally.

2. Pursuing a claim without merit against CSE while pleading unprovable allegations involving alleged intentional, malicious and conspiratorial actions on the part of CSE, exacerbated by the failure to investigate and consider fully the information supplied by CSE as to its alleged non- involvement in the events on which the litigation was based.

3. Failing, prior to litigation, to provide information to Carroll that could have eliminated errors in the report ultimately prepared by him.

4. Making an improper allegation against counsel for CSE that he had given an undertaking to make a witness available for trial when no such undertaking had been given.

5. Repeatedly failing to comply with court orders.

6. Failing to carry out trial procedures "in an effective manner" by using the trial process to discover the evidence of many witnesses, thereby prolonging the involvement of CSE in the trial.

(I have restated and reorganized the description of the matters listed by counsel for CSE in his submission by telescoping several into one (item 2); however, the essence of the submission remains the same.)

[68]                   Mr. Waring, in his affidavit in support of the application for costs characterized the actions and behaviour of the plaintiffs and their counsel in the following way:

"This company has been put through a very costly process in defending the action that had no merit against us. I believe the proceedings were motivated by mean spirited plaintiffs that had lost their business and who were only interested in making life miserable for anyone who had been involved with the F.V. Shelby Ann whether or not a cause of action existed." He described the actions of the plaintiffs and Mr. Myers as "reprehensible" and concluded:

"... this company has been seriously wronged by these events and I believe this action should stand as an example for those intent upon proceeding with litigation that they would be wise to first cautiously assess the defences disclosing other positions."

[69]                   Counsel for CSE reinforced this view with his submission that the actions concerned were:

"... oppressive, reckless, reprehensible; and in fact disgraceful, to drag a company through prolonged litigation on allegations that the company engaged in activity before it even existed. Furthermore it is particularly reckless when counsel has been informed at an early stage, months before trial, that it was physically and legally impossible for such liability to have occurred and no attempt whatsoever was made to verify the information. The plea was completely ignored and the action pushed on to trial."

Although counsel for CSE stated at the hearing that he was not alleging bad faith, the foregoing statements, by his client and him, if not implying bad faith, come very close. In fact, his submission was marked by hyperbole, some of which in the view I have taken of this matter, cannot be considered as an accurate characterization of what occurred.

[70]                   Unfortunately, counsel for CSE, in his legal submissions respecting the principles applicable to the awarding of solicitor and client costs, did not clearly differentiate between situations where such costs should be awarded against a party as opposed to against the solicitor of that party. It is fundamental, in my view, that these two situations be considered separately.

(a) Costs against the Plaintiffs

[71]                   As a general rule, costs follow the event. See Rule 55.03(1) and Sooley v. Canadian Home Insurance Co. (1994), 115 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 47; 360 A.P.R. 47 (Nfld. C.A.), per Morgan, J.A., at paragraph 13.

[72]                   Accordingly, CSE should be entitled, at the very least, to costs on a party and party basis for the whole of that portion of the trial in which they participated and in respect of all pre-trial applications in which CSE participated and in which costs were ordered in the cause.

[73]                   The question is whether, in the circumstances of this case, something further should be ordered.

[74]                   The underlying rationale for awarding costs in the ordinary course of things is one of indemnification. The awarding of party and party costs, of course, represents only a partial indemnity. Nevertheless, such an award is generally regarded as striking the proper balance between the burden of costs which must be borne by a potentially successful litigant and the risk of putting litigation beyond the financial reach of a potential loser. In other words, party and party costs represent the balance that is generally struck between the cost of justice and access to justice. For complete indemnity to be justified, therefore, it must appear that justice (taking into account the balancing of cost versus access) demands that result. In our system of justice, that would be the exceptional case.

[75]                   The starting point for consideration of this issue is the decision of the Supreme Court of Canada in Young v. Young et al., [1993] 4 S.C.R. 3; 160 N.R. 1; 34 B.C.A.C. 161; 56 W.A.C. 161; [1993] 8 W.W.R. 513; 108 D.L.R.(4th) 193; 84 B.C.L.R.(2d) 1; 18 C.R.R.(2d) 41; 49 R.F.L.(3d) 117, in which McLachlin, J., speaking for the majority, described the circumstances under which solicitor and client costs should be awarded against a party to litigation, in the following terms at p. 283:

"Solicitor-client costs are generally awarded only where there has been reprehensible, scandalous or outrageous conduct on the part of one of the parties. Accordingly, the fact that an application has little merit is no basis for awarding solicitorclient costs ...

The court affirmed the British Columbia Court of Appeal's ruling that the trial judge was in error in awarding solicitor-client costs except for four days of trial and four days of interlocutory proceedings concerning financial issues on the basis of the husband's nondisclosure of financial information. The court specifically rejected the notion that solicitorclient costs should be awarded solely on the basis of the fact that a claim had "little merit".

[76]                   The foregoing statement of principle in Young has been adopted and applied on numerous occasions by courts in this jurisdiction: House of Haynes (Restaurant) Ltd. et al. v. Snook et al. (1995), 134 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 23; 417 A.P.R. 23 (Nfld. C.A.); Canadian Paraplegic Association (Newfoundland and Labrador) Inc. v. Sparcott Engineering Ltd. et al. (1997), 150 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 203; 470 A.P.R. 203 (Nfld. C.A.); Perry v. Heywood et al. (1997), 175 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 253; 537 A.P.R. 253 (Nfld. C.A.); Rose (T.) Construction Ltd. v. Omex International Inc. et al. (1994), 122 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 280; 379 A.P.R. 280 (Nfld. T.D.) and Earle v. Christian Brothers of Ireland in Canada et al. (1995), 136 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 52; 423 A.P.R. 52 (Nfld. T.D.). Other cases, while not expressly referring to Young, affirm the same general approach: Charcoal Pit Ltd. v. Atlantic Insurance Co. et al. (1994), 123 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 255; 382 A.P.R. 255 (Nfld. C.A.); Janes v. Deer Lake (Town) (1993), 110 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 202; 346 A.P.R. 202 (Nfld. C.A.); and Sooley.

[77]                   In determining whether or not the conduct of a party has been reprehensible, scandalous or outrageous so as to merit an award of solicitor-client costs, it is the actions, not only of the party himself, but those of his or her solicitor and counsel which must be examined. Unless it can be said that the party's legal representative is acting without authority or wholly outside the scope of his retainer, the lawyer's conduct will be treated as the conduct of the party: House of Haynes; Canadian Paraplegic Association. (That is not to say, of course, that both client and lawyer cannot be jointly liable for solicitor-client costs where the lawyer's actions, even though taken with the approval or on the instructions of the client, merit reproof in their own right).

[78]                   The reprehensible, scandalous or outrageous conduct that merits an award of solicitor-client costs must occur either: (i) in the circumstances giving rise to the cause of action; or (ii) in the proceedings themselves. See Earle, per Mercer, J., at p. 56, which has recently been approved by the Court of Appeal in Heywood, per Cameron, J.A., at paragraph 63.

[79]                   What does reprehensible, scandalous or outrageous conduct encompass? In Heywood, Cameron, J.A., agreed with statements in other cases that "reprehensible" was "a word of wide meaning" and that:

"It can include conduct which is scandalous, outrageous or constitutes misbehaviour, but it also included milder forms of misconduct. It means simply 'deserving of reproof or rebuke.'"

Cameron, J.A., also referred with approval, however, to the comments of Marshall, J.A., in House of Haynes to the effect that awards of solicitor and client costs are generally reserved "to sanction misconduct by a party" and represent an expression of censure or chastisement and "will engender a certain amount of opprobrium". Reservation of awards of solicitor-client costs for such cases promotes the policy of not unduly discouraging access to the courts by allowing allegations to be made by way of pleading or argument so long as they are not clearly frivolous, scandalous or an abuse of process.

[80]                   Thus, while the effect of an award of solicitor-client costs is to provide a greater degree of indemnification than that provided by party and party costs (thereby serving a compensatory function), the justification for affording that greater degree of cost protection is the misconduct of the other party which is deserving of reproof or rebuke. The awarding of solicitor-client costs may therefore be justified where there is a causal relationship between the misconduct deserving reproof or rebuke and the special costs incurred for which additional indemnification is sought. For example, in Young the actions of the litigant against whom costs were awarded only attracted solicitor-client costs related to the four-day period by which the litigant's obstructive behaviour extended the proceedings and thereby exposed the other party to increased cost. There will also be cases, however, where solicitor-client costs may be awarded, based on conduct deserving reproof or rebuke, which nevertheless cannot be linked directly to any increase in cost or other harm suffered by another party. The court always retains an inherent jurisdiction to express its disapproval of the conduct of a party in litigation by the awarding or withholding of costs. The awarding of solicitor- client costs thus serves both a compensatory and a punitive function.

[81]                   In Heywood, Cameron, J.A., referred to a number of circumstances in which awards of solicitor-client costs might be considered appropriate:

-  Taking action in bad faith by making serious but unfounded allegations of wrongdoing such as fraud or arson.

-  Committing a fraud on the court by deceptive conduct or perjury.

-  Engaging in obstructionist tactics or consuming trial time on unnecessary or irrelevant issues to exhaust the opponent's resources.

-  Engaging in conduct that could be characterized as oppressive, as in the case of actions towards a minority shareholder in a corporation.

[82]                   Examples from other Newfoundland cases include:

-  not acting in good faith, by advancing a claim which had no factual basis, for the improper purpose of using the claim as a threat to obtain payments to which the plaintiff was not entitled: Bixby v. B & K Carpet Warehouse Co. (1987), 63 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 232; 144 A.P.R. 232 (Nfld. T.D.)

-  failing to disclose material information to the court on an ex parteapplication, thereby breaching one's obligation of candour to the court: Canadian Paraplegic Association

[83]                   By contrast, solicitor-client costs were denied in the following situations:

- suing an insurance company, even though it was not a party to the insurance contract in issue, where to do so was "understandable" out of "an abundance of caution": Sooley

- delaying the setting down of a case for trial where the plaintiff had "a legitimate reason" for its delay: Charcoal Pit

- delay by an administrative agency in issuing an unconditional licence necessitating litigation to require its issuance, where the delay in the circumstances could not be characterized as an abuse of process or scandalous or vexatious conduct constituting harassment: Wishingwell Early Childhood Centre Ltd. v. Daycare and Homemakers Service Licening Board (Nfld.) (1992), 99 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 336; 315 A.P.R. 336 (Nfld. T.D.) - "vigorously" defending and resisting a claim up to the date of trial and then choosing not to participate in the trial itself, and withdrawing one's defence, where there was no evidence to suggest that the actions were designed to obstruct or injure the claimant: Rose (T.) Construction

- pursuing a claim to which the plaintiff had no entitlement in law in the face of a defence which clearly identified the basis of the plaintiff's lack of entitlement but where the lack of entitlement "was not immediately apparent from the pleadings until demonstrated at trial" and where the claimant was acting for himself: Janes

[84]                   A comparison between cases which have awarded solicitor-client costs and those which have declined to do so reveals that not all types of behaviour constituting misconduct will necessarily attract such a costs award. The misconduct must be such that it is serious enough to injuriously affect the other party or is so flagrant that it merits the court's censure for that reason alone.

[85]                   In many cases, the claim for an award of solicitor-client costs arises where it is subsequently determined that a claim or defence in fact had no merit. The winning party then claims either that a more careful reading of the situation by the loser would not have provided a justification for forcing the other person to incur the expense of a full trial, or that the loser knew that his case had no merit but was nevertheless standing firm for some ulterior or improper motive. There are elements of those sorts of allegations in this case.

[86]                   No doubt, as Marshall, J. A., observed in Janes at p. 212, the jurisdiction to award solicitor-client costs may be invoked "to dissuade harassment of a party by the pursuit of fruitless actions". Such a policy also extends to a defendant who baselessly defends a legitimate claim. See Charcoal Pit, per Gushue, J.A., at p. 258 where he equated "misconduct" by a party, for the purposes of the rule to a situation "where a party has ... defended an action on an obviously frivolous or groundless basis or has acted on improper motives". Balanced against these statements, however, is the statement by McLachlin, J., in Young, previously quoted, that the fact that an application "has little merit" is no basis for awarding solicitor-client costs.

[87]                   The analysis of this issue must be undertaken from the point of view, reasonably assessed, of the party who is potentially subject to the award, and not from the judge's point of view with the benefit of hindsight after having heard the case. To do otherwise would expose potential litigants to a significant risk of costs, dissuade the development of the law by the submission of novel claims and discourage or impede litigants' access to the courts. The deliberate, or even possibly the reckless, pursuit of a claim known or believed to be unfounded will often be good evidence of having taken the action out of malice or for other improper motive and thereby constitute an abuse of process which would fall within the notion of reprehensible, scandalous or outrageous conduct. The importance of examining whether a claim is in fact unfounded is with respect to the inferences which may be drawn concerning the motivation of the claimant. It is for this reason that when examining the question of whether a claim is unfounded the focus must be on the knowledge base of the claimant and his solicitor rather than on the basis of hindsight. The correct approach is exemplified by the following comment of Hardinge, J., in Shedwill v. Wilson (1991), 48 C.P.C.(2d) 70 (B.C.S.C.) at p. 73:

"With the benefit of hindsight I have decided the allegation was unfounded. However, looking at the matter from the point of view of the plaintiffs when they commenced their action and even at trial, I am not prepared to go so far as to say the allegation was obviously unfounded, made recklessly or out of malice."

[88]                   The distinction must be drawn, when viewing cases from the point of view of the claimant, between those which have "little merit" which Young tells us does not form a basis for awarding solicitor-client costs, and those which have obviously no merit. This distinction explains comments in some of the Newfoundland Court of Appeal cases which assert that solicitor-client costs are appropriate where a party has commenced or defended an action "on an obviously frivolous or groundless basis" (per Gushue, J., A. in Charcoal Pit at p. 258) and where there is a "deliberate advancing of a frivolous claim" which would normally require, additionally, "fraud or other malicious, wanton or scandalous conduct" (per Marshall, J.A., in Janes at p. 212). The advancing of a weak case or one which becomes clear, only with the benefit of hindsight, that it was groundless, will not be sufficient. Sooley is an example of a case of that type. There, the taking of proceedings which might ultimately be determined to be groundless against an insurance company even though it was not a party to the particular insurance contract in question was held not to justify an award of solicitor-client costs because the action was taken out of an understandable abundance of caution.

[89]                   I will now turn to the consideration of the specific examples of alleged improper conduct (itemized in paragraph [67]) advanced by CSE in support of its claim for solicitor-client costs.

- Pursuing an alleged non-meritorious claim against Clarence Carroll

[90]                   The suggestion put forward by CSE's counsel is that it was wholly inappropriate for the plaintiffs to have sued Mr. Carroll in his personal capacity where there was no suggestion that his work with respect to the preparation of the report was done otherwise than in the course of his employment with either the Evans companies or CSE.

[91]                   Reliance on this circumstance as a basis for a claim by CSE for solicitor- client costs is not supportable. An application was made on behalf of Carroll by counsel, who at the time was also counsel for CSE, to have Carroll removed as a party. That order was granted and Carroll was awarded his costs. It is Carroll, if anyone, who could have made a claim, at the time of his removal as a party, for solicitor-client costs. Except insofar as the naming of Carroll as a party can be said in a general sense to be indicative of a "mind set" or improper approach by the plaintiffs to the litigation generally, including the claims against CSE, the fact that Carroll was ultimately removed as a party is not in itself a ground for an award of solicitorclient costs in favour of CSE.

[92]                   In any event, the making of a claim against Carroll did not, in the circumstances, amount to the assertion of a claim that was, to the knowledge of the plaintiffs or their counsel, wholly unsupportable. Until the inter-relationship between the two Evans companies and CSE became better known, it would not have been inappropriate to name Carroll as a party as being the person who actually allegedly negligently prepared and disseminated the report. Furthermore, as the discussion of this issue in London Drugs Ltd. v. Brassart and Vanwinkel, [1992] 3 S.C.R. 299; 143 N.R. 1; 18 B.C.A.C. 1; 31 W.A.C. 1; 97 D.L.R.(4th) 261 and Edgeworth Construction Ltd. v. Lea (N.D.) and Associates Ltd. et al., [1993] 3 S.C.R. 206; 157 N.R. 241; 32 B.C.A.C. 221; 53 W.A.C. 221, makes clear, it does not necessarily follow that an employee acting negligently but in the course of his employment and performing the "very essence" of his or her employer's contractual obligations to a customer, is always immune from personal liability. The particular facts of each case are relevant to that determination.

[93]                   It cannot be said in this case that, even if the pursuit of the claim against Carroll was relevant to the entitlement of CSE to solicitor-client costs, it was so plain and obvious that the claim being pressed was a frivolous one and was being advanced for a malicious or other improper motive so as to count as reprehensible, scandalous or outrageous conduct.

- Pursuing an alleged non-meritorious claim against CSE

[94]                   The success of CSE's non-suit motion in a sense vindicated its claim that it was not liable to the plaintiffs. My decision confirmed that CSE could not, as a matter of law, be held liable on the basis of assumption of liabilities previously incurred by the Evans companies and also confirmed that there was no evidence meeting the test of probative sufficiency that could link CSE to the negligence of Carroll in the preparation and distribution of his report. However, it does not follow from these findings that an award for solicitor-client costs is appropriate. The fact that the claim was ultimately found to have had no merit (in the sense of not meeting the test of probative sufficiency) does not mean that its pursuit necessarily amounts to reprehensible, scandalous or outrageous conduct. At the time of the issuance of the relevant statement of claim, the plaintiffs could not necessarily be expected to have known of the precise interrelationship of the proposed corporate defendants or of the essentially private commercial transaction whereby the Evans' assets were transferred to CSE or of the specific date and effect of such transfer. The initial suit against CSE could not therefore be faulted.

[95]                   CSE argues, however, that subsequent events should clearly have brought home to the plaintiffs or their counsel the fact that CSE could not be found liable or, if they could not be said to have known of that circumstance, their failure to examine the information provided by CSE amounted to a reckless closing of one's eyes to the reality of the situation, thereby leading to the inference that they must have been motivated by improper purposes to press on against CSE. There can be no doubt that from an early date counsel for CSE brought to the attention of the plaintiffs, through their then counsel, information concerning the nature of the relationship between the Evans companies and CSE and disclosed circumstances concerning the transfers between those corporate bodies. This disclosure of information was accompanied by requests to reconsider the necessity of keeping CSE as a party. CSE's position was reiterated in its defence. Applications were made to have CSE removed. At that time, further information and explanation were provided.

[96]                   There is no evidence to suggest that this information was considered, analysed and investigated in any concerted way. At most, it can be said that Mr. Petten remained steadfast in his belief, based upon certain incidental references in letters and upon Carroll's continuing involvement in subsequent discussions of the report while an employee of CSE.

[97]                   As noted, CSE made pre-trial applications to be struck as a party, as well as a pre-trial application for security for costs. In that latter context, the presiding applications judge, while denying the application, made some incidental comments suggesting weaknesses in the evidentiary base for the claim against CSE. It is argued that the actions of CSE in seeking to be struck and in its openness with respect to disclosure of the circumstances surrounding the take over of the Evans' assets by CSE, as well as the obiter judicial comments in court, should have signalled to the plaintiffs that the involvement of CSE in this matter was going to continue to be a serious issue and that, accordingly, the plaintiffs came under a duty to investigate the matter with care to satisfy themselves that they were not mistaken about the involvement of CSE and were not in fact pursuing a claim which in fact had no merit.

[98]                   Against this, however, one must consider the fact that in opposing CSE's applications to strike, Mr. Petten recited, in his affidavit material, some of the documentary evidence which he said supported his belief that CSE was in fact involved and, indeed, the court after hearing submissions, did not accede to CSE's request to strike. Furthermore, even though the judge reportedly made comments reflecting on the lack of strength of that evidence, he was not prepared to make an order for security for costs which, as noted previously, can sometimes be used as a signpost to a plaintiff that the law suit should not be undertaken lightly and that it is in danger of being considered to be frivolous. The success of the plaintiffs in these matters would no doubt have bolstered their belief that it was appropriate to continue to proceed against CSE.

[99]                   When the matter was once again raised before me during the course of the plaintiffs' case at trial, I again refused to strike out CSE as a defendant. Nevertheless, my comments at that time, to the effect that on the information then existing the claims against CSE were "tenuous at best" and that the plaintiffs were "assuming a significant risk that they will be unsuccessful at the end of trial against CSE", could not be taken other than as a blunt reminder that something further would likely have to be presented to the court for the plaintiffs to have a realistic chance of success against CSE. When those comments are coupled with the order which I made that the plaintiffs should particularize in their pleadings the factual bases upon which the liability of CSE, as separate from that of the Evans companies, was alleged to be founded, and the fact that I ordered security for costs specifically on the basis of the apparent weakness of the plaintiffs' case against CSE, the argument of CSE that the plaintiffs must have realized that their position against CSE had to be addressed becomes more compelling.

[100]               Furthermore, the amendment which I allowed the plaintiffs to make to their pleading, to allege that CSE assumed the liabilities of the Evans companies, would also have brought home to the plaintiffs that they would have to provide to the court some basis in the evidence or at law for concluding that that alleged assumption took place.

[101]               Notwithstanding these matters, it can be said that for the remainder of the plaintiffs' case, there was virtually no evidence presented which could be said to impact further on the issues between the plaintiff and CSE. Indeed, Carroll's evidence effectively eliminated potential reliance by the plaintiffs on the November 23 letter as a realistic basis for tying CSE into the original preparation of the report.

[102]               Can the failure on the part of the plaintiffs to present any further evidence, in the face of the previous comments on the apparent weaknesses in the existing evidence, while at the same time requiring CSE to remain as a defendant until the end of the plaintiffs' case, be said to amount to reprehensible, scandalous or outrageous conduct justifying solicitor-client costs?

[103]               As noted previously, the fact that I concluded that the plaintiffs should be nonsuited against CSE does not, in itself, equate to a conclusion that the plaintiffs should have been expected to cease proceeding against CSE at an earlier stage. One must approach the matter from the point of view of the reasonableness of the beliefs of the plaintiffs, given their knowledge base. Though a slender connecting thread, the plaintiffs had the statements in the November 23 letter to rely on. Ought they to have been expected to give up reliance on that in the light of the information provided by CSE as to the date when CSE in fact took over the Evans companies? It must be remembered that the statements in the letter were authored by Carroll at a time when he was an employee of CSE. They were, therefore, CSE's statements. In contentious litigation where tactics are important, it is not unreasonable for parties to be cautious before giving up a claim where there is a possibility that an opposing position - especially one stated by the other side - exists. This is especially so where the consequences might be significant. Having had the author of the report removed as a potential defendant and having been presented with the knowledge that the Evans companies were judgment proof, the consequence of CSE being removed as a defendant meant that the plaintiffs might face the prospect of a finding of negligence with no person to answer for it.

[104]               From the perspective of the plaintiffs, one can understand that, given the events that occurred pre-trial, they might be cautious and even mistrustful of CSE's motives in fighting so hard to be removed, given: (i) the previous undertaking given to Bartlett, J., that CSE would accept responsibility for Carroll's actions while an employee of CSE, ie. after June 1, 1989. (It is not without significance that the plaintiffs were also arguing that the actions of Carroll after June 1, 1989 reaffirmed his previous negligent misstatements and involved CSE in liability of that score as well); and (ii) the representations made to Bartlett, J., by Yeatman and counsel for CSE that the Evans companies would stand behind Carroll's actions prior to June 1,1989. The result of CSE's subsequent procedural manoeuvres was that CSE effectively sought, knowingly, to have only an insolvent company stand behind Carroll. The reaction of the plaintiffs was a reaction to positions taken by CSE.

[105]               Furthermore, the evidence of Carroll's involvement in discussions and investigations flowing from his report, after he became a CSE employee, must also be considered. The plaintiffs argued that this amounted to either separate acts of negligence or a reaffirmation of the previous negligence for which CSE could be vicariously liable. As indicated, I did not accept that characterization of that evidence; however, I cannot say that the plaintiffs' view was not a reasonably-held possible alternative interpretation to be placed on Carroll's actions. The fact that the evidence, given my characterization of its significance, did not meet the test of probative sufficiency in the non-suit motion does not mean that the plaintiffs were not justified in relying on it for the purpose of pressing on with their claim without having the inference drawn that they were abusing the process of the court, acting for improper motives or otherwise engaging in reprehensible, scandalous or outrageous conduct.

[106]               I do not accept, as suggested by Mr. Waring that the plaintiffs were "mean-spirited ... who were only interested in making life miserable for anyone who had been involved with the F.V. Shelby Ann whether or not a cause of action existed."; rather, they were acting out of an abundance of caution in complicated litigation that involved a complex web of interrelationships and evidence which, depending on how they or it were viewed by the trier of fact, might or might not have involved CSE in liability. It was not a case, as argued by CSE, of it being "impossible" for CSE to be held liable. The actions of Carroll in continuing to deal with the report after joining CSE opened the theoretical possibility (ultimately determined by me on the non-suit motion to be groundless) that CSE could be held liable on the basis of those actions. In stressing the "impossibility" of CSE being held liable because of its non-involvement prior to the take-over of the Evans companies, CSE appears to overlook, or at least unnecessarily downplay, the role of Carroll as an employee of CSE after June 1, 1989.

[107]               To require the plaintiffs to have taken a leap of faith and concluded, in advance of a ruling by the court, that there was no possibility of success against CSE would in the circumstances of this case be to place the threshold for awarding solicitor-client costs too low. It would require the plaintiffs to judge the evidence. True, the plaintiffs took a big risk of proceeding with a weak case against CSE, and lost. For that, they should pay, just as any losing litigant should, but that in itself does not justify an order for payment on the basis of solicitor-client costs. Even if the evidence of Carroll eliminated the possibility of the November 23 letter being relied on to connect CSE with the original preparation of the report, the subsequent actions of Carroll also provided a possible basis for the plaintiffs to argue for liability to be imposed on CSE. In light of that, no matter what degree of analysis (and acceptance) by the plaintiffs of the information provided by CSE as to the date and circumstances surrounding the take over, one could not necessarily expect that reasonable persons in the position of the plaintiffs would or should withdraw their claim entire-

[108]               In all the circumstances, even if it could be said that the plaintiff did not seriously consider the material submitted by CSE and that that failure constituted misconduct, it could not be said to amount to reprehensible, scandalous or outrageous conduct, nor is it the type of behaviour deserving of such reproof or rebuke as to attract an award of solicitor-client costs.

- Failing, prior to commencement of litigation. to provide relevant information to Carroll that could have affected the preparation of his report.

[109]               This submission is based upon evidence at trial that Mr. Petten contacted Mr. Carroll and told him that he did not have accurate plans of the vessel but nevertheless refused to provide those plans to him. In my decision on liability, I described these events as follows:

"[160] Mr. Petten became aware that Mr. Carroll was carrying out some sort of study of the vessel and contacted him to advise him that he did not have the most up-to-date sets of plans and that any work that Mr. Carroll did would therefore be inaccurate. Mr. Efford had previously instructed Mr. Carroll not to communicate with Mr. Petten. By this time Mr. Petten was also very defensive. Mr. Carroll said he asked Mr. Petten for the drawings and was told they were ESB's property and he would see them in court. As a result, all of the information from the builder's perspective including the 'hands on' knowledge of what was done during construction of the Shelby Ann, was not made available to Mr. Carroll."

[110]               While it is true that, as Heywood demonstrates, the pre-litigation activities of a party may form the basis of an award of solicitor-client costs, the circumstances of the present case do not provide that justification.

[111]               It is too simplisticto argue in effect, that in the circumstances of this case, Mr. Petten was the author of his own misfortune and that if he had disclosed to Mr. Carroll the most recent plans for the vessel the litigation would not have been necessary. My findings with respect to the deficiencies in the Carroll report were based not only on the absence of accurate plans but on other factors as well. Furthermore, I also noted that Carroll could have, but did not, properly qualify his opinions based upon the gaps in his knowledge base.

[112]               In any event, by the time Carroll was commissioned to prepare his report, there was very definitely an adversarial atmosphere existing. As I noted in my decision with respect to liability of the other parties, it was understandable for Mr. and Mrs. Petten to be suspicious and defensive given their exclusion from some of the previous discussions and investigations concerning the "problem" with the Shelby Ann. I agree with the argument of Mr. Myers' counsel that the fact that plaintiffs do not cooperate with a party adverse in interest relating to pending litigation should not generally attract solicitor-client costs. Furthermore, Mr. Carroll had been instructed by Harold Efford not to talk to Mr. Petten. Certainly, in the circumstances of this case one can understand the reluctance of Mr. Petten to cooperate where, if he did provide information to Carroll, the conversation would be only one-way and where his experience to date had been one of having to face accusation after accusation by others and having other information used against him. While Mr. Petten's actions could, in hindsight, perhaps be described as ill-advised, I cannot agree with CSE's counsel's characterization of these actions as "reprehensible" or "scandalous".

- Making an improper allegation against counsel for CSE respecting an undertaking that had not been given.

[113]               Counsel for CSE characterizes as "embarrassing disrespectful tactics" the assertion made by counsel for the plaintiffs at the close of the plaintiffs' case that counsel for CSE had given an undertaking to have Brian Waring testify at the trial when, in fact, no such undertaking had been given.

[114]               While on the face of it this incident should perhaps be more properly dealt with in connection with the claim for costs against counsel, it is clear that the Pettens also believed that this undertaking was given and Mr. Myers was certainly authorized to raise the issue.

[115]               I am satisfied that the assertion by counsel for the plaintiffs was made hastily under a misunderstanding arising out of previous discussions with counsel for CSE.

[116]               When faced with the denial by counsel for CSE that any such undertaking was given in respect of trial evidence, Mr. Myers, quite properly and professionally, withdrew his assertion and apologized. Under these circumstances, the hasty and mistaken action taken by Mr. Myers, which was subsequently withdrawn with an apology, does not merit censure in the form of an order for solicitorclient costs. There is no indication that the making of the allegation occasioned any additional expense to CSE and, given Mr. Myers' proper and professional response, I do not believe that an award to support the integrity of the judicial system is necessary.

- Repetitious non-compliance with court orders.

[117]               There is no doubt that the plaintiffs did not comply with the time limits imposed in respect of certain pre-trial court orders as well as orders during trial; however, the costs consequences of such non-compliance should have been, and in some cases were, dealt with at the time of subsequent applications seeking enforcement.

[118]               Some of the non-compliance is explicable. For example, the failure to comply with an order for production of documents finds at least a partial explanation in the fact that new counsel took over the file and faced a massive task of familiarizing himself with the material before preparing and filing an organized book of documents. Again, with respect to the failure to file security within the time limited by my first order for security for costs, the matter was complicated by the discussions which were ongoing between counsel with respect to the possibility of substituting other forms of security. Subsequently, I was persuaded, following argument, that an extension of the time should be granted. In so doing, I awarded CSE a portion of their costs on a solicitorclient basis.

[119]               This is not a case of a simple ignoring of court process indicating a flagrant disrespect for the court system. These events do not constitute reprehensible, scandalous or outrageous conduct meriting an award of solicitor-client costs on this basis.

- Failing to carry out trial procedures in an "effective" manner.

[120]               CSE's argument with respect to this allegation is essentially that the trial was unnecessarily prolonged because of the manner in which it was conducted. It was suggested that the manner of questioning witnesses indicated that counsel often had no idea as to what the witnesses were likely to say and was effectively using the trial process as a form of discovery. It was also suggested that the unfocused nature of the examination of the witnesses was what led to the application during trial, previously referred to, for substantial amendments to the pleadings calling for the addition of further causes of action.

[121]               There is nothing to suggest that the conduct of the trial was not done with the approbation of Mr. & Mrs. Petten. In fact, if anything the Pettens believed that more, not fewer, questions should have been asked and evidence presented.

[122]               I have to say that there were points during the trial where I experienced feelings of frustration over the manner in which the case was developing and I began to question the direction it was taking. That led me to suggest, after twenty- seven days of trial, that all counsel participate in a discussion on the record as to how the case could be more focused and organized. I do not place complete blame for this, however, at the feet of the plaintiffs or their counsel. It must be remembered that Mr. Myers assumed carriage of the file, following the withdrawal of Mr. Moores as counsel, after pleadings had closed and discoveries had concluded. The withdrawal of Mr. Moores as counsel was, in practical terms, beyond the control of the Pettens. The involvement of new counsel inevitably engages the possibility of a different approach to the conduct of the trial based upon differing perceptions of the strengths and weaknesses of various aspects of the case. In response to my request for a discussion as to the future direction of the case, counsel sought amendments to the pleadings. That was perhaps understandable under the circumstances. It is also not without significance that many of the witnesses called by the plaintiff were employees, or former employees of, or were otherwise associated with, persons adverse in interest to the plaintiff. In some cases, it could be said that they were not wholly cooperative and the plaintiff had to seek leave to cross-examine as hostile witnesses which, in some cases, was granted. This reality inevitably extended the examination process. No doubt, as well, some of the examination was in the nature of discovery but, again, counsel did not have the benefit of having conducted the original discoveries.

[123]               I am not satisfied that, in the circumstances obtaining, the conduct of the plaintiffs' case was such as to constitute reprehensible, scandalous or outrageous conduct. While it is true that I (and certainly counsel for CSE) was not entirely happy with the manner in which the case was conducted, I have to be mindful of the fact that it is counsel, not the court, who has control over the presentation of the case and has the better knowledge base as to the contents of the case. So long as counsel is attempting to do his or her job and is not acting in bad faith or with improper motives (such as for purposes of delay) inefficiency and even negligence, while they may be grounds for a complaint by the client against the lawyer, would not normally be grounds for an award of solicitor-client costs in favour of an opposite party.

(b) Costs Against Counsel

[124]               No suggestion has been made of any impropriety on the part of the plaintiffs' former counsel, Douglas Moores, Q.C. The allegations are directed solely to the alleged improper actions on the part of Mr. Myers. Accordingly, the only events which are of significance in this context are those in which Mr. Myers was a direct participant. Thus, of the categories of complaint which were previously listed in paragraph [67], items 1 and 3 need not be considered at all and, as well, events encompassed by items 2 and 5 which occurred before Mr. Myers' retainer are also irrelevant.

[125]               The authority of the court to award costs against a solicitor personally flows from the inherent jurisdiction of the court to control its own process and the conduct of persons coming before it (Young per McLachlin, J., at p. 284) and from Rule 55.14(2) which provides in pertinent part:

"(2) Where in a proceeding, costs are incurred improperly, or without reasonable cause, or arise because of delay, neglect or other default, the court may, when the solicitor whom it considers to be responsible, whether personally or through a servant or agent, is before the court or has notice, make an order

. . . . .

(c) directing the solicitor personally to indemnify any other party against costs payable by the party; ..."

[126]               The basis of the jurisdiction is founded on the notion of control and discipline of the courts' officers. Members of the Law Society of Newfoundland, with the designation of both barristers and solicitors, are officers of the Supreme Court: Law Society Act, R.S.N. 1990, c. L-9, s.79. The power to award costs against a lawyer personally is one which should be used with caution. Although emphasising that costs are generally awarded as compensation for the successful party, McLachlin, J., in Young described the dangers of too liberal an exercise of this jurisdiction as follows at p. 284:

"Any member of the legal profession might be subject to a compensatory order for costs if it is shown that repetitive and irrelevant material, and excessive motions and applications, characterize the proceedings in which they were involved, and that the lawyer acted in bad faith in encouraging this abuse and delay.

. . . . .

"Moreover, courts must be extremely cautious in awarding costs personally against a lawyer, given the duties upon a lawyer to guard confidentiality of instructions and to bring forward with courage even unpopular causes. A lawyer should not be placed in a situation where his or her fear of an adverse order of costs may conflict with these fundamental duties of his or her calling."

[127]               The majority of the Supreme Court generally approved the more detailed discussion of the costs issues engaged in by Cumming, J.A., in the British Columbia Court of Appeal in Young v. Young (1990), 75 D.L.R.(4th) 46 (B.C.C.A.). At p. 111, Cumming, J.A., stated:

"An award of costs should not be made against a solicitor personally on the ground that proceedings brought on behalf of a client lacked merit unless it is beyond doubt, not only that the proceedings are devoid of merit and that the solicitor knew or ought to have known them to be so, but also that the responsibility for continuing with the proceedings despite their lack of merit lies with the solicitor, rather than the client. Firstly, a solicitor should not usurp the function of the court by pre-judging a client's case. Secondly, it will generally be impossible for a solicitor to defend a charge that he or she is responsible for proceeding with a meritless claim, by showing that the client has been advised of the improbability of success and has nevertheless insisted on proceeding, without a violation or waiver of solicitor-client privilege."

I agree with these comments.

[128]               The comments of Cumming, J.A., emphasize the dilemma in which a solicitor may find himself or herself when dealing with an application for costs against him or her personally where it is conduct by the solicitor, acting on behalf of a client in the conduct of a case, which forms the basis of the claim: the difficulty of differentiating between actions of the solicitor undertaken with the approbation or under the instructions or implied authority of the client (which should generally be attributed to the client) and those for which, in the words of Cumming, J.A., "the responsibility ... lies with the solicitor, rather than the client". The disentanglement of these differing situations is complicated by the role of solicitor-client privilege or the potential occurrence, even if solicitor-client privilege is waived, of an unseemly exchange between the lawyer attempting to avoid personal responsibility and the client trying to distance himself or herself, after the fact, from the actions of the lawyer.

[129]               In this case, Mr. Myers sought a waiver of solicitor-client privilege from the plaintiffs but they failed to give it. He deposed generally, however, that at the outset of the trial he believed that if the witnesses called by the plaintiff testified as he was instructed they would, the plaintiffs had a prima facie case against CSE, but that many witnesses did not testify as he expected. He further deposed that "the plaintiffs attended every day of trial and sat directly behind me and gave me instructions both orally and in the form of notes which they passed to me during the trial" and that "I sought to follow my clients' instructions and act in their interests and in discharge of my obligation to the court at all times, to the best of my abilities". Mr. Myers was not cross-examined with respect to these assertions. The inference which I draw from this is that in pursuing the claim against CSE and in otherwise acting as he did in the course of the trial, Mr. Myers was acting within the scope of his instructions from his clients.

[130]               Of course, the blind following of clients' instructions, whatever they may be, does not absolve a lawyer from personal responsibility. As an officer of the court, he also owes duties to the court, to his fellow lawyers and the legal profession, and to the preservation of the system of administration of justice by ensuring that it is not abused or subverted for improper purposes or by false or misleading assertions. As stated by Professor Gold in "The Court's Authority to Award Costs Against Lawyers" in Gertner (ed.), Studies in Civil Procedure (Toronto: Butterworths, 1979) at p. 63, the obligation to act on a client's instructions is "limited to the employment of tactics, procedures and behaviour consistent with the goals of the judicial system." Thus, it is said that a lawyer has a duty not to pursue a claim which he knows to be false or without merit and if a client nevertheless insists upon going forward, the lawyer should withdraw from the case. Unfortunately, such a rule, which can be simply stated, has difficulty in application in a case such as this. Even if Mr. Myers believed that there was no basis for a claim against CSE (which, according to his uncontradicted assertion was not the case) and even if he unequivocally informed the plaintiffs of the lack of merit but they nevertheless insisted that he proceed, the fact is that he had taken over a case involving many issues and a number of defendants and the trial was well underway by the time I commented on the tenuousness of the claim and awarded security for costs in April of 1994. It is unrealistic to have expected in these circumstances for Mr. Myers, assuming there was a conflict between his view and his clients' instructions with respect to the claim against CSE, to seek to withdraw from the whole case, thereby placing the whole litigation in limbo. Indeed, in these circumstances it would not be automatic for the court to allow Mr. Myers to withdraw. In such a scenario, one must be extremely cautious in attributing responsibility to a lawyer personally for proceeding with a claim that is ultimately determined to have had no merit.

[131]               What, then, is the standard below which a lawyer must fall in order to be subject to an order for costs against him or her personally? As the discussion above indicates, the standard should not be set so high as to make the lawyer the judge of his or her own clients' case. On the other hand, a lawyer may not hide behind a client's instructions in order to do things that involve a breach of his or her duties as an officer of the court or his or her obligations to opposing counsel. He or she is expected generally to follow both the spirit and letter of the rules of battle.

[132]               There are no reported Newfoundland Court of Appeal decisions addressing this issue directly. However, in two decisions where the question was whether the alleged inappropriate actions of counsel should be attributed to a party for the purposes of awarding solicitor-client costs against that party, there were comments made which may be of assistance. In House of Haynes, Marshall, J.A., commented at p. 37:

"The grounds for such awards ought not be extended to mere negligence of the type attributed to counsel by the appeal court judge in the case at bar that is not accompanied by recklessness or misconduct.

"There are important policy reasons supporting such a stance. If the liability were extended to mere negligence, effective operation of legal proceedings would be impaired. A bar which was conscious that counsel could be sanctioned for misapprehensions, oversights or like errors by imposition of penal costs might well be inhibited in pursuing its advocacy with the force and rigour that the occasion demands. This would have an obviously undesirable and detrimental effect upon the justice system."

In Canadian Paraplegic Association, an order for solicitor-client costs was upheld on the basis of the failure of a solicitor to make material disclosure to the court since it amounted to a failure to observe a "fundamental obligation imposed on an officer of the court" to follow not only the words but the spirit of the rules of procedure, which justified indemnifying the other party against unnecessary costs associated with such behaviour.

[133]               These cases suggest that something more than mistake, error of judgment or mere negligence would be necessary to justify an order against a solicitor. Serious misconduct or disregard of duty will generally have to be shown. While the Supreme Court of Canada in Young does not expressly affirm that negligence alone will be insufficient, that is a fair inference to be drawn from the cautionary words used by McLachlin, J., which were previously quoted. That is certainly the approach taken in decisions in a number of lower courts. In Perley v. Sypher et al. (1990), 109 N.B.R.(2d) 427; 273 A.P.R. 427 (C.A.) the New Brunswick Court of Appeal held that in order to fix costs against a solicitor the solicitor's actions must amount to "a serious breach of duty", more than mistake, error of judgment or mere negligence. The Court approved the statement that "there must be a serious dereliction of the solicitor's duty to the court, something which justifies the use of the word "gross"". In Cini v. Micallef et al. (1987), 60 O.R.(2d) 584 (H.C.), Sutherland, J., stated at p. 606:

"The question is not whether the solicitor was negligent but whether there was gross negligence or a failure on the part of the solicitor to carry out his professional duty to the court."

[134]               Notwithstanding such statements, counsel for CSE asserts, nevertheless, that "the established law allows mere negligence to be sufficient for an award of costs against a solicitor". He bases this argument on two things. First, he points to the general statement made by McLachlin, J., in Young that the basis of a cost award is "compensation ..., not in order to punish a barrister" as implying that because compensation is the purpose, one need not show misconduct exceeding negligence before an award is made. Secondly, he relies upon the decision of the House of Lords in Myers v. Elman, [1940] A.C. 282 (H.L.) and in particular the statement of Viscount Maugham therein at p. 208:

"In my opinion the jurisdiction as to costs is quite different [from the jurisdiction to strike a solicitor off the rolls for professional misconduct]. Misconduct or default or negligence in the course of the proceedings is in some cases sufficient to justify an order. The primary object of the court is not to punish the solicitor, but to protect the client who has suffered and to indemnify the party who has been injured."

Counsel stresses the distinction drawn in Myers between the disciplining, for punitive purposes, of lawyers on grounds of professional misconduct which, in England, the court may also engage in, and the exercise of a compensatory jurisdiction by the awarding of costs. The argument presented was that once it is recognized that the punitive jurisdiction of the court is related to discipline for professional misconduct (a matter in which in this jurisdiction is assigned to the Law Society and not the court) it becomes clear that the fulfilling of a compensatory purpose need not be tied to the finding of serious misconduct on the part of the lawyer concerned.

[135]               These arguments cannot be sustained. When the judgments in Myers are read as a whole, a much more narrow test emerges. Other Law Lords in Myers asserted that the jurisdiction to award costs against a solicitor also fulfilled a punitive function. See Lord Atkin at pp. 302-304; Lord Wright at pp. 318-319; Lord Porter at pp. 333-335. Lord Russell of Killowen on the other hand, agreed generally with Viscount Maugham.

[136]               In any event, regardless of whether the jurisdiction fulfills punitive and/or compensatory purposes, that still begs the question as to what is the standard expected of a solicitor below which he or she must fall in order to trigger the right to compensation by an award of costs. Even Lord Maugham at other points in his judgment appears to set a higher standard than that of mere negligence. At p. 292 he observed that although it is not necessary to show that the lawyer has been guilty of conduct which would justify punishment by striking him off the rolls or suspending him from practice, he nevertheless asserted that the jurisdiction to award costs ought to be exercised "only when there has been established a serious dereliction of duty". Indeed, the facts of the case disclosed much more than negligence in any event. It involved conduct of the solicitor in permitting the swearing of an untrue affidavit of documents which Viscount Maugham described as "dishonourable conduct in the course of the proceedings". Lord Atkins spoke of the standard in terms of "gross negligence", as did Lord Porter. Lord Wright referred to it as "gross neglect or inaccuracy" including "a failure on the part of a solicitor ... to realize his duty to aid in promoting, in his own sphere, the cause of justice". He further stated, "a mere mistake or error of judgment is generally not sufficient".

[137]               Although Myers was referred to with apparent approval by the Supreme Court of Canada in Pacific Mobile Corp. v. Hunter Douglas Canada Ltd. and Robitaille, [1979] 1 S.C.R. 842; 26 N.R. 453, a case decided before Young, the court did not indicate which of the speeches, or which parts of the speeches, in Myers were specifically agreed with.

[138]               Read as a whole, the decision in Myers is not inconsistent with the approach taken in Canada, as disclosed in the cases cited above. Indeed, in subsequent cases in England, Myers has not been interpreted in the manner argued for by counsel for CSE. Even though the jurisdiction to award costs has a compensatory thrust, there is still a disciplinary element involved. In R & T Thew Ltd. v. Reeves (No. 2), [1982] 3 All E.R. 1086 (C.A.), Denning, M.R., stated at p. 1089:

"This compensatory jurisdiction still retains, however, a disciplinary slant ... The cases show that it is not available in cases of mistake, error of judgement or mere negligence. It is only available where the conduct of the solicitor is inexcusable and such as to merit reproof."

Denning, M.R. cited the decision of Myers v. Elman as his authority. Again, in Edwards v. Edwards [1958] 2 All E.R. 197 (P.D.) Sach, J., referred with approval to the portion of Viscount Maugham's judgment in Myers where he emphasized the necessity for a serious dereliction of duty, "something which justifies ... the use of the word gross". See also Holden & Co. v. Crown Prosecution Service, [1990] 1 All E.R. 368 (C.A.).

[139]               It is also not without significance that even if counsel for CSE's wider interpretation of Myers is correct, that case concerned an order that the solicitor pay one-third of the other side's party and party costs. The court did not have to consider what level of misconduct by a solicitor would attract liability for solicitor-client costs, which is what is sought here. I conclude from this analysis that although the basic purpose behind the award of costs may be the compensation of the party affected by the solicitor's conduct of the litigation, there are punitive elements still involved. Although the effect of an award may be to compensate the other side, the triggering event for recognizing such entitlement to compensation must involve serious misconduct on the part of the solicitor for which he or she, rather than the client, can be said to be responsible. By making such an award, the court indirectly expresses its disapproval of the conduct of the solicitor. In that sense, the exercise of the jurisdiction has punitive features. I do not regard the reference to compensation in Young as being inconsistent with this analysis. The expressions of caution about placing a lawyer in a situation where fear of an adverse order of costs may conflict with his or her duties, and by stressing matters of "bad faith" and "encouraging ... abuse and delay" and tying the court's inherent jurisdiction to "abuse of process" and "contempt", McLachlin, J., is implicitly setting the standard for an award of costs against the solicitor higher than that of mere mistake, error of judgment or negligence. That is the law in Canada regardless of whether or not counsel for CSE's interpretation of Myers v. Elman is correct.

[140]               Is CSE's position advanced by the application of Rule 55.14(2) instead of reliance on the inherent jurisdiction of the court to award costs against a solicitor? That rule allows such an award where costs are incurred (i) improperly; or (ii) without reasonable cause; or where they arise because of: (iii) delay; (iv) neglect; or (v) other default. It has generally equivalent counterparts in England and other Canadian jurisdictions. Does the fact that the rule refers to "neglect" and "other default" expand the circumstances where costs may be awarded against a solicitor personally? I do not believe so. In the Court of Appeal in Young, where the similar British Columbia rule (57(30)) was considered, the comments of Cumming, J.A., previously referenced, were nevertheless made; indeed, the views of McLachlin, J., in the Supreme Court, which acknowledged that the court's authority in this regard came not only from its inherent jurisdiction but also from statute, must be taken to have been intended to apply to the scope of the applicable British Columbia rule as well.

[141]               The view that the applicable rule does not alter the scope of the inherent jurisdiction of the court also exists in England. In Holden, Lord Lane, C.J. noted that in Myers v. Elman Viscount Maugham referred to the equivalent English rule but nevertheless stated that "the jurisdiction ought to be exercised only when there has been established a serious dereliction of duty ...". Lord Lane concluded that the current rules of court (which had since been amended to include a reference to "failure to conduct proceedings with reasonable competence and expedition") nevertheless did not alter the common law rules as to the jurisdiction of the court to award costs against a solicitor personally. Approving the comments of Denning, M.R. in Thew, he continued to formulate the test as one of "serious dereliction on the part of the solicitor of his duty to the court."

[142]               While I would not pretend that all cases interpreting similar rules in Canadian jurisdictions are uniform in their approach, it is apparent from the way in which some have applied the rule, that there is a concern not to apply it with too broad a brush. See, Kern v. Kern and Wylie (1986), 50 R.F.L.(2d) 77 (Ont. H.C.); Weldo Plastics Ltd. v. Communication Press Ltd. (1987), 19 C.P.C.(2d) 36 (Ont. Dist. Ct.); and Real Securities of Canada Ltd. v. Beland et al. (1987), 16 C.P.C.(2d) 230 (Ont. Dist. Ct.).

[143]               While it is true that the British Columbia and English rules refer to "misconduct" instead of "neglect" as in the Newfoundland rule, I do not believe that this necessarily signals a broader jurisdiction in this province. I do not regard the reference to "neglect" as being equivalent to negligence; rather, I would equate it more to the notion of dereliction of duty that appears in the cases dealing with the court's inherent jurisdiction. That would be consistent with the cautionary approach to the exercise of the power evident not only in Young but also in Holden.

[144]               Counsel for CSE relies on the factual situation in Edwards as indicating the appropriateness of awarding costs in this case. In Edwards, a matrimonial dispute, the wife's solicitors were ordered to pay costs personally (on a party and party basis) "where the solicitor throughout was bent on pressing on with litigation regardless of all other factors" even though as a result of discovery of the husband's financial information provided by the husband's solicitors, it was apparent that no challenge to the husband's financial circumstances could be successful. CSE analogizes that situation to the refusal to discontinue against CSE in this case notwithstanding the disclosure to the plaintiffs of information verifying that CSE could not have been involved before June 1, 1989.

[145]               I have concluded, however, that the two situations can, and should be, distinguished. As noted in the previous discussion relating to the liability of the plaintiffs to pay costs, there were acts of Carroll after June 1, 1989, which could reasonably have been viewed by the plaintiffs and relied on to form the basis for an argument that the negligent statements in the Carroll report were reasserted again by Carroll while an employee of CSE. While, obviously, I was ultimately of the view that that was not the correct interpretation, I cannot say that the contrary view was so fanciful that the plaintiffs' holding that view was unreasonable and without any realistic foundation. Thus, unlike Edwards, where the disclosure of information, if properly considered and analysed, provided a complete answer to the claim, CSE's simple emphasis on the timing of its acquisition of the Evans assets did not provide such a complete answer in this case.

[146]               In this case, there is nothing to suggest that Myers did not have an honest belief in a realistic possibility of success against CSE or to suggest that, in the circumstances, he was acting otherwise than pursuant to instructions to continue to pursue the claim against CSE. In the convoluted circumstances of this case, including the stage at which Myers became involved and his duty to act for his client with respect to the other issues and against the other defendants, it was not realistic, even if he himself could not have honestly believed in the possibility of success against CSE, for him to have sought to withdraw totally from the case. I draw the inference that the plaintiffs were determined to proceed against CSE based on the information they had and that, in the circumstances, Myers had no choice but to continue to seek to maintain a claim against CSE. At most, failure to present other evidence to support the claim against CSE (assuming, but without deciding, that such evidence existed) might constitute negligence for which Myers might be answerable to his own client but such negligence (if that is what it was) would not justify an order for costs in favour of CSE against Myers. In any event, it cannot be said on the information available that the actions of Myers amounted to negligence as opposed to compliance with instructions by the plaintiffs to continue to press the claim even though there was no other evidence available and to rely solely on the slender uncontextualized threads of connection that had previously been identified. The actions of proceeding against CSE in these circumstances must in the circumstances be regarded as the actions of the plaintiff and not those for which individual responsibility on the part of the solicitor must be attributed. There is, therefore, no basis for a claim for costs against Mr. Myers on the basis of the pursuit of an unmeritorious claim against CSE.

[147]               Neither is there a basis for an award to chastise Mr. Myers for accusing counsel for CSE of having made an undertaking to produce a witness when no such undertaking had been given. As noted previously, the allegation was withdrawn and an apology was given. No lingering impression of improper behaviour on the part of CSE's counsel was left with the court. Nothing further is needed to vindicate counsel's reputation or the proper administration of justice.

[148]               The allegation of "repeatedly" failing to comply with court orders also overstates the situation. As previously noted, some of the non-compliance has at least a partial explanation which certainly leads to the inference that there was no deliberate attempt at flouting the order of the court. Nor can it be said that non- compliance was so "repeated" that a pattern of disrespect for the court and its process was established.

[149]               As to the complaint that the trial procedures were not carried out by counsel "in an effective manner", I agree that the conduct of the trial was not performed in a text book manner; however, that is a far cry from saying that the conduct of the trial was such that it merits an order against Mr. Myers personally. With hindsight, one might question some of the lines of inquiry engaged in in respect of some of the witnesses. As noted, however, Mr. Myers came into the case after the pleadings had closed and discoveries had concluded. As well, he faced the difficult task of calling as his own witnesses persons who were associated with some of the other parties. I agree generally with the position put by counsel for Mr. Myers in this regard:

"The fact that Myers was hired 'at the eleventh hour' after pleadings closed and discoveries had concluded and accordingly that the trial was used to discover the evidence of witnesses is entirely beyond his control. The Pettens may bear the risk of extra costs thereby attracted; Myers does not. The fact that applications for amendments were therefore necessary stands on the same footing. If counsel hired on the eve of trial becomes personally liable for solicitor-client costs if he finds it necessary to seek amendments to pleadings and to obtain evidence which has not already been obtained at discovery, no party could ever change counsel."

[150]               Viewing the actions of Myers as a whole in the context of how this case developed, it cannot be said that the conduct for which he is responsible exhibited "bad faith" or "serious dereliction of duty" or "gross neglect" or "serious misconduct". There is therefore no basis for holding Mr. Myers liable personally for costs.

General Observations

[151]               In summary, it should be observed that the definition and scope of solicitor- client costs awards against opposing parties and/or their counsel engages broad issues respecting the role of the court in the administration of justice in the community.

[152]               No doubt, improper conduct of counsel in the representation of a client's claim as well as abuse of the court process for improper purposes by litigants have the potential of reflecting adversely on the reputation and effectiveness of the courts as the proper dispensers of justice to citizens. The court should be alert to these concerns and should, in appropriate cases, take steps to counteract and discourage such actions.

[153]               Counsel for CSE submits that such considerations are central to this case and justify the court awarding solicitor-client costs. In his words: "it is time for this court (meaning the collective not just the presiding judge) to act." In support of a pro active role for the court in this regard, he made reference to the Gold article previously cited where the following occurs at pp. 83 and 86:

"Courts, it is suggested, have done little to impose sanctions in cases of misconduct by lawyers. The tendency has been to focus only upon blatant misconduct or petty errors. There is no identifiable trend on the part of our courts to enforce the lawyer's duty to act honestly, fairly and competently in the conduct of litigation. While it may be that the profession itself should be more vigilant of its members' activities and more demanding of their performance, courts ought not to deny their responsibility to reprove misconduct and error.

"To succeed in creating the judicial process discussed earlier, it is necessary to promote activity which will result in its practical achievement. In other words, lawyers must be called upon to account for their actions. The lawyer's accountability for behaviour which constitutes a breach of duty is the basis of the jurisdiction to award costs against a solicitor.

. . . . .

"A fearless and independent bar must be faced squarely by a fearless and independent judiciary, one that is prepared to exercise its authority to sanction misconduct and negligence."

[154]               While these comments were made in the context of a discussion of awarding costs against lawyers personally, the tenor of counsel for CSE's submissions respecting the claim for solicitor-client costs against the plaintiff equally involved exhortations to the court to be pro-active to control, by the use of special costs awards, the assertion of claims which do not have merit.

[155]               As important as the control of improper conduct of counsel and litigant is in the effective administration of justice, other fundamental values are equally important. Central among these is the need to ensure easy access to the courts. It is imperative that no undue tariff be placed on people for seeking resolution of their claims by invoking the help of the courts. Freedom of access to the court is one of the bases of social stability, so that disputes may be resolved by application of law rather than by force or other types of self-help by frustrated or disgruntled individuals who feel their views are not being heard and have nowhere else to turn.

[156]               The control of the improper use of the court for the presentation of meritless claims can be effected by resort to other mechanisms besides the imposition of solicitor-client costs. Rule 14.24 enables frivolous and vexatious claims or ones that are an abuse of the process of the court to be dismissed or stayed on application by the affected party. The very fact that the courts are reluctant to invoke that power unless the circumstances are "plain and obvious" (per Wilson, J., in Hunt v. T & N pic et al., [1990] 2 S.C.R. 959; 117 N.R. 321; 4 C.C.L.T.(2d) 1; 43 C.P.C.(2d) 105; 49 B.C.L.R.(2d) 273; 74 D.L.R.(4th) 321; [1990] 6 W.W.R. 385, at p. 980 [S.C.R.]) that the claim is certain to fail, is reflective of the policy of not denying a litigant access to the court to have his or her claim dealt with on the merits. Again, even where the untenability of a claim is not apparent from the pleadings, but a party nevertheless believes that on the available evidence it is impossible for liability to be imposed on a given issue or against a particular defendant, mechanisms such as applications for trial of a preliminary question of law or fact under Rule 38 or for summary trial of an issue under Rule 17A can be availed of to derail the proceeding and save the expense of a full trial.

[157]               Placed in perspective, therefore, using solicitor-client costs as a means of controlling meritless claims is not necessarily the preferred mechanism to be employed to accomplish this objective. Other mechanisms might well be able to be invoked in appropriate cases without the risk of discouraging access to the courts.

[158]               Such considerations must be balanced against the understandable desire not to let the system be subverted for improper purposes. Litigants already operate with the Damoclean sword of a potential adverse award of party and party costs hanging over their heads. The threat of an additional award of special costs, unless judiciously applied, may well have the effect of driving litigants from the courtroom or dampening the efforts of counsel in the fearless pursuit of the client's interests. This is the theme underlying Young, where McLachlin, J., spoke against such costs awards against parties even where the claims had "little merit" and warned against placing the lawyer in a situation "where his or her fear of an adverse order of costs may conflict with ... fundamental duties of his or her calling." That is why it is not inappropriate to set the threshold high and generally to limit awards of solicitor-client costs to events which can attract the types of descriptions discussed earlier in this decision: scandalous, outrageous, reprehensible, deserving of reproof or rebuke, bad faith, serious misconduct, flagrant acts meriting censure, acting on improper motives, serious dereliction of duty and gross neglect - conduct bordering on abuse of the court and its process, not merely negligence or mistake in bona fide assuming a case has some merit when in fact it does not. It is at that point where the scales tip in favour of using costs more aggressively because, then, it can fairly be said that the other side has abused the privilege of easy access to the system and should no longer be entitled to that privilege without having to compensate fully the other party affected, or without having to risk the censure of the court.

Judgment Interest on Costs

[159]               With respect to entitlement to prejudgment interest, s. 3(2) of the Judgment Interest Act, R.S.N. 1990, c. J-2 provides:

"(2) The court shall not award interest

. . . . .

(d) on an award of costs in the action;"

[160]               In the context of ordinary court proceedings, where the awarding of costs is made as part of or shortly after the judgment dealing with the substantive issues in dispute, the intent of this provision is clear: unlike monetary awards arising out of the substantive judgment, entitlement to costs will not, for the purpose of calculation of pre-judgment interest, be treated as arising at the time of the accrual of the cause of action on which they are based, but will be calculated at the earliest, from the date of judgment. Only post-judgment interest is therefore payable on costs.

[161]               Where the awarding of costs occurs as part of the judgment on the merits the party entitled to costs can, in short order, proceed to have them taxed and seek payment from the other side. But, what of the situation where the determination of costs is, at the request of the parties, reserved and a long delay occurs before the costs disposition is made? Does interest run from the date of the costs award or from the date of the judgment on the merits on which the later costs award is based? Put another way, is post-judgment interest payable only post-(cost) judgment, or is it payable post-(merits) judgment by notionally back-dating the costs disposition to the date of the decision on the merits which after all is the decision on which any claim to costs depends?

[162]               The resolution of this question is of some significance in this case. Here, CSE was successful on a non-suit motion halfway through the trial. In theory, it was entitled to ask the court to proceed to make a costs disposition arising from that decision. Indeed, counsel for CSE did make such an application before the trial of the other issues concluded; however, in the exercise of my discretion I declined to hear the application until the full trial was completed so that all findings of fact were known and all issues of costs involving the other parties could also be dealt with. I remain of the view that that was the right course of action in the circumstances. Indeed, as this judgment indicates, I have referred to certain findings in my decision on the merits in the course of making my costs disposition in this case.

[163]               The consequence of that decision, however, is that CSE was delayed in recovering its costs for the remainder of a very long trial and for a considerable period after its conclusion. If it is entitled to its costs without any interest prior to the date of this decision, the value of the award in its hands today is less than if it had been able to have a decision for the same amount at the conclusion of the non- suit motion, which, as I have said, it was in theory entitled to have.

[164]               It is true that success on the merits of a case does not guarantee an award of costs, because costs are always in the discretion of the court; yet as Sooley v. Canadian House Insurance Co. reiterates, a successful party, in the absence of special circumstances, has a "reasonable expectation" of obtaining an order for payment of at least party and party costs. Thus, while, technically, a party has no legal entitlement to costs until they are awarded, success on the merits for practical purposes will, except in special cases, amount to a signpost of entitlement.

[165]               In principle, where the delay has not been caused by CSE, it should not be placed in any worse position as a result of the events that transpired. The plaintiffs are not really in a position to complain since, at least in theory, they have had the use of the money in the interim or at the least have been spared having to face paying the costs at an earlier date.

[166]               Section 5 of the Judgment Interest Act, which deals with post-judgment interest, provides that "every judgment debt" bears interest from the day on which it is payable "under the judgment". Section 2(d) defines "judgment debt" to include "a sum of money or costs ... made payable under a judgment ...". The Act appears to operate on the assumption (correct in most cases) that a costs award will form part of the judgment on the merits. It does not expressly address the situation where there is a bifurcation in the overall dispository decision.

[167]               The Act must be given a purposive interpretation to enable its objectives to be accomplished. Its application should not depend on the happenstance of two decisions temporally separated, instead of one composite disposition, being made. Section 5 mandates the payment of interest post-judgment. A costs award, whether made at the same time as the decision on the merits or subsequently, is still part of the overall disposition in the case. The judgment is not over and the court not "functus officio" until all required dispositions have been made.

[168]               The costs award is parasitic upon the disposition on the merits. It is that decision which triggers the "reasonable expectation" of a costs award. A costs decision is therefore part of the original judgment in the sense of arising out of or, to use the words of the Act, "under" that judgment.

[169]               Accordingly, there is no compelling reason why the notion of "judgment" in the Act should be dissected into constituent dispositions for the purpose of awards of post-judgment interest on costs, as opposed to treating it as the decision from which the reasonable expectation of costs flows.

[170]               This interpretation does not contravene the prohibition in s. 3(2) (d) against awarding pre-judgment interest on costs because once the judgment "under" which the costs are payable is treated as the judgment on the merits which triggers the right to claim costs, the issue is one of post-judgment interest rather than pre-judgment interest. The notion of denying interest on costs prior to judgment is still respected.

[171]               Accordingly, I would declare that CSE is entitled to interest, calculated according to the rates prescribed under the Judgment Interest Act, on the costs awarded in this matter, assessed from the date of filing of my decision in the non-suit application.

Summary and Disposition

1. The first defendant, CSE Marine Services Inc. is entitled to costs, to be taxed, on a party and party basis jointly and severally against the plaintiffs in respect of all portions of the trial up to and including the date of judgment upholding CSE's motion for a non-suit, and in respect of all pre- trial applications involving CSE in which costs were ordered in the cause.

2. CSE is entitled to interest calculated according to the rates prescribed under the Judgment Interest Act, on the costs awarded to CSE in this matter, assessed from May 18, 1995.

3. The claim for an award of solicitorclient costs against the plaintiffs is hereby dismissed.

4. The claim for an award of solicitorclient costs, or costs on any other basis, against the intervenor Owen Myers personally is hereby dismissed.

5. Upon production pursuant to Rule 20.12(2) of a certificate from the Registrar that money was in fact paid into court pursuant to my Order of April 29, 1994, the said funds shall, pursuant to Rule 21.03, be paid out (together with accrued interest, less Registrar's compensation provided for by Rule 20.12(3)) to the solicitors for CSE in partial satisfaction of the party and party costs, as taxed, to which CSE may be entitled.

6. Because the primary issue on this application was whether solicitor-client costs should be awarded - an issue on which CSE lost - the plaintiffs and the intervenors, Owen Myers and Law Society of Newfoundland, shall be entitled to their party and party costs, to be taxed, against CSE in respect of this application.
