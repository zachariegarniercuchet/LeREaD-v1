I.  INTRODUCTION

 

[1]   On September 28, 2006, the Respondent imposed a 45-day suspension of the liquor licence of the Applicants and the maximum fine of $5000.00 under s.36(1) of the Liquor Act, R.S.N.W.T. 1988, c.L-9, as duplicated for Nunavut by s.29 of the Nunavut Act, S.C. 1993, c.28, and as amended.  On October 20, 2006, the Applicants filed a Notice of Appeal under s.23(2) of the Liquor Act.  They also filed an Originating Notice under Rules 591-606 of the Nunavut Rules of Court, R.N.W.T. R-010-96, as duplicated for Nunavut by s.29 of the Nunavut Act, S.C. 1993, c.28, (Rules) which outline the requirements for a judicial review application.

 

[2]   The Applicant filed four affidavits in support of the motions but the Respondent was unable to file the documents specified in Rule 598 because of the short notice.

 

[3]   Notices of motion were filed in both actions seeking a stay of proceeding pending a hearing on the merits.  The stay was granted after argument and these are the reasons for my decision.

 

 

II.  FACTS

 

[4]   The following facts are derived from the affidavits of the Applicants and the statements of facts of the application briefs.

 

[5]   The Applicants own and operate a restaurant in Iqaluit, Nunavut, under the name and style of the Kamotiq Inn Restaurant (Kamotiq Inn).  The business holds a dining room licence, issued by the Respondent, authorizing the sale of alcohol to the customers of the business.

 

[6]   The Kamotiq Inn was the subject of an initial show cause hearing under s.35 of the Liquor Act in 2004.  The first hearing was scheduled for December 9, 2004, and adjourned three times at the request of the Applicants.  A day-long hearing was finally held on June 8, 2005, where the Kamotiq Inn was represented by legal counsel.  The Respondent held that the Kamotiq Inn had permitted drunkenness on six occasions in the restaurant contrary s.98(2)(a) of the Liquor Act.  The Respondent imposed a fine of $1000 for some incidents and $1500 for the others as well as a four-day licence suspension.  In addition, the Respondent ordered that the Kamotiq Inn provide server training for all staff, along with satisfactory proof of compliance to the Manager of Liquor Enforcement by August 15, 2005.

 

[7]   On June 25, 2005, Marcel Mahe wrote to liquor inspector, Ms. K. Pumphrey, and advised her that the server training ordered by the Respondent had been completed by Loredana Mahe.

 

[8]   Loredana Mahe carried out the server training in June and again in November 2005.

 

[9]   On March 16, 2006, the Chairperson of the Respondent wrote to the Kamotiq Inn and advised that the renewal of the liquor licence effective on April 1, 2006, was subject to providing proof of compliance with health and fire regulations and the server training.  The letter also indicated the licence would expire on June 22, 2006, in conjunction with a show cause hearing if the necessary proof was not provided by March 31, 2006. 

 

[10]  On March 30, 2006, the Kamotiq Inn alleges that it sent a report to Simon Okpatauyak, the executive secretary of the Respondent, containing documentation that provided proof of the completion of the server training that had been ordered.  Loredana Mahe alleges she telephoned Mr. Okpatauyak on the same day and received confirmation from him that the report was satisfactory.

 

[11]  The status of the Kamotiq Inn licence was on the agenda of the June 2006 meetings of the Respondent, but was not discussed because the Board ran out of time.

 

[12]  Around September 11, 2006, the Kamotiq Inn was served with a notice of a show cause hearing scheduled for September 27, 2006.  The notice specified that the Kamotiq Inn was required to show cause why its licence should not be suspended because of the failure to provide proof of compliance with the Order of the Respondent of June 5, 2005, to carry out the server training before August 15, 2005.

 

[13]  At the time of service of the notice of hearing, the Applicants were in Gatineau P.Q. where Marcel Mahe was scheduled for cancer biopsy surgery.  He had waited six months for the appointment and wrote to the chairperson of the Respondent on September 20, 2006, to explain the position of the Kamotiq Inn.  He indicated he could not afford to hire a lawyer and could not attend the hearing because of his scheduled surgery.  He also provided the same documentation that had been sent to Simon Okpatauyak on March 30, 2006, and informed him that Mr. Okpatauyak had confirmed that the Order of the Respondent had been complied with.

 

[14]  Recognizing that there may have been a problem with communications, Loredana Mahe contacted the Chief Liquor Inspector to find out why the hearing had been scheduled and was informed that it was because she was not qualified to conduct the server training.  Then she spoke with Patrick Galbraith, the Executive Director of the Respondent, who informed her that he could not find the Notice of Hearing.  Next she spoke with Simon Okpatauyak who advised her to send someone to attend the hearing.  Finally she telephoned Stu Kennedy, who is a member of the Respondent, and provided him with a copy of all the relevant documentation.  He assured her that the documentation was satisfactory to show cause to the satisfaction of the Respondent.

 

[15]  The Applicants sent Dana Mahe to represent the Kamotiq Inn at the hearing on September 27.  She is the daughter of the Applicants and works in the restaurant as a waitress and manager.  She discovered that Stu Kennedy was not sitting on the Board panel.  She put forward the position of the Kamotiq Inn  but was aggressively cross-examined by the legal counsel for the Board and made some mistakes because she lacked personal knowledge.  She recognized that she needed some help and called Loredana Mahe for advice.

 

[16]  Loredana Mahe instructed her to ask for an adjournment so that a lawyer could be retained.  The adjournment was strongly opposed by Counsel for the Respondent on the basis that the matter had been outstanding for over a year and that the Kamotiq Inn was knowledgeable about the procedures of the Respondent.  After deliberation, the Board refused the adjournment without reasons.

 

[17]  Simon Okpatauyak then testified that he did not receive the materials about the server training on March 30, 2006.  The Respondent gave more weight to the evidence of Simon Okpatauyak than the hearsay evidence of Dana Mahe but permitted her until noon the next day to produce the necessary documentation.

 

[18]  The Respondent reconvened the next day and Dana Mahe produced additional documentation but did not give any additional evidence.  The documentation did not include any fax confirmation sheets proving the service of the documents on March 30, 2006, but did contain unsworn statements from employees of the Kamotiq Inn indicating that they attended server courses in June and November conducted by Loredana Mahe.

 

[19]  The Respondent, in written decisions of September 27 and 28, 2006, rejected the evidence of the Applicants and accepted the evidence of Simon Okpatauyak.  The Kamotiq Inn was fined $5000.00 and its license was suspended until November 12.  It was also ordered to provide server training to its current employees from a qualified trainer by November 12 and to provide proof of the payment of the expenses of the trainer.  The licence would remain suspended until the proof was provided to the Executive Secretary of the Respondent.

 

[20]  The licence suspension has caused bad publicity and considerable economic damage to the Kamotiq Inn.  Numerous employees have been laid off and sales are very low.  There is a risk that the business may close permanently.

 

 

III.  ISSUES

 

[21]  The only issue in this interim application is whether a stay of proceedings should be granted.  The Respondent has raised some preliminary issues on the admissibility of the affidavit evidence at the hearing on the merits of the appeal and/or judicial review. Those objections do not apply to the application for the stay but merit some comment to assist in narrowing the issues at the hearing on the merits.

 

 

IV.  Affidavits

 

A.  Arguments

 

[22]  The Respondent argued that affidavit evidence is not admissible to supplement the record that will be filed in the near future on the appeal or judicial review.

 

[23]  The statutory appeal under s.23(2) of the Liquor Act is restricted to errors of law and excess of jurisdiction.  These grounds create an appeal on the record of the Tribunal rather than a trial de novo.  In this type of appeal, new evidence is not generally admissible except under stringent conditions.  The Respondent opposes the admissibility of any affidavit evidence but cited no authorities.

 

[24]  On the judicial review application, the Respondent relied on R. v. Northumberland Compensation Appeal Tribunal, ex parte Shaw, [1952] 1 All E.R. 122, [1952] 1 K.B. 338 (C.A.) (Northumberland), to argue that the only evidence that was admissible was that contained in the record that will be filed by the Respondent in the near future.  The only exception to this rule is evidence that pertains to due process and natural justice.  The Respondent argued that the facts deposed to, in the affidavits to support the grounds alleged, did not reveal any errors of fact or law to ground an assertion that the Board acted in excess of jurisdiction.  As a result, the Applicants failed to satisfy the exceptions to the rule.  Admission of the affidavits would only obscure the basic fact that the Applicants failed to comply with an order of the Respondent.

 

[25]  If the affidavits did fall within the exceptions, the Respondent objects to a number of paragraphs and requests that they be struck out because they are not in compliance with Rule 373(3) of the Rules.  The offending paragraphs are 4, 10 and 11 of the affidavit of Marcel Mahe and 4, 7, 12, portions of 17 and 18, as well as 24, 26, 27, 29 and 30 of the affidavit of Loredana Mahe.

 

[26]  The Applicants relied on Whitehouse v. Sun Oil., [1982] 6 W.W.R. 289, [1982] A.J. No. 656 (AB. C.A.), to support the admission of the affidavits.  In that case, the Court of Appeal asserted its right to hear evidence and consider the merits of the decision of the tribunal.

 

B.  Analysis

 

(i) Admission of Affidavits

 

[27]  Counsel for the parties chose not to address the issue of the alternate remedies and the availability of certiorari when there is a statutory right of appeal.  However, consideration of this issue is an essential prerequisite to determining the admissibility of the affidavits.

 

[28]  As noted at pages 3-6 and 3-7 of Judicial Review of Administrative Action in Canada , the failure of an applicant to pursue a statutory remedy will usually bar relief in judicial review proceedings if the other remedy is considered to be an adequate alternative to judicial review.  In each case, the reviewing court must determine whether the statutory procedure provides an adequate remedy to the grievance of the applicant.

 

[29]  The provision of a statutory remedy by way of an appeal to the courts prima facie indicates a legislative intention to exclude the courts’ exercise of their supervisory jurisdiction .  Some courts have held that the existence of an appeal right deprives them of jurisdiction  because it indicates a legislative judgment that it is better that a court’s consideration of a matter be based on the full administrative record with a broader scope of review and possibly a less deferential standard of review applied.  Courts generally decline a remedy pursuant to a judicial review and strike out the application for judicial review where both an appeal and a judicial review are sought .

 

[30]  These principles were applied in the Northwest Territories in Baffin Plumbing & Heating Ltd. v. Northwest Territories (Labour Standards Board), [1993] N.W.T.R. 301, [1993] N.W.T.J. No 111, and that case provides sound guidance for this Court.  Vertes J. noted that certiorari will be refused where a statutory appeal is provided and the allegation is non-jurisdictional errors of law.  If the allegations go to questions of jurisdiction or raise issues of natural justice then certiorari will lie.  He described three type of errors that could be committed by a tribunal as follows:

 

"[21] When referring to the various types of errors that could be committed by a tribunal, I use them in this context:

 

(i) an "error of law" is an error committed by a tribunal in good faith in interpreting or applying a provision of its enabling statute or in making a decision within its jurisdiction;

 

(ii) a "jurisdictional error" is an error relating to a misinterpretation of the statutory provisions that describe, list, or limit the tribunal's powers; and,

 

(iii) a "natural justice" issue goes to jurisdiction in theory but it is more appropriately a collateral error going to fundamental fairness, such as bias or a failure to afford equal opportunity for both sides to be heard.

"

 

[31]  Vertes J. concluded that the words, "any error of law raised before the Board," in the Labour Standards Act, encompassed all three types of error.  He then referred to the well-established case law, that where there is a broad jurisdiction in the statutory appeal, judicial review should be refused.

 

"[24] I am of the opinion that appeals pursuant to s.53(4) encompass errors of law, jurisdictional errors, and natural justice issues.  There is extensive case law from which to conclude that certiorari is not an appropriate vehicle when there is an adequate statutory appeal process.  The jurisdiction of the court pursuant to the statutory appeal process is co-extensive with the jurisdiction of a reviewing court under certiorari proceedings. "

 

 

[32]  Section 23 of the Liquor Act states:

 

"23(1) Subject to this section, every order of the Board is final.

 

(2) A licence holder that is a party to a decision or order of the Board may appeal the decision or order to the Supreme Court on the ground that the Board has erred in law or exceeded its jurisdiction.

 

(3) The Board or the Minister may state a case to the Supreme Court in the manner provided for in the Criminal Code.

 

(4) Where an appeal of a decision or order of the Board has been made, a judge of the Supreme Court may by order stay the execution of the decision or order on the terms that the judge considers just."

 

[33]  The appeal grounds for review by this Court specified in s.23 are an error or law or excess of jurisdiction.  These grounds are broader than an "error of law raised before the board" and, in my opinion, encompass all three types of error.  As a result, the application for judicial review should be refused.

 

[34]  Vertes J. also expressed the opinion that both applications should not be sought for the same relief to prevent superfluous litigation and possible conflicting judgments.  The same view was expressed by the Northwest Territories Court of Appeal in Baffin Plumbing & Heating Ltd. v. Northwest Territories (Labour Standards Board), [1997] N.W.T.J. No. 26.  However, in Marren v. Echo Bay Mines Ltd., 2000 NWTCA 6, [2000] N.W.T.J. No. 62, the Court of Appeal implicitly recognized the necessity to proceed in this manner where the extent of the statutory jurisdiction was unclear.  When both proceedings are commenced, the Court should consider the statutory appeal first and, if it provides the necessary jurisdiction and remedy, the judicial review should be refused.  Since there is limited administrative case law authority in this jurisdiction, it was appropriate to launch both applications.

 

[35]  The general rule on a judicial review application is that the reviewing court should only look at the material that was before the tribunal.  The authority cited for the rule by the Respondents was the classic case of Northumberland.  An excellent summary of the application of the Northumberland case in Canada appears in McFadyen v. Canada (Attorney General), 2005 FCA 360, [2005] F.C.J. No. 1817, where the Court stated:

 

"[15] The principle that evidence outside the administrative record, that is outside the record before the tribunal, can be considered where the grounds for review are any of the various forms of jurisdictional error is well-established: as Denning L.J. noted in an early English leading case,

 

"[w]hen certiorari is granted on the ground of want of jurisdiction, or bias, or fraud, affidavit evidence is not only admissible, but it is, as a rule, necessary" [Northumberland at 131]

 

This principle has been approved by the Supreme Court of Canada on numerous occasions...  A breach of procedural fairness is also one of the forms of jurisdictional error to which this principle applies…"

 

[36]  The documentation that constitutes the record varies from jurisdiction to jurisdiction.  There are specific rules governing the admission of new evidence in Newfoundland and under the Federal Court of Canada.  Nunavut does not have a comparable rule.  Rule 598 requires the tribunal to file a record that contains the judgment order or decision, the process commencing the proceeding, the evidence and all exhibits filed, all things touching the matter and the originating notice served on the respondents.  This Rule is identical to that used in Northwest Territories, Alberta and Nova Scotia.

 

[37]  As noted in Waverly (Village) v. Minister of Municipal Affairs), [1993] N.S.J. No. 238, [1993] CarswellNS 201 (S.C.), and Canada Life Assurance Co. v. Nova Scotia (Minister of Municipal Affairs), [1995] N.S.J. No. 500, [1995] CarswellNS 260 (S.C.), the traditional concept of the record has been modified by these Rules of Court.

 

[38]  In McFadyen, the applicant alleged a breach of procedural fairness because of bias and was permitted to file a lengthy affidavit outside the record.

 

[39]  In Daku v. Saskatchewan (Agriculture and Food Lands Appeal Board), 1998 CanLII 13481, [1998] S.J. No. 139 (Q.B.), the applicants relied on the Northumberland case to argue that a transcript of the proceedings before the tribunal should form part of the appeal record.  McIntyre J. took an expansive view of the record in a statutory appeal adopting the comments of the Supreme Court of Canada in Canadian Union of Public Employees, Local 301 v. Montreal (City), [1997] 1 S.C.R. 793, 1997 CanLII 386, stating:

 

"[39] The within appeal process is the statutory appeal on questions of law.  As noted earlier, there is a broad right of appeal on questions of law and there is no issue of curial deference.  The statute does not dictate what material should form the record to be placed before the court but rather it is left to the court's discretion.  While the Board is not required by statute or common law to record its proceedings it has done so and a transcript could be provided

 

...

 

[42] Where the court is hearing a statutory appeal as opposed to an application for judicial review, and absent statutory requirements, the Supreme Court of Canada in Montreal (City) has, in my view, adopted a practical approach to the question of what material ought to be before the court.  The issue is to be determined by reference to what material is required in order to enable the court to properly dispose of the appeal."

 

[40]  The Respondent objects to the content of the affidavits on the basis that they do not fit the rules for the use of affidavits outside the administrative record in judicial review.  The first objection is everything about the participation of Stu Kennedy.  This includes paragraph 10 of the affidavit of Marcel Mahe, paragraphs 5, 6 and 15 of the affidavit of Dana Mahe, and paragraphs 17, 18, and 19 of the affidavit of Loredana Mahe.  The Respondent argues that it was improper to approach a single board member because it was contrary to the rules of the Board.  Furthermore, relying on MacQueen v. New Brunswick (Assessment and Planning Appeal Board), 2005 NBQB 154, [2005] CarswellNB 315, in law, the Board cannot be bound by whatever a single member may say or do.

 

[41]  There is some merit to the argument of the Respondent that the applicants acted improperly in approaching Mr. Kennedy.  However, this only happened as a last resort when they could not find out from the Executive Director or Executive Secretary of the Board why the hearing was being held.  While Mr. Kennedy cannot bind the Board, the evidence of his participation is relevant to the state of mind of the Applicants on the preparation for the hearing and the reasons for not attending in person.

 

[42]  The second objection surrounds the submissions contained in paragraphs 55-61 of the pre-hearing brief of Counsel for the Applicants.  Counsel for the Respondent interprets these arguments as a bias argument.  She submits that the affidavits provide no additional insight into the Board’s decision beyond what can be understood from the Board’s decisions themselves.

 

[43]  I agree that the argument of Counsel for the Applicants appears to be that the Board was biased toward the Applicants.  The non-participation of Mr. Kennedy may not be any evidence of bias by the Board as a whole but it does raise the question of why he did not participate.  It may well be because of his prior contact with the Applicants, but it may also be for other reasons that call for an explanation in a reply affidavit.  The same goes for the statements in the affidavit about the documentation in the possession of the Chairperson.

 

[44]  The third objection concerns the nature of the proceedings on September 27 and 28.  Counsel for the Respondent submits that the Applicants misapprehended what occurred on September 28.  It was not a new hearing but a continuation of the one started on September 27.

 

[45]  The fourth category of objection related to what was characterized by Counsel for the Applicants as errors of law and fact.  She submitted that the Applicant’s argument about hearsay was not an error of law.  Finally, she argued that the allegation of the patent unreasonableness of the Board’s finding on the failure of the Respondents to comply with the Order was not supported by the facts as found by the Board.  As a result, none of the grounds revealed an error of fact or law sufficient to ground an assertion that the Board acted in excess of its jurisdiction so as to permit the admission of the affidavits.

 

[46]  There is little about the nature of the proceedings on September 27 and 28 in the affidavits.  The objection goes to the arguments of the Applicant about the events revealed in the written decisions.

 

[47]  Since this is a statutory appeal, I adopt the comments of  McIntyre J. in Daku about the broad discretion to admit outside evidence.  The grounds and arguments alleged by the Applicant raise many issues but the thrust of the affidavits is directed to the issue of the denial of natural justice by refusing the request for an adjournment.  The Applicants allege that they were treated unfairly by being forced to participate in a hearing they did not understand and were unable to attend because of the medical condition of Mr. Mahe.  The affidavits are all generally relevant to this basic issue.  I am therefore disposed to allow the admission of the affidavits as part of the record subject to further arguments that may be made at the hearing on the merits before me or another judge assigned to hear the motion.

 

(ii) Technical Objections to Affidavits

 

[48]  The Respondent argued that, even if the affidavits could be legally admitted outside the record, some paragraphs should be struck because they concern contentious facts that are based on information and belief rather than personal knowledge contrary to Rule 373 of the Rules which states:

 

"373. (1) Subject to subrule (3), a deponent may state in an affidavit only what the deponent would be permitted to state in evidence as a witness in court.

 

...

 

(3) An affidavit may contain statements of the deponent's information and belief with respect to facts that are not contentious, if the source of the information and the fact of the belief are specified in the affidavit."

 

 

[49]  I considered the meaning of contentious in Whitty and Associates Inc. et. al. v. Kakivak Developments et. al. (16 April 2004), Iqaluit CV-0000407 (Nu. C.J.), stating:

 

"[32] Rule 373(3) differs from the Alberta equivalent in that the affidavit can only be used in non-contentious matters.  The Nunavut rule is based on Ontario Rule 39.01.  The meaning of contentious was considered in Metropolitan Toronto Condominum Corp. No 781 v. Reyhanian, [2000] O.J.No. 2640, where Lamek J. stated:

 

“[16] The more important issue is whether hearsay (or information and belief) evidence is admissible at all in support of the contempt motion.  The question becomes whether the matters upon which such evidence is tendered are "contentious".  Mr. Shiller referred me to certain dictionary definitions, I did not consider them particularly helpful - or, at least, not to his position.  The Dictionary of Canadian Law, for example, defined "contentious" as "contested".  In my view, none of the matters that are said to be hearsay is disputed or contested in the sense that the truth of it has been challenged or contradicted.”

 

[50]  At this stage of the proceeding, no opposing affidavits have been filed by the Respondents and the affidavits therefore cannot be considered contentious.  Once opposing affidavits are filed the conflicts may be examined to determine whether they are contentious.  After filing the response affidavits, Counsel for the Applicants should be in a position to understand which points are contentious.  Under Rule 383(4), a supplementary affidavit may be filed to address the issues raised in the opposing affidavits.  Arguments can then be advanced on these preliminary issues before moving to the merits.

 

 

V.  STAY APPLICATION

 

A.  Law

 

[51]  The Applicants rely of s.23(4) of the Liquor Act which authorizes this Court to stay an order of the Respondent.  Since little guidance is provided on the meaning of the word "just" unlike with Rule 604 of the Rules, it was submitted that this Court should apply the rules for the granting of an interim injunction.  Counsel for the Respondent agreed that this was appropriate and advanced arguments on the threefold tests for interim injunctive relief.

 

[52]  As held in North American Tungsten Corp. Ltd. v. MacKenzie Valley Land and Water Board, 2003 NWTCA 3, [2003] N.W.T.J. No. 4 (North American Tungsten), the principles that apply to the interim injunctions also apply to applications for a stay of proceedings.  The Supreme Court of Canada stated in Manitoba (A.G.) v. Metropolitan Stores (MTS) Ltd., [1987] 1 S.C.R. 110, 1987 CanLII 79:

 

"[p.127]  A stay of proceedings and an interlocutory injunction are remedies of the same nature.  In the absence of a different test prescribed by statute, they have sufficient characteristics in common to be governed by the same rules and the courts have rightly tended to apply to the granting of interlocutory stay the principles which they follow with respect to interlocutory injunctions."

 

[53]  The principles governing the issuance of interim injunctions were recently applied by this Court in S. (J.) (Litigation Guardian of) v. Nunavut, 2006 NUCJ 01, [2006] Nu.J. No. 1.

 

"[39] Both counsel relied on RJR-MacDonald Inc. v. Canada (Attorney General), [1994] 1 S.C.R. 311, 1994 CanLII 117, that outlined a three-part test for granting interlocutory relief. The requesting party must establish that:

 

(a) there is a serious legal issue to be determined;

 

(b) the moving party will suffer irreparable harm prior to the hearing if the interlocutory relief is not granted;

 

(c) the balance of inconvenience, taking into account the public interest, favors a departure from the status quo.”

 

B.  Arguments and Analysis

 

(i) Serious Issue

 

[54]  The Applicants argue that there are serious issues raised in the originating motion and supporting affidavits on the issues of natural justice and errors of law and fact.  The natural justice issues are the denial of the adjournment and bias by the Respondent.  Counsel for the Respondent argues that the only serious issue is the request for an adjournment.

 

[55]  While the arguments on bias are weak, the arguments on the denial of the adjournment are much stronger.  The remaining arguments are hard to assess at this stage.  I am satisfied that this part of the test has been satisfied.

 

(ii) Irreparable Harm

 

[56]  The Applicants argue that there is ongoing financial harm being sustained by the Kamotiq Inn because of the suspension of the liquor licence.  The financial losses cannot be recovered if the decision of the Respondent is quashed because of s.18(5) of the Liquor Act, which prevents any legal action against the Respondent for acts carried out in good faith.

 

[57]  The Respondent argues that the suspension will be over in six days and that the losses are speculative.  The fine and cost of server training are recoverable if the decision is quashed.

 

[58]  While the particulars of the losses were not well-defined it seems clear that there are significant losses being incurred.  The business of the Applicants has fallen off to a fraction of the normal business and most of its employees have been laid off.  The inability to recover the losses by an action in damages means the loss is permanent.  The fine imposed is the maximum under the Liquor Act and if it is not paid in full the suspension will continue.  The suspension has caused unfavorable publicity and the stigma may deter patrons of the business from returning, thereby endangering the existence of the business.  Adding this cash flow stress to the losses to date that cannot be recovered satisfies me that there will be irreparable harm if the stay is not granted.

 

(iii)  Balance of Convenience

 

[59]  The Applicants argue that the public interest will not be harmed by the stay.  They were diligent in commencing these proceedings even though the Respondent took over a year to follow up on the March 16, 2006, letter from the Chairperson and did not feel it was important enough to deal with at its June 2006 meeting.  There will be no harm to the public interest by granting a short stay until the merits can be argued.

 

[60]  The Respondent argues that it is in the public interest to ensure that the Respondent receives the deference to its authority that the Legislature intended in order to secure the responsible use of alcohol in Nunavut.  The Respondent cited North American Tungsten in support of this argument where Vertes J. stated:

 

"[5] a court must consider the effect on the public interest before issuing a stay against a public authority."

 

[61]  While acknowledging the impact of a stay on the public authority, Vertes J. found that this consideration had to give way, on a balance of convenience, to a possible shutdown of the business of the company by the failure to renew the water licence.  The same is true in this case.

 

 

V.  CONCLUSION

 

[62]  I accordingly grant the stay of proceedings requested by the Applicants with costs to be in the cause.
