Burlington Railway et al

 

v.

 

Baseline Industries and Cerescorp Incorporated

 

 

REASONS FOR JUDGMENT OF THE HONOURABLE MR. JUSTICE CUMMING:

 

 

 

1         This is an appeal from the judgment of Mr. Justice Leggatt pronounced March 12, 1992 in which he held that the Trustee in Bankruptcy of Baseline Industries Ltd. had the right to possession of the lands and premises in question notwithstanding the breach of the covenant to pay rent by Baseline and the proceedings taken by Burlington Northern Railway Company under the provisions of the Commercial Tenancy Act, R.S.B.C. 1979, c.54.

 

FACTS:

2         Burlington leased to Baseline a dock in Vancouver.  The respondent Cerescorp Incorporated has operated the dock and terminal facilities on the premises pursuant to an exclusive licence granted by Baseline on April 14, 1988.  Cerescorp was added as a party to these proceedings by the Order of the Court of Appeal, on review.

 

3         Under the lease, Baseline was to pay $600,000 to Burlington in the first five years of the lease.  This amount was payable in 60 monthly instalments of $10,000 commencing on March 1, 1988.

 

4         In May 1989, Baseline was in arrears and on May 24, 1989 Burlington made a demand for payment of arrears which stipulated that Baseline had 30 business days to pay or "Burlington Northern shall re-enter the premises and declare the lease forfeited".

 

5         Baseline did not make payment of the arrears until sometime in August of 1989 but apparently Burlington took no steps to forfeit the lease.  Baseline ceased making lease payments on August 31, 1989.

 

6         Clause 14(a) of the lease reads as follows:

14.(a)    If and whenever the rent hereby reserved or any part thereof shall not be paid on the day specified for payment thereof, whether demanded or not or in case of breach, non-performance or non-observance by the Lessee of any of the covenants, agreements, or provisos on the part of the Lessee to be kept, performed or observed herein or in case the Premises shall be vacated or remain unoccupied for 15 days, then and in every case, it shall be lawful for the Lessor to give notice that the term hereof shall be forfeited and void and thereupon all of the right, title and interest of the Lessee hereunder shall expire and cease absolutely and the Lessee shall thereupon quit and surrender the Premises to the Lessor.

PROVIDED THAT:  The Lessor shall not re-enter and forfeit this lease by reason of the Lessee's default unless and until the Lessor shall have given to the Lessee at least 30 business days' notice of its intention to do so and setting forth the default complained of and the Lessee shall have failed to remedy such default within such 30 day period;

 

 

7         On August 15, 1991 Burlington made the following demand for payment of arrears.

 

TAKE NOTICE pursuant to paragraph 14.(a) of the Lease that:

1.   You are in default  of your obligations under the Lease to pay rent in accordance with the terms thereof and that as of the date of this notice the amount owed for rent and additional rent under the Lease is as set forth in Schedule "A" hereto: and

 

2.   The Lease and the term thereof shall be forfeited and void and that the undersigned intend to re-enter and forfeit the Lease by reasons of the said default unless within 30 business days of the date of this notice, the said default has been remedied by payment of all rent and additional rent due to the date of payment.

 

 

8         There was no response to the demand and on January 10, 1992 Burlington filed a petition under s.28 of the Commercial Tenancy Act requesting that a summons be issued calling on the tenant to show cause why an order should not be made for delivering up possession of the premises to the landlords.  The petition did not specify whether Burlington was relying upon s.28(1)(a) or s.28(1)(b) of the Act but simply referred to s.28.  I shall return to this aspect of the matter later.

 

9         Section 28 of the Commercial Tenancy Act provides:

28.(1)    In case a tenant

(a)fails to pay his rent within 7 days of the time agreed on; or

(b)makes default in observing any covenant, term or condition of his tenancy, the default being of a character as to entitle the landlord to enter again or to determine the tenancy,

 

and wrongfully refuses or neglects, on demand made in writing, to pay the rent or to deliver the premises leased, which demand shall be served on the tenant or on some adult person on the land or, if vacant, be affixed to the dwelling or other building on the land, or on some portion of the fences, the landlord or his agent may apply to the registrar of the Supreme Court on affidavit

 

(c)setting forth the terms of the lease or occu­pancy;

(d)the amount of rent in arrears, and the time for which it is in arrears;

(e)producing the demand made for the payment of rent or delivery of the possession, and stat­ing the refusal of the tenant to pay the rent or to deliver up possession and the answer of the tenant, if an answer was made; and

(f)setting forth that the tenant has no right to set off or the reason for withholding pos­session, or setting forth the covenant, term or condition in performance of which default has been made, and the particulars of the forfeiture;

 

and on filing of the affidavit, the registrar shall issue a summons calling on the tenant, 3 days after service, to show cause why an order should not be made for delivering up possession of the premises to the landlord, and the summons shall be served in the same manner as the demand.

 

(2)  An application under subsection (1) shall be commenced at a registry of the Supreme Court located in the judicial district where the land is situated.

 

10        On January 29, 1992 an ex parte order was issued requiring Baseline to show cause why such an order should not be issued.

 

11        On February 13, 1992 Baseline made an assignment in bankruptcy which was filed with the British Columbia Division of the Official Receiver's office on February 14, 1992.  The Trustee in Bankruptcy has paid or tendered occupation rent in the amount of $11,000 per month.

 

12        The Trustee in Bankruptcy for Baseline claims the right of possession of the lands and premises under the lease pursuant to s.30(1)(k) of the Bankruptcy Act, R.S.C. 1985, c.B-3:

 

30.  (1)  The trustee may, with the permission of the inspectors, do all or any of the following things:

 

                             * * *

 

(k) elect to retain for the whole part of its unexpired term, or to assign, surrender or disclaim any lease of, or other temporary interest in, any property of the bankrupt; and

 

                             * * *

 

 

Burlington contends that the lease was forfeited.  The critical issue is whether the lease was terminated prior to the appointment of the Trustee in Bankruptcy.

 

13        In his reasons for judgment the learned judge below said, at p.154:

 

     In the present case, Baseline failed to pay its rent within 7 days of the time agreed upon under the lease and thus s.28(1)(a), rather than s.28(1(b), of the Commercial Tenancy Act was invoked. Therefore, as stated in Pridham Estates, "all the tenant need do to retain or remain in possession is 'to pay the rent' (and costs)." In fact, the demand issued by Burlington on August 15, 1991 expressly provides that the default in lease payments could be "remedied by payment of all rent and additional rent due to the date of payment." The terms of that demand were consistent with the conduct of Burlington. At no time did Burlington unequivocally request Baseline to deliver up possession of the lands and premises at issue.

 

     Burlington relies on Standard Trusts v. David Steele Ltd. (1921), 2 C.B.R. 183 (B.C.S.C.), [as affirmed by the Court of Appeal: (1922), 3 C.B.R. 141], to support the contention that a claim by a landlord for arrears under a lease may, in some circumstances, be deemed equivalent in law to re-entry. In that case, a landlord issued a writ of summons in an action claiming double the yearly value of certain land until possession of that land was given up by the tenant. Mr. Justice Murphy held that the lease was terminated and stated at pp.185-6:

 

"Moore v. Ullcoats Mining Co. [1908] 1 Ch. 575, 77 L.J. Ch. 282, is authority for the proposition that a writ claiming possession simpliciter and any further relief which is incidental to a claim for possession is equivalent to re-entry. The claim for double yearly value can only be valid if the lease is at an end. The language of sec. 14 [16?] of the Landlord and Tenant Act, R.S.B.C., 1911, ch. 126, is explicit on this point in my opinion. If so, the claim is one incident to a claim for possession."

 

     In Standard Trusts, unlike in the case at bar, the claim for rent due was incidental to a claim for pos­session.

 

     In the present case, no covenant in the lease was breached nor was the lease automatically terminated upon the bankruptcy of Baseline. Moreover, the demand made by Burlington on August 15, 1991 could not be said to be equivalent in law to re-entry. The demand was made in apparent anticipation of Baseline making payment of arrears, as it had done before, and did not amount to an unequivocal election to treat the lease as terminated.

 

     As the lease was not forfeited prior to the appoint­ment of the Trustee in Bankruptcy, the Trustee has the right to possess the leased land and premises under s.30(1)(k) of the Bankruptcy Act, and s.32(2) of the Commercial Tenancy Act.

 

 

14        In their factum the appellants submit that the learned judge below erred:

A.   in finding that the lease was not forfeited and void prior to the appointment of the Trustee in Bankruptcy, and

 

B.   in finding that the appellants were applying under s.28(1)(a) of the Commercial Tenancy Act and that Baseline could have remained in possession by paying the rent in arrears (and costs).

 

DISCUSSION:

15        With respect to ground A. the appellants submit that the chambers judge erred in finding that the lease was not forfeited and void prior to the appointment of the Trustee in Bankruptcy by the process of the breach of the covenant to pay rent by Baseline, the notice sent to Baseline by the appellants on August 15, 1991 and Baseline's subsequent inaction during the period of the notice.  They submitted, alternatively, that the lease came to an end when Burlington issued its petition pursuant to s.28 of the Commercial Tenancy Act which, Mr. Harney argued, constituted in law a re-entry, with the result that the lease was forfeited.  Mr. Harney conceded that if he could not satisfy us that the lease had been effectively terminated in one or the other of these two ways this appeal must fail.

 

16        I am not persuaded by the appellants' submissions with respect to ground A.

 

17        The appellants contend firstly that the chambers judge did not give effect to the default clause in the lease agreement between Burlington and Baseline.  Mr. Harney argued that the lease was terminated on the giving of the notice of August 15, 1991 or, at the latest, 31 business days thereafter, the arrears of rent not having been paid in the interval.

 

18        The first part of Clause 14(a) appears to support the appellants' submission that the lease would be forfeited and void upon the giving of the notice.  However, the latter portion of clause 14(a) provides that if the lessor intends to re-enter and forfeit the lease by reason of the lessee's default, it must give the lessee at least 30 business days' notice of its intention to re-enter and forfeit the lease and set out the default complained of.  The lessee would then have 30 business days to remedy the default, after which time the lessor would be entitled to proceed to re-enter the premises and forfeit the lease.  Clause 14(a), read in its entirety, clearly indicates that the lessor must do something beyond merely giving the notice it gave in order to re-enter and forfeit the lease.

 

19        If clause 14(a) of the lease ended before the term "PROVIDED THAT" and did not contain the words which follow, it might have been open to Burlington, Mr. Edgson argued, simply to give notice that the term shall be forfeited and thereupon the interest of the lessee would be at an end and it would be obliged to deliver up the premises.  But, he submitted, the saving provision, on its true construction, clearly contemplated and required a two step process calling for two notices.  The first would declare the term forfeit and the second, not to be acted upon by re-entering for 30 business days, would give notice "of its intention to do so", and during that period the lessee would have the right and opportunity to remedy the default.  No such second notice was given.

 

20        With respect to the notice itself, Mr. Edgson submitted that, as the lease cannot be forfeited twice, that notice, without more, cannot operate to forfeit the lease.  It is in essence a demand for the arrears of rent and, in the event of non-compliance, notice that the lease shall, in the future, be forfeited and that Burlington then intends to re-enter.  However, it could not do so without giving the second notice required by clause 14(a).

 

21        I think these submissions are sound.

 

22        In May of 1989 Burlington gave notice to Baseline pursuant to paragraph 14(a) of the lease that Baseline was in default of the payment of rent.  The letter of May 24, 1989, after alleging the default, stated:

 

You have thirty (30) business days to pay Burlington Northern $80,753.31 thereby curing the default or Burlington Northern shall re-enter the premises and declare the Lease forfeited. (emphasis added)

 

 

Notwithstanding the giving of this notice, Burlington took no steps to re-enter and forfeit the lease and in August of 1989 the arrears were paid.

 

23        For convenience I will set out here again the critical wording of the notice Burlington gave Baseline on August 15, 1991, which, after stating Baseline was default for non-payment of rent, continued:

 

The Lease and term thereof shall be forfeited and void and that the undersigned intend to re-enter and forfeit the Lease by reason of the said default unless within 30 business days of the date of this notice, the said default has been remedied by payment of all rent and additional rent due to the date of payment. (emphasis added)

 

 

24        The language of both notices is prospective.  In each Burlington referred to a future act or future intention which, in the event, did not come about.  In both cases the continued occupancy by Baseline during the 30 day period was contemplated by the notice.  As such, the notice of August 15, 1991 cannot amount to a forfeiture of the lease.  See Re Simpson and Young & Biggins Ltd., [1972] 1 O.R. 103 (C.A.); Foreshore Projects Ltd. v. Warner Shelter Corp. (1983), 49 B.C.L.R. 26 (B.C. Co. Ct.).

 

25        It is possible to terminate a tenancy upon the terms set out in the lease without resort to the provisions of the Commercial Tenancy Act but here the appellants did not do so.  The demand letter of August 15, 1991 was not sufficient to terminate the lease and the appellants took no further steps to do so until January 10, 1992 when they filed a petition, not under s.18 but under s.28 of the Commercial Tenancy Act.

 

26        Section 18 provides:

     18.(1) In case a tenant, after his lease or right of occupation, whether created in writing or verbally, has expired, or been determined, either by the landlord or by the tenant, by a notice to quit or notice under the lease or agreement, or has been determined by any other act whereby a tenancy or right of occupancy may be determined or put an end to, wrongfully refuses, on written demand, to go out of possession of the land leased to him, or which he has been permitted to occupy, his landlord may apply to the Supreme Court

 

(a)setting out in an affidavit the terms of the lease or right of occupation, if verbal;

 

(b)annexing a copy of the instrument creating or containing the lease or right of occupation, if in writing;

 

(c)if a copy cannot be annexed by reason of it being mislaid, lost or destroyed, or of being in possession of the tenant, or from any other cause, then annexing a statement setting forth the terms of the lease or occupation, and the reason why a copy cannot be annexed;

 

(d)annexing a copy of the demand made for deli­vering possession, stating the refusal of the tenant to go out of possession, and the rea­sons given for his refusal, if any; and

 

(e)any explanation in regard to the refusal.

 

     (2)  This section extends and shall be construed to apply to tenancies from week to week, from month to month, from year to year, and tenancies at will, as well as to all other terms, tenancies, holdings or occupa­tions.

 

     (3)  An application under subsection (1) shall be commenced at a registry of the Supreme Court located in the judicial district where the land is situated.

 

 

27        With reference to these two sections of the Act Wong C.C.J. (as he then was) said, in Foreshore Products Ltd. v. Warner Shelter Corp. (1983), 49 B.C.L.R. 26 at 28-29:

 

     Summary dispositions of conflicting claims between landlord and tenant are provided by two separate pro­cedures outlined in ss.18 and 28 of the Commercial Tenancy Act. Both sections are to be construed strictly: see Gulf Oil Can. Ltd. v. Persall (1977), 75 D.L.R. (3d) 471 (B.C. Co. Ct.), and cases cited therein at p.473. In addition, forfeitures are regarded with disfavour by the courts and their upholding will be avoided even for trifling reasons: Big Valley Collieries v. MacKinnon (1915), 9 W.W.R. 4, 23 D.L.R. 62 (Alta. T.D.).

 

     If a landlord complains of a breach of covenant and seeks an order for possession, he must elect from the start whether he is going to proceed under s.18 or s.28. The crucial difference between the two sections is that s.18 requires a determination or end to the lease on the part of the landlord by notice, before he can move under s.18; but no such step to end the lease is required before the landlord can proceed under s.28: Misarol Farms Ltd. v. Lemer (Prelutsky) (1978), 6 B.C.L.R. 170 at 173, 5 R.P.R. 178 (Co. Ct.).

 

     Since it is a condition precedent to the jurisdic­tion of this court under s.18 of the Commercial Tenancy Act that the lease must first be terminated and then followed with a demand for possession, do the letters of 8th and 29th March and 20th April 1983 from the land­lord's solicitors to the tenant constitute determination?

 

     It is clear that the letter of 8th March 1983 is merely an indicated intention on the part of the landlord to forfeit the lease if conformed use of the premises by the tenant is not forthcoming.

 

     In Re Simpson and Young & Biggin Ltd. (1971), 22 D.L.R. (3d) 291 (Ont. C.A.), it was held that a notice by a landlord terminating a lease for non-payment of taxes and demanding possession on a fixed date about a month ahead was not sufficient to forfeit the lease since the notice itself contemplated continued occupancy by the tenant. Such also is the situation here with the letter of 29th March 1983 granting continued possession until 13th April 1983.

 

     The letter of 20th April 1983 is nothing more than a demand for possession. Although the demand for pos­session is also a condition precedent to the right to invoke s.18, it cannot be served until after the lease has been terminated: Re Anderson (1966), 56 W.W.R. 30, 57 D.L.R. (2d) 561 (B.C. Co. Ct.).

 

     Therefore, at law, I must conclude that the pur­ported determination is not valid and a necessary step to invoke s.18 has not been taken. Accordingly, on this application under s.18, the court is without jurisdic­tion.

 

 

28        Mr. Harney stated in the course of his argument that Burlington elected to proceed under s.28 rather than s.18 because, by doing so, it would not have the burden of proving that the lease had been terminated by the notice of August 15, 1991.  For the reasons which I have given, that notice did not terminate the lease, nor did it demand possession; it was nothing more than a demand for rent and a statement of intention to forfeit the lease and re-enter.

 

29        It is incongruous for the appellants to suggest that the lease was terminated and then bring its action pursuant to s.28 of the Commercial Tenancy Act.  An application under s.28 of the Commer­cial Tenancy Act presupposes that the tenancy has not been determined.  In the result, until an order for possession is made, Baseline was still a tenant and, at the time of the application before the learned chambers judge, the Trustee in Bankruptcy had been appointed and s.32 of the Commercial Tenancy Act and s.30(1)(k) of the Bankruptcy Act applied.

 

30        Mr. Harney then argued, in the alternative, that by institut­ing proceedings under s.28 the appellants had in law effected a re-entry and thereby brought the lease to an end.  He referred to Robinson, Little & Co. (Trustees of) v. Marlowe Yeoman Ltd. (1986), 5 B.C.L.R. (2d) 67 (B.C.C.A.) where Craig J.A. said, at 71:

 

     Paragraph 7(a) of the lease provides that the landlord may determine the lease by re-entry if the lessee breaches any of the conditions specified in the paragraph. Mr. Mass, the associate counsel for the appellant on the appeal and solicitor for the appellant, forwarded a notice to quit to the trustee on 20th June stating that because of the breach, the lease was "terminated immediately". The following day he forwarded a demand for possession. At first blush, one might reasonably think that this would be tantamount to re-entry, but English and Canadian text writers on the law of landlord and tenant, citing cases, state that a notice to quit and demand for possession is not tantamount to re-entry; Moore v. Ullcoats Mining Co., [1908] 1 Ch. 575; Prudential Ins. Co. of Amer. v. McLean, [1943] O.R. 377, [1943] 3 D.L.R. 307 (C.A.); Chernec v. Smith, [1946] 3 D.L.R. 765, [1946] O.W.N. 513 (C.A.); Winbaum v. Ginou, [1947] O.R. 242, [1947] 2 D.L.R. 619. In Prudential Ins. Co. v. McLean, the Ontario Court of Appeal pointed out that if a lease provides for forfeiture by re-entry, the landlord's right to re-enter can be exercised "... only by an actual re-entry, or by the issue of a writ ..." or by taking some proceeding under a statutory authority to recover possession.

 

 

 

31        Mr. Harney relies on the words underlined.  In this case, however, the petition did not consitute re-entry because it was brought under s.28 of the Act which clearly contemplates the continuance of the lease.  Mr. Harney referred as well to Prudential Insurance Co. v. McLean, [1943] O.R. 377, [1943] 3 D.L.R. 307, a decision of the Ontario Court of Appeal, but in my view that case is distinguishable.  It turned upon the provi­sions of s.17 of the Landlord and Tenant Act, R.S.O. 1937, c.219 which read:

17.─(1)   In every demise, whether by parol or in writing and whenever made, unless it is otherwise agreed, there shall be deemed to be included an agreement that if the rent reserved, or any part thereof, shall remain unpaid for fifteen days after any of the days on which the same ought to have been paid, although no formal demand thereof shall have been made, it shall be lawful for the landlord at any time thereafter, into and upon the demised premises, or any part thereof in the name of the whole, to re-enter and the same to have again, re-possess and enjoy as of his former estate. (emphasis added)

 

 

At p.382 Robertson C.J.O. said:

 

 

The right that the landlord has under s.17 of The Landlord and Tenant Act is a right to re-enter. This right can be exercised only by an actual re-entry, or by the issue of a writ, or taking such a proceeding as the present to recover possession. Notice of his intention to re-enter for non-payment of rent need not be given by the landlord (s.18, subs.2), and where given it is not effective to put an end to the lease when a re-entry is prescribed: Moore v. Ullcoats Mining Company, Limited, [1908] 1 Ch. 575 at 588. When the landlord has in fact re-entered, or has done the equivalent by taking proceed­ings to eject the tenant, his determination to exercise his right of forfeiture so evidenced is irrevocable.

 

 

There is no equivalent to s.17 in the Commercial Tenancy Act.

 

 

32        Further, the Prudential case is distinguishable because the lease there in question did not contain any provision equivalent to clause 14(a) of the lease before us.  Had it done so s.17 of the Ontario Act would not have applied.

 

33        I am unable to accept Mr. Harney's alternative submission.  In Moore v. Ullcoats Mining Co., supra, Warrington J. at 587 stated the applicable principle thus:

 

. . . the writ, in order to be equivalent to re-entry under the proviso for re-entry, must be an unequivocal demand for possession and nothing else.

 

 

34        Where a lease has not been effectively terminated, as is the case here, the institution of proceedings under s.28 is not an "unequivocal demand for possession" because, by reason of s.29(3), the tenant, where the default is non-payment of rent, has the right before enforcement of the order to pay up and have the proceedings stayed.  If Mr. Harney's position were correct, the tenant's rights under s.29(3) would be rendered nugatory.

 

35        In Pridham Estates Ltd. v. Janowski, [1968] 66 W.W.R. 695 (B.C. Co. Ct.), Washington C.C.J. said, at 697:

 

     It is clear from the wording of sec.29 [now s.28] that the landlord can proceed in two "cases" as set out in (a) and (b). But it is important to note that the section has, after the "case" in (a) and before the "case in (b) the word "or."

 

     So, a careful reading of the section, I feel, discloses quite clearly that if the landlord proceeds under sec.29 (a) then his demand must be one "to pay the rent." If the landlord proceeds under sec. 29(b) then his demand must be one "to deliver up the demised premises."

 

     Quite conceivably there could be no rent in arrears, or rent in arrears for a period of less than seven days; while at the same time the tenant has made default in observing a "covenant, term or condition of his tenancy * * * of such a character as to entitle the landlord to re-enter or to determine the tenancy." Obviously in such a "case" the landlord could not proceed under sec. 29 (a) but could proceed under sec. 29 (b).

 

     Moreover, if the landlord proceeds under sec. 29 (a), all the tenant need do to retain or remain in possession is "to pay the rent" (and costs); while if the landlord proceeds under sec. 29 (b) and is successful, mere payment of any rent in arrears would not entitle the tenant to remain in possession. This, I think, is made abundantly clear in sec. 30(2) of the Act.

 

 

36        Further, by refraining from specifying whether it was proceeding under s.28(1)(a) or s.28(1)(b) of the Act, and invoking s.28 generally, Burlington demonstrated the equivocal nature of its position.

 

37        The second ground of appeal is a submission that the chambers judge erred in finding that the appellants were applying under s.28(1)(a) of the Commercial Tenancy Act and that Baseline could have remained in possession by paying the rent in arrears and costs.

 

38        This submission is founded upon the proposition set out in the appellants' factum thus:

 

14.  This finding ignores the fact that the term of the Lease was forfeit and void following the notice of August 15, 1991 and the passage of 30 business days after the notice. There was no lease in existence at the time of the assignment in bankruptcy. The Appellants petitioned for occupation of the Premises, not for payment of the rent in arrears.

 

 

39        In view of the conclusion I have reached with respect to the effect of the notice of August 15, 1991, I must reject this ground of appeal.

 

40        In any event, since the only alleged default was non-payment of rent, whether the appellants were proceeding under s.28(1)(a) or s.28(1)(b), Baseline always had the right before the enforcement of any order for possession to pay the arrears of rents and all costs and thereby be permitted to continue in its former tenancy.  Therefore, the proceeding by the appellants pursuant to s.28 of the Commercial Tenancy Act based upon an alleged breach of non-payment of rent cannot be said to be an unequivocal election by the appellants to treat the lease as terminated and an action for possession simpliciter.  As such, the lease would not be terminated by the commencement of the action.

 

41        In my opinion the learned chambers judge did not err.

 

42        I would dismiss the appeal.
