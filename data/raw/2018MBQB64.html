GREENBERG J.

[1]              The defendants seek summary judgment dismissing the plaintiffs’ claim.  The claim relates to negotiations between the parties towards the purchase by the defendant, Winnipeg Exclusive Bus Tours Inc. (“WE”), of the assets of the plaintiff, Free Enterprise Bus Lines Inc. (“FE”).  In their claim, the plaintiffs allege that a binding agreement of sale was concluded but was not honoured by the defendants.  Alternatively, they allege that the defendants are liable in damages for representing to the plaintiffs that a contract would be concluded.  The defendants say that the claim does not raise a genuine issue requiring a trial.

background

[2]              The evidence presented on this motion shows the following.[1]  The plaintiffs Hernan Silva and Vickie Comeau are a married couple who owned and operated FE, a charter bus business.  The defendant Walt Morris owns WE, a charter and tour bus business.  At the relevant times, the defendants Maisie Hicks and Carolyne Braid were employed by WE.

[3]              Sometime in 2013, Silva and Comeau made a decision to either sell or close down FE.  In October 2013, while attending a legislative committee hearing, they were introduced to Morris.  They understood that Morris was interested in buying a bus company and asked whether he would be interested in buying FE.  There followed discussions and the exchange of information between the parties.   

[4]              At the time, FE held a licence under the Motor Vehicle Transport Act, R.S.C., 1985, c. 29 (3rd Supp.), to operate an extra-provincial bus undertaking.  It owned three motor coaches and leased one.  Although WE also operated a tour bus company, it did not own any buses, nor did it have a licence to operate a bus company.  It carried out its contracts by renting buses from other licensed operators. However, Morris was interested in acquiring a licence and that could only be accomplished through the purchase of a company that held a licence.

[5]              Most of the negotiations regarding the sale of FE to WE were conducted between Comeau, on behalf of FE, and Hicks, on behalf of WE.  But Comeau knew that Morris owned WE and that any discussions between her and Hicks had to be run by him.  Hicks told Comeau that Morris would not accept their asking price of $400,000 and that the price would have to be negotiated.

[6]              On January 20, 2014, while negotiations were ongoing between the parties, Comeau received an email from Canadian National (“CN”) with a Request for Proposals (“RFP”) regarding a contract to provide transportation services for CN employees.  Comeau had never done business with CN and did not know why FE received the RFP.  As FE was going out of business, she had no intention to bid on the contract.  Comeau immediately forwarded the RFP to Hicks with the caption “Good Luck to you and make it happen”. 

[7]              On January 27, 2014, at Hicks’ request, Comeau signed a letter stating that FE released all interest in quoting on the CN contract and all interest in any contract should WE be successful in its bid.  WE sent in its bid to CN on January 31, 2014 along with a covering letter which stated that WE provided charter bus service “in partnership” with FE and that it had four buses in its fleet.  The letter was signed by Morris, Hicks, Silva and Comeau. 

[8]              At a meeting between the parties on February 24, 2014, Morris presented Silva and Comeau with a “Term Sheet” which had been prepared by Morris’ lawyer and which Silva and Comeau signed.  The Term Sheet states that WE would purchase the assets of FE subject to financing and due diligence. The Term Sheet also contained the following clause:

Upon signing the Term Sheet, the parties will diligently negotiate the terms and conditions of a definitive purchase agreement documenting the business terms highlighted above and other representations, warranties, conditions, covenants and indemnities which are usual for a transaction of this nature.  Hernan Silva and Vickie Comeau (the “shareholders”) shall be jointly and severally liable with [FE] for [FE] representations, warranties and indemnities.  Except as set forth in the next paragraph, the parties intend that no legal rights or obligations between them will come into existence until the definitive agreement is executed and delivered by the parties.

[emphasis added]

 

[9]              The next paragraph in the Term Sheet was a “lock up clause” which stated that FE would continue operating in the normal course but could not enter into any significant contracts without WE’s consent and could not discuss the sale of their business to anyone else.  The lock up clause stated that it would terminate on April 15, 2014 (the closing date) if a definitive purchase agreement had not been executed by that date.

[10]         When Morris presented the Term Sheet to Comeau and Silva, the purchase price was blank.  After discussions between them, Morris handwrote the price on the Term Sheet as $150,000 plus “commission on CN 6 yr. contract 5% of sale to Vickie and Hernan. If no CN contract purchase price will be $180,000.”  In March, WE was in fact awarded the CN contract.

[11]         Silva and Comeau did not have a lawyer when they signed the Term Sheet but subsequently retained Jim Saper to represent them.  Following the signing of the Term Sheet, WE’s lawyers sent to Mr. Saper a number of drafts of an asset purchase agreement, a management agreement, a non-competition agreement, and a commission agreement. 

[12]         On March 26, 2014, Hicks had a meeting with FE’s drivers where she told them that WE had a deal to purchase FE and asked the drivers if they wished to stay on.  At her examination for discovery, Hicks acknowledged that, as no deal had in fact been reached at this point, her statements to the drivers were misleading.

[13]         No agreements were ever signed by the parties.  In her examination for discovery, Comeau said that she declined to sign the agreements drafted by WE’s lawyers on the advice of her lawyer.  She also said that she was not prepared to conclude the transaction until the paperwork was in a form acceptable to her.  No evidence was led by the plaintiffs as to what aspects of the draft agreements were unacceptable and, in particular, whether the terms to which they objected were, in their view, essential or non-essential terms of the agreements. 

[14]         In any event, the draft management agreement that WE’s lawyers had sent to FE’s lawyer provided that WE would manage all aspects of FE’s business commencing on April 1, 2014 until the earliest of September 30, 2014 and the date FE’s licence was transferred to WE.  The agreement provided for adjustments and the return of records if the purchase agreement did not close.  Although the management agreement was never signed, WE did begin to manage FE’s business on April 1, 2014, with the assistance of Comeau.  And when WE began to perform the CN contract on April 6, 2014, they used FE’s buses and drivers.

[15]         On April 30, 2014, Comeau sent a text message to Morris expressing concern about the progress of the negotiations:

I think we need some kind if [sic] resolution.  I know that you wanted the buses inspected but this is not working for us anymore.  We have proved ourselves over and over again and we still are being questioned.  It’s not working for us.

 

[16]         On May 1, 2014, several text messages were exchanged between Comeau and Morris regarding the commission on the CN contract:

Hi Walt, here is option 3 or 4.  I lost track now.  If we were to stick around and receive the CN contract commission it would be at least $1800.00 a month.  Pay us out and you won’t have to deal with us again.  So you pay the 75,000.00 and then buy us out if [sic] the CN.  Thoughts?

 

[17]         On May 2, 2014, Comeau sent this text to Morris:

Hi Walt, we really need an answer today, if not, we’ll take that as the deal is off.  We need to make arrangements and move on.

 

[18]         An hour later, Morris responded:

Well, with sadness we are not going to complete this deal.  This deal is off.

 

[19]         Comeau immediately replied:

No problem.  I will invoice you for the past month of using the buses.

 

[20]         Comeau then offered to make FE’s buses available to WE until WE bought its own.  On May 5, 2014, Comeau emailed Hicks asking that the financial documents that she had sent to WE be returned or destroyed.  She also asked for copies of fuel receipts and drivers’ pay so that she could determine what WE owed it “for the last month of CN bus rentals.”    WE did pay FE for the use of FE’s buses.

[21]         Comeau claims that throughout the negotiations she received assurances from both Morris and Hicks that they wanted to make the deal work.   In particular, in her affidavit, she states that Morris and Hicks made representations at the February 24, 2014, meeting, when the Term Sheet was signed, that WE would be purchasing the assets of FE; that Silva and her would be hired to work for WE; that there would be a commission agreement with respect to the CN contract; and that WE would be managing FE pending the transfer of FE’s licence to WE.  She said that it was those representations that prompted her to sign the Term Sheet.  However, at her examination for discovery, which took place almost two years before she signed the affidavit, she said that she did not remember details of the February 24, 2014 meeting and did not remember whether Hicks was present.

[22]         While Comeau says that she thought they had a deal with WE, she knew that no deal would be completed until WE had conducted due diligence.  A consent to due diligence was sent to her lawyer on March 31, 2014.  The text messages referred to above suggest that, as of May 1, 2014, WE had not completed its inspection of FE’s buses.  And, as of that date, the parties continued to negotiate essential terms, including the contract price. 

[23]         There is not a lot of evidence regarding Braid’s involvement in the negotiations.  She attended some of the meetings with Comeau and Silva and, with input from Morris and Hicks, she drafted the cover letter that WE sent to CN with their bid.  She no longer works for WE.

 

causes of action

[24]         The plaintiffs allege three causes of action.  The primary cause of action is based on breach of contract.  FE alleges that WE agreed to buy FE’s assets and that WE breached that agreement by failing to follow through with the purchase.  FE has since sold its assets to a third party for substantially less than the purchase price set out in the Term Sheet and it claims damages for the difference.

[25]         The claims against the individual defendants Morris, Hicks and Braid are based on the torts of negligent misrepresentation and conspiracy.  The plaintiffs claim that, even if no contract was reached, the three defendants should be liable to pay damages to the plaintiffs for representing to them that WE would be buying FE’s assets, a representation on which the plaintiffs say they relied.  The plaintiffs also claim that the three defendants should be liable in the tort of conspiracy because they agreed to lie to CN in order to obtain the CN contract and, in doing so, caused harm to the plaintiffs.   

[26]         A fourth cause of action raised in the claim, unlawful interference with economic interests, is not being pursued by the plaintiffs.

 

the test on summary judgment

 

[27]         Amendments to the Queen’s Bench Rules, Man. Reg. 553/88, as amended by Man. Reg. 130/17, which came into effect in January of this year, make significant changes to the summary judgment process.  The new rules are designed to promote proportionality to ensure “the just, most expeditious and least expensive determination of every civil proceeding on its merits” (Rule 1.04(1)) and reflect the Supreme Court’s direction in Hryniak v. Mauldin, 2014 SCC 7 (para. 2), [2014] 1 S.C.R. 87, that “a culture shift is required in order to create an environment promoting timely and affordable access to the civil justice system.”

[28]         Manitoba’s new summary judgment rules are similar to the Ontario summary judgment rules interpreted by the Court in Hryniak.  In that case, Karakatsanis J. explained (at para. 45) that the Ontario rules “are designed to transform Rule 20 from a means to weed out unmeritorious claims to a significant alternative model of adjudication.”  To that end, the test on a summary judgment motion is no longer whether there is a “genuine issue for trial” but whether there is a “genuine issue requiring a trial” (Rule 20.07(1)).   The traditional trial is no longer the default position but should be pursued only where the judge cannot “achieve a fair and just adjudication of the issues” on the basis of the evidence produced on the summary judgment motion (Rule 20.03(5)).  

[29]         To facilitate the use of the summary judgment motion as an alternative means of adjudication, the rules contemplate more flexibility in the type of evidence tendered and authorize the judge to weigh evidence, evaluate credibility of deponents and draw reasonable inferences (Rule 20.07(2)).  Whereas under the former rules issues of credibility usually required a trial, the new rules facilitate the resolution of those issues on summary judgment.  A trial is not required unless the judge is not confident making the necessary fact findings on the basis of the evidence presented and tools available on the motion.  As explained in Hryniak:

[56]     While I agree that a motion judge must have an appreciation of the evidence necessary to make dispositive findings, such an appreciation is not only available at trial. Focussing on how much and what kind of evidence could be adduced at a trial, as opposed to whether a trial is "requir[ed]" as the Rule directs, is likely to lead to the bar being set too high. The interest of justice cannot be limited to the advantageous features of a conventional trial, and must account for proportionality, timeliness and affordability. Otherwise, the adjudication permitted with the new powers -- and the purpose of the amendments -- would be frustrated.

[57]     On a summary judgment motion, the evidence need not be equivalent to that at trial, but must be such that the judge is confident that she can fairly resolve the dispute. A documentary record, particularly when supplemented by the new fact-finding tools, including ordering oral testimony, is often sufficient to resolve material issues fairly and justly. The powers provided in Rules 20.04(2.1) and 20.04(2.2) can provide an equally valid, if less extensive, manner of fact finding.

 

 

[30]         In Hryniak, the Court suggested that the new powers to evaluate the evidence and order oral evidence should only be engaged where the judge cannot decide the issues without resorting to those powers.  Karakatsanis J. said:

[66]     On a motion for summary judgment under Rule 20.04, the judge should first determine if there is a genuine issue requiring trial based only on the evidence before her, without using the new fact-finding powers. There will be no genuine issue requiring a trial if the summary judgment process provides her with the evidence required to fairly and justly adjudicate the dispute and is a timely, affordable and proportionate procedure, under Rule 20.04(2)(a). If there appears to be a genuine issue requiring a trial, she should then determine if the need for a trial can be avoided by using the new powers under Rules 20.04(2.1) and (2.2). She may, at her discretion, use those powers, provided that their use is not against the interest of justice. Their use will not be against the interest of justice if they will lead to a fair and just result and will serve the goals of timeliness, affordability and proportionality in light of the litigation as a whole.

 

[31]         And in the companion case, Bruno Appliance and Furniture, Inc. v. Hryniak, 2014 SCC 8, [2014] 1 S.C.R. 126, she said:

[22]     Summary judgment may not be granted under Rule 20 where there is a genuine issue requiring a trial. As outlined in the companion Mauldin appeal, the motion judge should ask whether the matter can be resolved in a fair and just manner on a summary judgment motion. This will be the case when the process (1) allows the judge to make the necessary findings of fact, (2) allows the judge to apply the law to the facts, and (3) is a proportionate, more expeditious and less expensive means to achieve a just result. If there appears to be a genuine issue requiring a trial, based only on the record before her, the judge should then ask if the need for a trial can be avoided by using the new powers provided under Rules 20.04(2.1) and (2.2). She may, at her discretion, use those powers, provided that their use is not against the interest of justice.

 

 

[32]         In my view, the two-step approach described in Hryniak is not consistent with the process contemplated by the Manitoba rules.  Unlike the Ontario rules considered in that case, the Manitoba rules require the motion judge to hold a summary judgment conference before the hearing where the judge may give directions as to the nature of the evidence, including directing that oral evidence be presented (Rules 20.03(6), 20.03(7)).  In Hryniak, at paras. 69 to 73, the Court suggested that a motion for directions before the hearing would allow early judicial involvement to manage the time and cost of a summary judgment motion.  The summary judgment conference under the Manitoba rules is a substitute for the motion for directions suggested in Hryniak and allows the judge to shepherd the process.  As the motion judge considers, in advance of the hearing, whether oral evidence would be helpful, it would not make sense that she would then, at the hearing, first consider whether the issues could be determined without the advantage of that oral evidence. 

[33]         The summary judgment conference is a significant piece of the new approach to summary judgment motions in Manitoba.  It requires the judge to consider whether a summary judgment motion can “achieve a fair and just adjudication of the issues” (Rule 20.03(5)), that is to say, whether the judge will be able to determine the factual and legal issues on the basis of the summary judgment process.  This screening process ensures the summary judgment process is not used inappropriately.  It avoids the expense of a failed summary judgment motion where the nature of the issues or the proposed evidence is such that the case would clearly be best resolved by a traditional trial with viva voce evidence (see e.g. Berscheid v. Federated Co-operatives et al, 2018 MBCA 27 (CanLII)). 

[34]         The summary judgment conference also allows the judge to consider whether, even if the case is conducive to resolution on a summary judgment motion, a motion would be no less expensive or expeditious than a trial.  Summary judgment motions are not inexpensive.  Affidavits must be prepared, cross-examinations pursued, transcripts ordered and briefs prepared. These costs must be kept in mind in considering the relative cost of a motion versus a trial.  

[35]         Finally, as mentioned above, the summary judgment conference allows the judge, with the input of counsel, to give directions regarding the evidence to be relied upon on the motion. For example, the judge may decide that certain witnesses should provide their evidence orally on the motion or that, while affidavit evidence may be relied upon, cross-examination on those affidavits should be conducted before the judge at the hearing as opposed to in the lawyer’s office.  But, if the judge feels that the case can only be decided with extensive oral evidence, it may be that a summary judgment motion is not the most expeditious way to proceed.  

[36]         The fact that a judge determines that the summary judgment process is not appropriate does not mean that a traditional trial is required.  Even where a case is proceeding to trial, the new Manitoba rules give pre-trial judges broad discretion to limit the nature of the evidence or the manner of its presentation to ensure the most expeditious and least expensive determination of an action (Rules 50. 05(3) and (4)).

[37]         The other significant piece of the new summary judgment process is that, if after hearing the motion, the judge finds that some or all of the issues require a trial, where practicable, she will remain seized of the file and will preside at the trial (Rule 20.10).  That is not to say that where a motion is dismissed, the judge must direct a full trial.  It should be remembered that the motion judge is involved in directing what evidence should be tendered at the motion and whether oral evidence should be called at that time.  However, it may be difficult to predict before the hearing what kind of record will be necessary to resolve the issues fairly. If the judge ultimately feels unable to decide the issues based on the evidence produced at the hearing, the failed motion may still be “salvaged”, to use the language in Hryniak.  Because the judge remains seized of the case, she can continue to rely on the evidence produced on the motion with direction to the parties as to what further evidence she would need to come to a decision.             

[38]         These two aspects of the new summary judgment rules - the summary judgment conference and the judge staying seized - create a judge-managed process.   The judge is involved at the “front end” to provide direction to the parties and to help ensure that the procedure is tailored to the requirements of the case.  While the parties no longer control the process, they benefit from the flexibility which the new process provides.   

[39]         The new procedure for summary judgment motions also means that the two-step analysis described by the court in Homestead Properties (Canada) Ltd. v. Sekhri et al., 2007 MBCA 61 (CanLII), no longer fits.  In this oft-quoted passage from that case, Freedman J.A. explained:

14     The test for summary judgment is well established, and no further detailed explanation is needed. The test is to the same effect regardless of whether the moving party is the plaintiff or the defendant. When the defendant moves, as here, he must prove, on a prima facie basis, that the plaintiff's action must fail. If he meets that burden, then the plaintiff has the burden to establish that there is a genuine issue for determination. He must show that his claim is "one with a real chance of success" (see Hercules Managements Ltd. v. Ernst & Young, [1997] 2 S.C.R. 165 at para. 15; see also Manitoba Hydro Electric v. Inglis (John) Co. et al. (1999), 142 Man.R. (2d) 1 (C.A.)). If he fails to do so, summary judgment dismissing the claim will follow.

 

 

[40]         Because the new summary judgment procedure is no longer designed to weed out claims but is rather an alternate means of resolving the case, a shifting onus no longer makes sense.  The respondent may still advance arguments as to why the matter must go to trial.  But those arguments should first be advanced at the summary judgment conference before the parties have gone to the expense of preparing the motion.   That is not to say that any formality needs to be introduced into the summary judgment conference but the need for a trial should be explained to the judge at that point so that she has the information necessary to determine if the summary judgment motion should proceed.  

[41]         Once the judge has screened out those cases that clearly require a trial, the judge should consider all of the evidence presented by the parties at the hearing in determining whether she can decide the issues fairly on the basis of that evidence or whether some type of further hearing is necessary.  In my view, it is not helpful to make this determination on the basis of a shifting onus.  I note that the Court in Hryniak made no reference to onus in discussing the test for summary judgment.  It would seem that to the extent that onus plays a role in the process, it is with respect to the substantive issues, as it is in a traditional trial.  

 

 

analysis

 

The Contract Claim

 

[42]         The plaintiffs claim that FE had a binding agreement with WE for the sale of FE’s assets which, although not committed to writing, was evidenced by the conduct of the   parties. 

[43]         The Manitoba Court of Appeal reviewed the requirements for an enforceable contract in Matic et al v Waldner et al, 2016 MBCA 60 (CanLII), application for leave to appeal to S.C.C. denied, [2016] S.C.C.A. No. 359 (QL).  Pfuetzner J.A. explained:

55     The standard for determining whether an agreement, written or oral, has been reached, is whether an "objective reasonable bystander", looking at all the material facts, would say so. GHL Fridman, The Law of Contract in Canada, 6th ed (Toronto: Carswell, 2011) describes the test as follows (at p 15):

Constantly reiterated in the judgments is the idea that the test of agreement for legal purposes is whether parties have indicated to the outside world, in the form of the objective reasonable bystander, their intention to contract and the terms of such contract. The law is concerned not with the parties' intentions but with their manifested intentions. It is not what an individual party believed or understood was the meaning of what the other party said or did that is the criterion of agreement; it is whether a reasonable man in the situation of that party would have believed and understood that the other party was consenting to the identical terms. As Fraser C.J.A. said in Ron Ghitter Property Consultants Ltd. v. Beaver Lumber Co. [2003 ABCA 221 at para 9, 330 AR 353]:

the parties will be found to have reached a meeting of the minds, in other words be ad idem, where it is clear to the objective reasonable bystander, in light of all the material facts, that the parties intended to contract and the essential terms of that contract can be determined with a reasonable degree of certainty.

[emphasis in original]

56     The requirements for the formation of a contract were described in Bawitko Investments Ltd v Kernels Popcorn Ltd (1991), 53 OAC 314, a leading decision on oral contracts. Robins JA wrote (at para 21):

However, when the original contract is incomplete because essential provisions intended to govern the contractual relationship have not been settled or agreed upon; or the contract is too general or uncertain to be valid in itself and is dependent on the making of a formal contract; or the understanding or intention of the parties, even if there is no uncertainty as to the terms of their agreement, is that their legal obligations are to be deferred until a formal contract has been approved and executed, the original or preliminary agreement cannot constitute an enforceable contract. In other words, in such circumstances the "contract to make a contract" is not a contract at all. The execution of the contemplated formal document is not intended only as a solemn record or memorial of an already complete and binding contract but is essential to the formation of the contract itself.

[emphasis added]

 

[44]         There is no dispute that an agreement need not be in writing to be binding.  The question is whether the evidence in this case establishes an enforceable oral agreement or only a “contract to make a contract”.

[45]         The following are undisputed facts.  Some time in the late fall of 2013, the parties entered into negotiations regarding the purchase by WE of FE’s assets. The plaintiffs initially asked for $400,000 but Morris rejected that offer. On February 24, 2014, the parties signed a Term Sheet that stipulated that WE would purchase FE’s assets for $150,000 plus a five percent commission on the CN contract.  The Term Sheet provided that the sale was subject to financing and due diligence and that no obligations would arise until a definitive agreement was executed.  Negotiations continued after the Term Sheet was signed.  WE’s lawyers sent draft agreements to FE’s lawyers, including an asset purchase agreement which set the sale price at $150,000 and a commission agreement whereby the plaintiffs would receive six percent commission on the CN contract, an amount greater than set out in the Term Sheet.  The plaintiffs refused to sign the agreements on the advice of their lawyer.  There is no evidence before me that the plaintiffs suggested amendments to the agreements and no evidence that the terms that were unacceptable were non-essential terms.  Although no written agreement was executed, on April 1, 2014, WE took over management of FE as contemplated by the draft management agreement.  In an exchange of text messages on May 2, 2014, Comeau asked for some confirmation as to whether the deal was on.  Morris texted that the deal was off and Comeau responded, “No problem”.  FE sent WE an invoice for the use of its buses in April 2014 and that invoice was paid.  

[46]         Notwithstanding the stipulation in the Term Sheet that no obligations would arise “until the definitive agreement is executed and delivered,” the plaintiffs say that the defendants conducted themselves as though a sale had been finalized.  They say that the objective reasonable bystander would believe that the parties intended to contract and that the essential terms of the contract were clear. 

[47]         In their written brief, the plaintiffs also rely on the letter sent to CN with WE’s bid as evidence that an agreement of sale had been concluded.  In that letter, sent on January 31, 2014, the parties stated that WE and FE were in partnership.  However, it is clear that, at that point in time, the parties had come to no agreement on the terms of sale and the Term Sheet signed subsequently makes it clear that the only agreement between the parties was an agreement to negotiate.  In oral argument, plaintiffs’ counsel said that he was relying on events after the Term Sheet was signed as evidencing a verbal agreement.  He argued that a reasonable bystander looking at the conduct of the parties would find that the parties intended to contract.  But the reasonable bystander would have all of the material facts, including the stipulation in the Term Sheet that no obligations arose until a definitive agreement was executed and delivered.

[48]         Moreover, in order to find the existence of a binding contract, the essential terms must be reasonably certain.  The text messages between the parties on May 1 and 2, 2014 show that the contract price, a term usually considered to be essential, was still being negotiated.    

[49]         While Comeau relies on representations made to her that a sale would occur, it is clear from the statements in her examination for discovery that she never believed an agreement had been concluded:

Q : …There was no final deal as of April 1, 2014, correct?

A:  Not written.

Q:  Not at all.

A:  Not written.

Q:  Not written.  And what were the terms of a final agreement on April 1, 2014?

A:  There were no final terms.  There were no final terms discussed.  We didn’t come to that point that there was – that the terms were all written out.  There was no final agreement with that.

Q:  Right, but there was nothing --

A:  Written.

Q: Okay.  You are suggesting that there was something verbal?

A: It was always promised to us, and it was always led us to believe that this was going to be resolved, that we were going to have the sale.

Q: I understand your position is that you were led to believe that there was going to be a sale, right?

A: Yes.

Q: But that sale had to be on terms and conditions that were acceptable to you, correct?

A: Correct.

Q: So, by April 1, 2014, you didn’t have terms and conditions that were acceptable to you, correct?

A: No

Q: The only thing you are saying you had of that day was, we’re going to try to make this happen, is that fair?

A: That that’s what they had said?

Q: Yes.

A:  Yes, that we were going to try to make this happen, yes.

 

[50]         The plaintiffs point to the fact that WE had taken over management of FE on April 1, 2014, as evidence of a binding contract.  But, as I said, the parties were still negotiating the essential terms.  I note that the draft management agreement contemplated that WE would take over management before an agreement was executed but provided for an accounting of earnings and expenses if the purchase agreement did not close.

[51]         The following comments in Ward v. Ward, 2011 ONCA 178 (CanLII), at para. 53, cited with approval in Matic, at para. 61, are apt:

At common law, an agreement is binding if the parties consider that it contains all essential terms, even if the parties also agree that those terms will subsequently be recorded in a more formal document together with the usual terms ancillary to that type of agreement. However, an agreement is not final or binding if it is merely an agreement to later agree on essential provisions, or to defer the binding nature of the agreement until the execution of the proposed subsequent formal contract.  

 

 

[52]         The plaintiffs argue that a trial is required to determine whether there was an agreement because all of the relevant evidence is not before the court.  In particular, the plaintiffs say they would call Jim Saper at the trial.  Mr. Saper represented the plaintiffs in the negotiations and may have relevant evidence.  But the plaintiffs cannot respond to a summary judgment motion by arguing that they will be able to prove their case at trial.  They must put their best case forward now (Rule 20.05).  Counsel was not able to explain at the hearing why an affidavit from Mr. Saper was not filed on this motion.

[53]         The plaintiffs also say that the evidence on this motion raises issues of credibility that cannot be resolved without hearing from the parties at trial.  Counsel for the plaintiffs points to the letter to CN where WE alleged it was in partnership with FE.  In his examination for discovery, Morris admitted this was not true.  When pressed, he agreed it was a “lie”.  But, in her examination for discovery, Comeau also admitted signing the letter knowing the contents were not true.   It appears that both parties were prepared to distort the truth to bolster WE’s bid for the CN contract.  But to the extent that these “lies” are relevant, they are apparent on the evidence before me.  Further examination at a trial would add nothing.  

[54]         Counsel for the plaintiffs also points to discrepancies between the evidence in the affidavits and the evidence at the examinations for discovery which he says can only be sorted out at a trial.  What is curious about this argument is that the discrepancies are primarily in the evidence of his own client.  In particular, there are a number of discrepancies between statements in Comeau’s affidavit and statements that she made two years earlier at her examination for discovery. Even assuming that a party can raise her own credibility as a matter warranting a trial, I am confident that I do not need a trial to make the findings necessary to determine the issues.  As set out above, the factors that are key to determining if there was an agreement in this case are not contentious.

[55]         I am satisfied I can reach a fair and just determination of the contract issue without a trial.  I am confident that I have the evidence necessary to determine that the parties never concluded an agreement for the sale of FE’s assets to WE. 

Negligent Misrepresentation

[56]         The five elements of the tort of negligent misrepresentation are set out by the Supreme Court in Queen v. Cognos Inc., [1993] 1 S.C.R. 87 (at para. 33):

(1)      there must be a duty of care based on a "special relationship" between   the representor and the representee;

(2)      the representation in question must be untrue, inaccurate, or       misleading;

(3)      the representor must have acted negligently in making said         misrepresentation;

(4)      the representee must have relied, in a reasonable manner, on said         negligent misrepresentation; and

(5)      the reliance must have been detrimental to the representee in the          sense that damages resulted.

 

[57]         In Cognos, the Court found the tort had been established where the plaintiff entered a contract of employment to work for the defendant as a result of representations made to him during the job interview that the project for which he was being hired would last a minimum of two years.  The plaintiff gave up a lucrative job and moved his family halfway across the country only to be laid off a year later.  The Court found that the defendant had a duty not to represent to the plaintiff that the job offered was a secure one when they knew or should have known it was not.  

[58]         The plaintiffs here argue that the defendants owed a duty of care to them based on a special relationship between them; that the defendants represented to the plaintiffs on numerous occasions that they were going to purchase FE’s assets and pay them a commission on the CN contract; that these representations were untrue or misleading and the defendants were negligent in making them; and that the plaintiffs reasonably relied on those representations to their detriment.

[59]         The plaintiffs argue that the defendants owed a duty of care to them because the plaintiffs were business neophytes while the defendants were sophisticated business persons.  However, the test for establishing a duty of care is far more complex.  In Deloitte & Touche v. Livent Inc. (Receiver of), 2017 SCC 63, [2017] 2 S.C.R. 795, the Court explained:

[29]     Where an established proximate relationship cannot be found, courts must undertake a full proximity analysis. To determine whether the "'close and direct' relationship which is the hallmark of the common law duty of care" exists (Saadati v. Moorhead, 2017 SCC 28, [2017] 1 S.C.R. 543, at para. 24, citing Cooper, at para. 32, and Donoghue v. Stevenson, [1932] A.C. 562 (H.L.), at pp. 580-81), courts must examine all relevant "factors arising from the relationship between the plaintiff and the defendant" (Cooper, at para. 30 (emphasis in original); Edwards, at para. 9; Childs, at para. 24; Odhavji, at para. 50; Hill, at para. 24; Fullowka, at para. 26; Saadati, at para. 24). While these factors are diverse and depend on the circumstances of each case (Cooper, at para. 35), this Court has maintained that they include "expectations, representations, reliance, and the property or other interests involved" (ibid., at para. 34; Odhavji, at para. 50; Fullowka, at para. 26) as well as any statutory obligations (Cooper, at para. 38; Edwards, at paras. 9 and 13; Odhavji, at para. 56).

 

[60]         In cases, like Cognos, where a duty of care has been found, there was a contractual relationship between the parties.  The argument in that case was that the plaintiff relied on representations to enter the contract and that the “representations made in a pre-contractual setting did not correspond with the post-agreement reality” (Cognos, at para. 36).  Unlike Cognos and the other cases relied upon by the plaintiffs (Smith v. Union of Icelandic Fish Producers, 2005 NSCA 145 (CanLII); TRC Enterprises v. Tobmar Newstands Inc. et al, 2010 MBQB 112 (CanLII)), there was no contractual relationship between the parties here.  

[61]         Even assuming that a duty of care can be found between parties who are negotiating a contract, the evidence does not support the other elements of the tort. First, the impugned representations here relate to the intention of the defendants to contract. There is debate in the case law as to whether representations relating to the intent of the parties, as opposed to matters of fact, can found an action in tort (see Smith at paras. 75-76).  Second, the evidence does not support the allegations that the representations were untrue or misleading.  Draft agreements, approved by Morris, were sent by his lawyers to the plaintiffs’ lawyer.  It was the plaintiffs who chose not to sign the draft agreements. 

[62]         The evidence before me does not establish negligent misrepresentation nor is there anything before me that suggests that further evidence is needed to resolve this issue.

 

Conspiracy

[63]         Two types of conspiracy are recognized in Canadian tort law – predominant purpose conspiracy and unlawful means conspiracy. They are explained in Pro-Sys Consultants Ltd. v. Microsoft Corporation, 2013 SCC 57, [2013] 3 S.C.R. 477:

[74]     Predominant purpose conspiracy is made out where the predominant purpose of the defendant's conduct is to cause injury to the plaintiff using either lawful or unlawful means, and the plaintiff does in fact suffer loss caused by the defendant's conduct. Where lawful means are used, if their object is to injure the plaintiff, the lawful acts become unlawful (Canada Cement LaFarge Ltd. v. British Columbia Lightweight Aggregate Ltd., [1983] 1 S.C.R. 452, at pp. 471-72).

. . .

[80]     The second type of conspiracy, called "unlawful means conspiracy", requires no predominant purpose but requires that the unlawful conduct in question be directed toward the plaintiff, that the defendant should know that injury to the plaintiff is likely to result, and that the injury to the plaintiff does in fact occur (Cement LaFarge, at pp. 471-72).

 

[64]         In the statement of claim, the plaintiffs allege that “Hicks, Braid and Morris were parties to an agreement to deceive CN into believing that [WE] was qualified for the CN contract when it was not, that their actions were directed towards the plaintiffs with the intention to cause them injury and that they knew or ought to have known that the plaintiffs would suffer injury.”   Plaintiffs’ counsel made no argument at the hearing regarding the claim of conspiracy but relied on the plaintiffs’ written brief which explains:

26.  The evidence is clear that Walt, Maisie and Carolyn lied on numerous occasions to several different parties.  They lied to the plaintiffs.  They lied to CN.  They lied to the drivers.

27.  Why did they lie?  They lied because they wanted the plaintiffs’ authority [transport licence] and believed that without it, they could not get the CN contract.  As the evidence shows, they never had a bona fide intention to honour their commitments to the plaintiffs.  They made an agreement amongst themselves to lie to CN, the plaintiffs and the drivers which resulting [sic] in the plaintiffs’ [sic] suffering damages.  As indicated in Alevizos, there was a concerted action taken on their part, pursuant to an agreement, that was likely to cause the plaintiffs harm, and did in fact cause them harm.

 

 

[65]         It is not clear from the written argument whether the plaintiffs are alleging predominant purpose conspiracy or unlawful means conspiracy.  However, as no argument was made that the conduct on which the conspiracy is based – lying – was “unlawful” (see A.I. Enterprises Ltd. v. Bram Enterprises Ltd., 2014 SCC 12, [2014] 1 S.C.R. 177), I will assume that the claim is based on predominant purpose conspiracy.   

[66]         There is no evidence to support the plaintiffs’ claim that Morris, Hicks and Braid lied to them when they led them to believe that WE intended to purchase FE.  Rather, the evidence is that WE made offers to FE which FE rejected.  Morris approved the draft agreements sent to the plaintiffs’ lawyer by his lawyers and was prepared to sign them.    

[67]         As for the allegation that the defendants agreed to lie to CN, it is true that the covering letter sent with WE’s bid was misleading in referring to a partnership between WE and FE even though no purchase agreement had been concluded.  But, to the extent that there was any agreement to lie to CN, the plaintiffs were parties to it. Both Comeau and Silva signed the letter to CN.  In her examination for discovery, Comeau admitted that she signed the letter knowing that the representation in it was untrue.      

[68]         Moreover, the predominant purpose of the “agreement to lie” to CN was not to cause injury to the plaintiffs.  It was to ensure that WE got the CN contract.  As the plaintiffs were to get a commission on that contract if the sale went through, the lie would benefit them, not injure them.  

[69]         I am confident on the evidence before me that there is no merit to the claim of conspiracy and that a trial of the issue would not provide anything further to substantiate the claim.     

 

conclusion

 

[70]         The plaintiffs claim that WE concluded an agreement with FE to purchase FE’s assets or, at least, they were led to believe an agreement would be concluded.  I have explained why I have found that there is no genuine issue regarding the claim of contract or the torts of negligent misrepresentation or conspiracy.  And I am satisfied that the evidence necessary to fairly adjudicate these issues is before me or could have been put before me.  While Mr. Saper may have been able to shed some light on the issues, if he in fact would have anything to add, his evidence should have been introduced on this motion.

[71]         It appears that the plaintiffs’ real complaint is that the defendants did not negotiate with them in good faith.  The statement of claim does not plead a breach of the duty of good faith as a cause of action.  But, in any case, while the law recognizes a duty of good faith in the performance of a contract (Bhasin v. Hrynew, 2014 SCC 71, [2014] 3 S.C.R. 494), it does not recognize a duty of good faith in negotiating a contract (Martel Building Ltd. v. Canada, 2000 SCC 60, [2000] 2 S.C.R. 860).  The adversarial nature of commercial negotiations must temper the expectations of the parties.  As stated in Martel, at para. 62, “The primary goal of any economically rational actor engaged in commercial negotiation is to achieve the most advantageous financial bargain.”  The failed negotiations in this case do not give the plaintiffs a claim against the defendants.

[72]         The motion of the defendants to dismiss the plaintiffs’ claim is granted.

[73]         Costs may be spoken to if they cannot be agreed upon.
