Introduction

 

[1]   The Municipal Corporation of the Hamlet of Cambridge Bay (Cambridge Bay) passed a water rate by-law, which defines Amulti plex units@ and classifies them as commercial users and thereby subject to commercial rates. The rates for commercial users are substantially higher than non-commercial rates. Kitikmeot Corporation (KC), the owner of multi plex units, brought an application for judicial review on the grounds that the by-law was ultra vires and discriminatory.

 

[2]   The chambers judge upheld the by-law, but found that it was discriminatory in its operation vis-à-vis two single dwelling residences owned by the appellant. KC appeals and Cambridge Bay cross-appeals the chambers decision.

 

 

Facts

 

[3]   KC owns a number of residential housing properties in Cambridge Bay which it rents to its employees and other Inuit organizations. Five of the properties have a water tank for each residential unit. Cambridge Bay directly invoiced the tenants for the water supplied and billed them at the non-commercial rate. One five-unit apartment building did not have individual water tanks, but rather a central tank which supplied water to the tenants. For that property, Cambridge Bay invoiced KC at the commercial rate.

 

[4]   On August 30, 2004, Cambridge Bay passed a by-law, #188, more than doubling the rates for commercial users. After the by-law was passed, KC was invoiced directly at the commercial rate for all of the residential buildings, not just the five-unit building. The evidence is that Cambridge Bay, unilaterally, and without request of the tenants, closed their accounts and began to invoice KC, as landlord, directly for the water consumed by the tenants. The water charges were passed onto the tenants resulting in KC=s tenants paying much higher water rates than residential users who own their own homes.

 

[5]   The chambers judge found that the multi plex units had not been invoiced as commercial users under the previous by-law, with the exception of the five-unit apartment with the central tank. The other units, which came within the definition of Amulti plex@, were invoiced directly to the tenant at the non-commercial rate.

 

[6]   He held that the municipality could create different classes of consumers but cannot unreasonably discriminate among its customers. By-law #188 delineated a number of classes based on the nature of the use of the property. The fact of separate water tanks was immaterial.

 

[7]   The chambers judge found there was no discrimination against KC. The five-unit apartment had been billed at the commercial rate under the previous by-law. However, Cambridge Bay had overlooked the other units which qualified as multi plex units. KC, as an owner, was a user of the water even though the tenants were the consumers.

 

[8]   The chambers judge concluded that the commercial category was intra vires Cambridge Bay=s authority and did not discriminate.

 

[9]   He found, however, that the single family units did not come within the definition of a multi plex. By including the single family units in the commercial category, Cambridge Bay created two classes of single family users. This created a problem of discrimination between classes. This type of discrimination must be specifically authorized by the enabling legislation. The judge could find no authority in the Hamlets Act, R.S.N.W.T. 1988, c. H 1, authorizing such discrimination, nor was it a necessary incident to exercising the power delegated by Nunavut. The discrimination was not in the by-law but in the way it was interpreted and enforced. If the single family dwellings were removed from the commercial class, the discrimination would end.

 

[10]  KC appeals the determination that by-law #188 was not ultra vires. Cambridge Bay cross- appeals the finding that the enforcement vis-à-vis the two single dwelling units was discriminatory.

 

Relevant legislation

 

[11]  The powers of Cambridge Bay are derived from the Hamlets Act, as duplicated for Nunavut by section 29 of the Nunavut Act, S.C. 1993, c. 28 and as amended by An Act of Amend the Hamlets Act, S. Nu. 2003, c. 3.

 

[12]  The general powers of the hamlet are set out in section 53.1 as follows:

 

"53.1. A municipal government is a corporation and, subject to this Act, has the rights and is subject to the liabilities of a corporation and may exercise its powers for municipal purposes."

 

[13]  The ability to maintain and operated services and utilities is provided in section 53.94:

 

"53.94. (1) A municipal corporation may, for municipal purposes, do the following:

 

(a)   construct, operate, repair, improve and maintain works and

improvements;

 

(b)   acquire, establish, maintain and operate services, facilities and

utilities, excluding electrical utilities;

 

(c)   use municipal equipment, materials and labour to carry out private

works on private property.

 

Terms and conditions

 

(2)   A municipal corporation exercising its powers under subsection (1) may, by by law, subject to the Utility Rates Review Council Act, set terms and conditions *in respect of users*, including

 

(a)   setting, charging and collecting the rates or amounts of deposits, fees and other charges;

 

(b)   providing for a right of entry onto private property to determine compliance with other terms and conditions, to determine the amount of deposits, fees or other charges, or to disconnect a service; and

 

(c)   discontinuing or disconnecting a service and refusing to provide the service to users who fail to comply with the terms and conditions."

[* emphasis added]

 

[14]  The power to pass by-laws is set out in section 54. The powers are broadly stated in section 54.1 as follows:

 

"54.1. The power given to a council under section 54.2 to pass by laws is stated in general terms:

 

(a)   to give broad authority to the council and to respect its right to govern the municipality in whatever way the council considers appropriate, within the jurisdiction given to it under this and other Acts; and

 

(b)   to enhance the ability of the council to respond to present and future issues in the municipality."

 

[15]  Section 54.3 specifically provides that classes may be created and dealt with differently:

 

"54.3. Without restricting the general application of section 54.2, a council may in a by law passed under this Part:

 

(e)   deal with any development, activity, industry, business or thing in different ways, including dividing each of them into classes and dealing with each class in different ways;"

 

[16]  By-law #188 is relatively short and sets up a number of categories of users who are charged for water in accordance with a schedule of charges. Section 3(b) states that users shall be charged in accordance with the rates set out in Schedule A.

 

[17]  Section 2 of by-law #188 defines the following terms:

 

"(b)  'Commercial User' means a legal entity, incorporated or unincorporated, carrying on a business *for the purpose of the provision of services wholesale or retail sales*, but does not include industrial users *but includes Multi Plex Units*.

 

(c)   *'Multi Plex Unit' means a building used for housing separate families where two or more units are attached.*

 

. . .

(h)   'Industrial Users' means a legal entity, incorporated or unincorporated, engaged in the business of processing renewable or non renewable resources or of manufacturing products, but does not include commercial users.

 

. . .

 

(j)   'Non Commercial' includes the occupant of residential property, which includes condominiums, a society, association or other like non profit organizations, *but does not include* government, commercial, industrial users or *Multi Plex Dwelling Users*.

 

. . .

 

(q)   'User' means a commercial user, government user, industrial user, non commercial user or a multi dwelling user."

[* emphasis added]

 

 

Standard of review

 

[18]  The standard of review for determining the vires of a municipal by-law is correctness. The Supreme Court of Canada stated in United Taxi Drivers= Fellowship of Southern Alberta v. Calgary (City), 2004 SCC 19, [2004] 1 S.C.R. 485 at para. 5:

 

"[5] The only question in this case is whether the freeze on the issuance of taxi plate licences was ultra vires the City under the Municipal Government Act. Municipalities do not possess any greater institutional competence or expertise than the courts in delineating their jurisdiction. Such a question will always be reviewed on a standard of correctness: Nanaimo (City) v. Rascal Trucking Ltd., [2000] 1 S.C.R. 342, 2000 SCC 13, at para. 29. There is no need to engage in the pragmatic and functional approach in a review for vires; such an inquiry is only required where a municipality=s adjudicative or policy making function is being exercised."

 

 

Analysis

 

[19]  The United Taxi Drivers decision also set out the proper approach to interpreting municipal powers. Bastarache J., for the court, stated at para. 7, Ato determine whether a municipality is authorized to exercise a certain power, such as limiting the issuance of taxi plate licences, the provisions of the Act must be construed in a broad and purposive manner.@ He continued at para. 8:

 

"[8] A broad and purposive approach to the interpretation of municipal legislation is also consistent with this Court=s approach to statutory interpretation generally. The contextual approach requires Athe words of an Act . . . to be read in their entire context and in their grammatical and ordinary sense harmoniously with the scheme of the Act, the object of the Act, and the intention of Parliament@: E. A. Driedger, Construction of Statutes (2nd ed. 1983), at p. 87; Bell ExpressVu Limited Partnership v. Rex, [2002] 2 S.C.R. 559, 2002 SCC 42, at para. 26. This approach is also consistent with s. 10 of Alberta=s Interpretation Act, R.S.A. 2000, c. I 8, which provides that every provincial enactment must be given a fair, large and liberal construction and interpretation that best ensures the attainment of its objects."

 

[20]  The power of Cambridge Bay to set water rates is found in section 53.94(2) of the Hamlets Act which provides that a municipal corporation may set terms and conditions in respect of users, including setting rates. Both parties agree that because the term Ausers@ is not defined in the Hamlets Act, it should be given its plain and ordinary meaning.

 

[21]  The Ontario Court of Appeal decision in 710357 Ontario Ltd. v. Penetanguishene (Town of) (1998), 164 D.L.R. (4th) 755, was urged upon the chambers judge by KC. In that case, the town sought to bill water rates to landlord-owners instead of the tenants in residential apartment units. The applicable public utilities act gave authority to a municipal corporation to fix the price for water usage, but stated it was Ato be charged to the various classes of consumers@. The section stated:

 

"In fixing the rents, rates or prices to be paid for the supply of a public utility the corporation may use its discretion as to the rents, rates or prices to be charged to the various classes of consumers and also as to the rents, rates or prices at which a public utility shall be supplied for the different purposes for which it may be supplied or required." 

 

[22]  The court held, with regard to a water utility, the Aconsumer@ meant the person who uses or consumes the water, and not the landlord-owner. The court stated at para. 26:

 

"[26] In my view, if a person other than the user of water, that is a consumer of that utility, was obligated to pay for it, the Act would have plainly said so. When the legislature dealt with the obligation to pay public utility (water) rates, it imposed the obligation on the utility's user, that is the consumer of the utility, without reference to the relationship of the payer to the property to which the utility is supplied. In my view, landlords, as owners, are only liable to pay for water charges if the landlord uses the water, as would occur in a superintendent=s apartment occupied by the landlord, or its employee. In that case, the landlord=s obligation to pay for water would accrue because the landlord is a consumer, not because the landlord is an owner."

 

[23]  The chambers judge distinguished Penetanguishene by making a distinction between Aconsumers@ as used in the Ontario legislation and Ausers@, the term used in this case. He held that the owner makes use of the water in operating his rental business, even though it is the tenant who consumes the water.

 

[24]  With respect, the premise of the chambers judge is not correct. There was no evidence that KC made Ause of the water in operating the business of owning and renting residential housing units@. KC merely rented the units to the tenants, i.e., provided them with possession of the units for the rental term. The provision of water is not an incidence of a landlord-tenant relationship and there is no evidence that KC contracted to supply water to its tenants. To the contrary, the evidence is that the tenants contracted directly with Cambridge Bay for the water delivered to them. The hamlet had no authority to switch these accounts to the landlord, and to start charging the landlord for water supplied to its tenants.

 

[25]  Even with respect to the five-unit apartment without separate water meters for each unit, the evidence does not suggest that the water was acquired by the landlord either for its own use or for supply to the tenants at a profit or on any other commercial basis. It appears that KC simply facilitated the tenants, who used the water, with the invoice being apportioned amongst the tenants.

 

[26]  Further, the language of the by-law is problematic in its reference to multi plex units. The definition of a Acommercial user@ expressly Aincludes multi plex units@ which are defined to mean a Abuilding used for housing separate families where two or more units are attached@. A multi plex unit is a thing as distinct from being a user. The use of the unit is for residential purposes.

 

[27]  The authorizing statute permits the hamlet to set terms and conditions in respect of users of utility services. The authority to set rates is not in respect to the user of the building, but rather in respect to the user of the utility service. Here, the user of the utility service is the tenant, as distinct from the unit. The by-law confuses the use of the building with the user of the service.

 

[28]  The chambers judge erred in distinguishing Penetanguishene by relying upon dictionary definitions. In Statutory Interpretation (Concord: Irwin Law, 1997), Sullivan states that a dictionary meaning is not the same as ordinary meaning. Moreover, some dictionary meanings for Ause@ includes Aconsume@. We agree with the Ontario court=s interpretation in Penetanguishene that the ordinary meaning for Auser@ includes Aconsumer@.

 

[29]  In short, the by-law is ultra vires in attempting to impose commercial rates on occupants of multi plex units, through the stratagem of defining the units as a consumer user. The statute requires that the rates be set with reference to the user of the utility service, in this case the residential tenants. There is no authority to establish rates by reference to apartment units, however owned, rather than in respect of users of the service.

 

[30]  Further, KC=s tenants are members of the same class of users, namely residential users. The Hamlets Act does not allow a municipality to deal differently with users of the same class. There is no provision in the Act that authorizes Cambridge Bay to treat residential users occupying multi plex units differently from those occupying single family residential dwellings.

 

[31]  There is ample authority for the principle that by-laws must not discriminate unless the enabling authority authorizes such discrimination. In Montréal (City of) v. Arcade Amusements Inc., [1985] 1 S.C.R. 368, the Supreme Court of Canada held that the power to pass municipal by laws does not entail that of enacting discriminatory provisions (i.e., of drawing a distinction) unless in effect the enabling legislation authorizes such discriminatory treatment. See also Rogers, The Law of Canadian Municipal Corporations (2nd ed. 1971) at 406.3 406.4:

 

"It is a fundamental principle of municipal law that by laws must affect equally all those who come within the ambit of the enabling enactment. Municipal legislation must be impartial in its operation and must not discriminate so as to show favouritism to one or more classes of citizens. Any by law violating this principle so that all the inhabitants are not placed in the same position regarding matters affected by it is illegal."

 

The general principle does not apply where the enabling statute clearly specifies that certain persons or things may be excepted from its operation or expressly authorizes some form of discrimination."

 

[32]  As the Supreme Court of Canada further explained in R. v. Sharma, [1993] 1 S.C.R. 650 at para. 25:

 

"[25] The rule against discriminatory by laws is an outgrowth of the principle that, as statutory bodies, municipalities Amay exercise only those powers expressly conferred by statute, those powers necessarily or fairly implied by the expressed power in the statute, and those indispensable powers essential and not merely convenient to the effectuation of the purposes of the corporation@ (Makuch, Canadian Municipal and Planning Law (1983), at p. 115)."

 

[33]  Cambridge Bay points to section 54.3, which specifically provides that classes may be created and dealt with differently. However, even assuming that this section applies to utility services, the classes must be of users. Here, it would seem that the hamlet has attempted to create a separate class, not by reference to different users, but rather by reference to different building structures. The fact that some residential users occupy multi plex units does not make them a separate or different class from other residential users occupying single dwelling or other types of residential properties. The by-law effects discrimination amongst the same class of users.

 

[34]  The chambers judge found that the by-law created two classes of single family users; those who rented and those who owned their homes. He found the discrimination was not in the by-law, but in the way it was interpreted and enforced, and concluded that if the single family dwellings were removed from the commercial class, the discrimination would end. But neither the act of renting nor the act of owning is a use of the utility service. Both renters and owners are residents of dwelling units and their use is the same, namely residential.

 

[35]  Moreover, the problem of discrimination extends further than a problem of enforcement. The discrimination is inherent in the classes set out in by-law #188.

 

[36]  A by-law can be ultra vires because of unauthorized discrimination, even if such discrimination has not occurred. In School District No. 61 v. District of Oak Bay, 2006 BCCA 28, the British Columbia Court of Appeal stated at para. 21:

 

"[21] As Sharma illustrates, it need not be shown that a particular property or property owner or occupier is >singled out= by the by-law for different treatment than others, but only that the by-law draws a distinction which the enabling legislation does not authorize, either expressly or by necessary implication."

 

[37]  By-law #188 distinguishes non-commercial residential users and multi plex dwelling residential users, i.e., between occupants of single dwelling residential property and occupants of a building used for housing separate families where two or more units are attached. In other words, people residing in multi-unit buildings are treated differently than people residing in single family dwellings. This would appear to be a distinction regardless of whether the resident was a tenant or an owner and the effect would be to charge different water rates to the same class of users.

 

[38]  The discrimination thus extends beyond charging commercial rates to tenants in single family units as opposed to owners in single family units. The problem of discrimination is also that persons residing in multi plex units are charged commercial rates when persons in single units are charged non-commercial rates. As earlier noted, the by-law purports to attach different rates to ownership and building structure, rather than to different users; i.e., the residents of the dwellings all make residential use of the water supplied to them.

 

[39]  As stated in Sharma at para. 25, quoting from Rogers, The Law of Canadian Municipal Corporations:

 

"Municipal legislation must be impartial in its operation and must not discriminate so as to show favouritism to one or more classes of citizens. Any by law violating this principle so that all the inhabitants are not placed in the same position regarding matters affected by it is illegal."

 

[40]  There is nothing in the Hamlets Act permitting discrimination against residents on the basis of the type of dwelling they are living in. By-law #188, therefore, is ultra vires on this basis as well.

 

 

Conclusion

 

[41]  KC=s appeal is allowed and the cross-appeal of Cambridge Bay is dismissed. By-law #188 is declared to be ultra vires to the extent that it imposes, and was used to collect, commercial rates for water used by the tenants of multi plex units. Further, Cambridge Bay had no authority to impose commercial rates for water supplied to the two single-dwelling residences owned by KC. Cambridge Bay is directed to calculate and refund the overcharges to KC for the benefit of its tenants. KC is entitled to recover interest on the overcharges from the date of the commencement of these proceedings until payment thereof at the rate of prejudgment interest prescribed by The Judicature Act, S.N.W.T. 1998, c. 34, as enacted for Nunavut by section 29 of the Nunavut Act, R.S.C. 1993, c. 28. Any dispute as to the applicable rate or calculation of the interest shall be referred to the clerk of the Court for determination.
