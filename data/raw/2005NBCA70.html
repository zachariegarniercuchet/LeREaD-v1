I.          Introduction

 

[1]                                       This is the first time this Court has been presented with an appeal involving a worker’s right to benefits for psychological injuries suffered in the workplace, as provided for under the Workers’ Compensation Act, R.S.N.B. 1973, c. W-13. The essential facts are as follows.

 

[2]                                       The appellant and her co-workers had been unsuccessful in convincing their employer to make changes to their work environment (noise and lighting problems). The impasse led to a verbal confrontation in which the appellant’s supervisor castigated her in a manner best described as a bombastic rant, punctuated by yelling. The appellant’s physician described the event as “emotionally traumatizing.” The appellant did not return to work and was subsequently diagnosed as suffering from a “major” and “resistant” depression. After exhausting benefits under the employer’s disability plan and the federal unemployment insurance regime, the appellant filed a stress-related claim with the respondent Commission. The claim was rejected on the basis that the psychological injury complained of did not qualify as an “accident”, as that term is defined in s. 1 of the Act. Subject to one exception, that definition expressly excludes “the disablement of mental stress or a disablement caused by mental stress...” The exception requires the claimant to establish that the disability arose as a result of “an acute reaction to a traumatic event…”. The Commission ruled against the appellant on this point, as did the Appeals Tribunal established under the Workplace Health, Safety and Compensation Commission Act, S.N.B. 1994, c. W-14. For the reasons that follow, I conclude that the Appeals Tribunal did not err in the result. That said, this Court must address certain issues that eluded the Appeals Tribunal.

 

[3]                                       As to the proper review standard, there is no consensus on whether the Appeals Tribunal’s decision involves a question of fact or law. The two respondents contend that the Appeals Tribunal approached the issue as a question of causation and, correlatively, a question of fact. With respect, I disagree.

 

[4]                                       In my opinion, the Appeals Tribunal rejected the appellant’s appeal because the confrontation did not qualify as a traumatic event. I agree with the appellant that the Appeals Tribunal failed to acknowledge the Commission’s reasons for rejecting the appellant’s claim. Those reasons are tied to two policy directives that outline the criteria to be applied by Commission staff when assessing stress-related claims. Specifically, the appellant questions the Commission’s application of a tri-partite objective test. It is trite law that Commission policies are non-binding and that it is the responsibility of the Appeals Tribunal, and ultimately this Court, to rule on the proper interpretation of the Act. This includes the articulation of a legal framework through the identification of relevant criteria. However, claimants have a right to know whether the Commission’s policy directives are consistent with the provisions of the Act when properly construed. I recognize the right of the Appeals Tribunal to conduct what amounts to a de novo hearing and to make independent findings of fact based on fresh evidence. However, it makes no sense that the Commission would be able to formulate and apply a tri-partite objective test, together with its own definitions of critical statutory terms when assessing a disability claim, and then turn around and abandon that legal framework on appeal by re-characterizing the issue as a question of fact.

 

[5]                                       I am of the view that the appellant’s primary challenge represents a pure question of law for which the review standard would have been correctness had the Appeals Tribunal dealt with the interpretative issue and, correlatively, the correctness of the Commission’s policy directives under attack. Deference cannot be accorded a tribunal’s failure to address a pertinent interpretative issue. In fairness to the Appeals Tribunal, there is an explanation for the omission.

 

[6]                                       The Commission did not participate in the one and one half hour hearing before the Appeals Tribunal and neither the appellant nor the respondent employer was represented by legal counsel. The appellant retained a “labour consultant” who raised, but did not aggressively pursue, the validity of the Commission’s policy directives. The employer sent a claims officer and the local office manager to defend its position. Only after the appeal to this Court was filed did the Commission elect to intervene as a party. Before this Court, all three parties elected to be represented by legal counsel.

 

II.         Background

 

[7]                                       The appellant had been working for the respondent, Via Rail Canada Inc., for 15 years before commencing work, on October 15, 2001, at its call center in Moncton, New Brunswick. Shortly after moving into premises under renovation, the appellant and other employees complained of poor working conditions. Specifically, they complained of noise levels and poor lighting conditions. Over the ensuing seven months the employees’ repeated requests for workplace modifications went unanswered by their immediate supervisor. In July of 2002, the appellant spoke directly with her supervisor’s superior with a view to having management address the employees’ concerns. On learning that the appellant had approached upper management over the poor working conditions, the supervisor called the appellant into his office on July 15, 2002. The appellant described the meeting as one in which “[the manager] threatened and intimidated me for going over his head to the Director concerning the fixing of the working conditions…” The appellant also characterized the encounter as a traumatic event which resulted in her leaving her employment. In her statement of facts, the appellant states that she “went off work on July 15th 2002 as a result of a disability flowing from the noise level in the workplace, the excessive and difficult lighting at the workplace and the inappropriate actions of the manager...” 

 

[8]                                       Following the confrontation with her supervisor, the union representing the appellant pursued a grievance against her employer with respect to its refusal to pay the appellant for the six hours not worked on July 15, 2002. Eventually, that grievance was abandoned.  From July 19, 2002 until September 7, 2002, the appellant received disability benefits under an employer-funded short term disability plan administered by a private insurer, Great West Life. The insurer agreed to provide the benefits until such time as the appellant could seek and obtain benefits from the Commission. Apparently, Great West Life decided to discontinue payment of the benefits after concluding that the appellant’s illness was work-related. This finding was based on a medical report submitted on the appellant’s behalf. Great West Life also indicated that as physical alterations had been made to her work environment, there was nothing to prevent the appellant from going back to work. On the basis of this evidence, the insurer advised her to pursue a compensation claim with the Commission. However, the appellant sought and obtained benefits under the Employment Insurance Act, S.C. 1996, c. 23. She received benefits for a total of 15 weeks, ending on December 5, 2002. Finally, the record does disclose that the appellant’s supervisor no longer holds that position and that Via Rail made the requested environmental modifications to the workplace.

 

[9]                                       On December 23, 2002, the appellant applied for workers’ compensation benefits. On January 22, 2003, an adjudicator rejected her claim on the basis that the appellant’s condition related to the “usual stresses and strains of the workplace” and not an acute reaction to a traumatic event. Subsequently, the appellant sought a reconsideration of her claim. The matter was reviewed and on October 27, 2003 the same adjudicator confirmed the original decision to reject the appellant’s claim. The rejection letter of October 27, 2003 states that in order for the appellant to qualify for benefits she had to establish that she suffered an acute reaction to a traumatic event and that to come within the exception to the general rule three tests had to be met: (1) the condition must be an acute reaction to a traumatic event and not due to chronic or cumulative stress; (2) the event must directly produce a condition which meets established diagnostic criteria; and (3) the claim must also arise out of and in the course of employment. Applying those criteria, the adjudicator wrote:

 

With respect to the first test, [the appellant’s] mental stress was the result of a combination of factors or an accumulation of factors and not the result of an acute incident. Secondly she was diagnosed with an adjustment disorder July, 2002. Dr. Nasri, indicates [the appellant] had a problem with her mood sometime in October, 2001 and suffered from major depression. An adjustment disorder does not meet the requirement of the second test which is that the event must directly produce a condition which meets established diagnostic criteria.

 

The American Psychiatric Association defines post traumatic stress disorder as a “disorder which arises following a psychologically distressing event that is outside the range of usual human experience. The stressor producing this syndrome would be markedly distressing to anyone, and is usually experienced with intense fear, terror and helplessness.”

 

During the adjudication process of claims for acute reactions to traumatic events, the Commission must ensure that the event was acute and not cumulative in nature. The event must be measured by objective standards and actual events rather than the worker’s perception. There must be a causal connection between the traumatic event, the stress complained of, and the worker’s disability.

 

The event itself of July 15, 2002 when there was a disagreement between [the appellant] and her Supervisor was unfortunate, however, it remains the opinion of the Commission that this event would not qualify as an acute reaction to a traumatic event considering the evidence of file. [The appellant] has not yet been diagnosed with a condition which meets the established diagnostic criteria. In addition, in reviewing the above noted definition of post-traumatic stress disorder, I do not feel that the event of July 15, 2003 would qualify as a stressor that “would be markedly distressing to anyone.” Again as noted above, the Commission must measure the event by objective standards and actual events rather than the worker’s perception.

 

 

[10]                                   On December 4, 2003, the appellant sought a further reconsideration of her claim and offered as additional evidence a report prepared by her psychiatrist. The following passages from his report are of relevance to this appeal:

 

In my opinion [the appellant] has been suffering from Major Depression which really got worse in July after the incident at work, which from a psychosocial model of mood disorder was “the last straw that broke the camel’s back” resulting in depressed mood, low energy and poor concentration. The problem with her mood started with changing work environment. Especially the event at work where she had a argument with her supervisor was emotionally traumatizing which was followed by anxiety attack later leading to loss of appetite, problem with sleep, headaches, somatic complains including palpation, insomnia, problem with memory and a low level of energy. If this particular event had not occurred it is unlikely that she would have suffered from such a severe depression, which has caused a significant decline in her level of functioning.

 

In view of the fact that there is no history of previous inpatient or outpatient psychiatric treatment this work-related stress is one of the major contributing factors toward her problem with her mood. One of the recommendations would be that a change in work environment where she is exposed to less noise and less bright lights. This possibly could help her with her attention span and concentration and eventually help with her mood. The other recommendation would be pharmacotherapy with periodic assessment of medications.

 

In my opinion [the appellant] is suffering from resistant depression that has not responded to a trial of two medications and a poor response to augmentation. She did have a second opinion from another psychiatrist and one recommendation was to put her on a tricyclic antidepressant like Amitriptyline unfortunately this lady is suffering from Glaucoma because of which she cannot have a trail of SSRI at a therapeutic dosage of Amitriptyline of 200mg or above. Because of her resistant depression she will not be able to work for another ten to twelve weeks.

 

 

[11]                                   On December 12, 2003, the same adjudicator wrote to the appellant reaffirming the original decision rejecting her claim. On January 4, 2004, the appellant appealed the Commission’s decision to the Appeals Tribunal. The one and one half hour hearing was held on June 11, 2004 and a decision rendered on July 15, 2004. The Appeals Tribunal accepted the testimony of the appellant and a fellow employee that they felt “threatened” during the July 15, 2002 meeting with the manager. The Appeals Tribunal went on to conclude that it was clear that both the physical and psychological working environment was “less than ideal.” The only medical evidence referred to in the Appeals Tribunal’s decision is the psychiatric report referred to above. The Appeals Tribunal’s reasons for rejecting the appellant’s appeal, as it relates to the issue of stress, is limited to the following passages:

 

The representative argued in the alternative the event was a traumatic event and therefore came within the definition of accident. This, in the view of the Panel, is not supported by the evidence in the Appeal Record.

 

In the view of the Panel, under the definition of accident, the traumatic event must create an acute reaction leading to a disablement caused by the mental stress. Consequently, the mental stress follows the ‘traumatic event’ causing an acute reaction. In the present case, as noted by Dr. Nasri in his November 27, 2003 report:

 

In my opinion [the appellant] has been suffering from Major Depression which really got worse in July after the incident at work. […] [sic] [page 103 of the Appeal Record]

 

In the view of the Tribunal, the event of July was not a traumatic event leading to disablement but rather the culmination of a series of events both personal and at work which led to the appellant suffering a major depression. Consequently, since the condition of the appellant is a disablement caused by mental stress, or a disablement of mental stress, it is not considered under the Act ‘an accident’ since it is not an acute reaction to a traumatic event.

 

  

III.       The Proper Standard of Review – A Question of Fact or Law?

 

[12]                                   In all cases, it is incumbent on this Court to identify the proper review standard (see Voice Construction Ltd. v. Construction & General Workers’ Union, Local 92, [2004] 1 S.C.R. 609). On this issue the parties disagree, principally because they are at odds as to whether the issue involves a question of law or fact. Both the Commission and Via Rail advance the argument that the Appeals Tribunal’s finding involves a question of fact for which patent unreasonableness is the applicable review standard. Thus, they argue that this Court cannot intervene unless it is established that the Appeals Tribunal made a palpable and overriding error in reaching its decision. The Commission’s characterization of the issue as one of fact hinges on the understanding that the issue before the Appeals Tribunal was simply one of causation. At paragraph 12 of its submission the Commission writes: “The determination of the causality of the appellant’s mental stress is a question of fact within the exclusive jurisdiction of the Commission.” With respect, this argument is entirely misplaced. Let me explain.

 

[13]                                   I start with the proposition that the issue of causation in the context of the Workers’ Compensation Act may involve a question of law, a question of fact or even a question of mixed law and fact (the application of a legal test or framework to primary facts). The proper characterization depends on the circumstances of each case. In the typical compensation case, the issue of causation must be addressed because the Act’s definition of “accident” imposes a requirement that it have arisen “out of and in the course of employment…” This requirement reflects the Act’s primary objective, which is to make no-fault insurance benefits available to those who suffer injuries in the workplace. The Act was not intended to serve as a general compensation scheme that embraces non-occupational injuries. In furtherance of that object, the Act requires that the injury be caused by some event or condition tied to the workplace. Note that the expression “arising out of and in the course of employment” contains two distinct but interrelated concepts. The general understanding is that if the injury occurs at work, it “arose in the course” of the employment. This requirement deals with the time, location and activity surrounding the accident (the “when”, “where” and “what” of the injury so to speak). If, however, the injury is caused purely by natural phenomenon (e.g., an aneurism) such that the injury would have resulted irrespective of the employment situation, it cannot be held that the injury “arose out” of the employment. This is the “how” of the injury. Note also that s. 7(2) of the Act provides that once it is established that the accident arose out of the employment it is presumed that the accident occurred in the course of employment and vice versa. See generally Douglas G. Gilbert & L.A. Liversidge, Workers’ Compensation in Ontario, 3rd ed. (Aurora, Ont: Canada Law Book Inc., 2001), at 19, and Terence G. Ison, Workers’ Compensation in Canada, 2nd ed. (Toronto: Butterworths, 1989), at 35.

 

[14]                                   As Justice Drapeau (as he then was) explained in Martin v. Commission de la santé, de la sécurité et de l’indemnisation des accidents au travail (N.-B.) (1998), 205 N.B.R. (2d) 319 (C.A.) at para. 14: “[t]he existence of a causal link between disability by reason of a personal injury and an accident arising out of and in the course of employment is a question of fact.” In cases where an employee has suffered a physical injury on the job site, it is not difficult to establish causation within the framework of the Act. The more difficult cases involve an employee alleging disability tied to an occupational disease. For example, in Martin, the issue was whether a worker’s medical problems were caused by his exposure to toxic fumes at his workplace. See also Moyer v. Workplace Health, Safety and Compensation Commission (N.B.) (2004), 277 N.B.R. (2d) 111 (C.A.) and Athey v. Leonati, [1996] 3 S.C.R. 458 at 466-67 dealing with causation in the context of a negligence suit. 

 

[15]                                   I agree that the causation issue in the typical compensation case involves a question of fact. But this does not mean that the causation issue cannot involve a question of law. Recall that causation is a theoretical construct that can involve pure questions of law (see Snell v. Farrell, [1990] 2 S.C.R. 311 at 326-30). A decision of this Court that reflects the understanding that the causation issue may involve a question of law is Keddy v. Workplace Health, Safety and Compensation Commission (N.B.) et al. (2002), 247 N.B.R. (2d) 284 (C.A.). In that case, the central issue was whether the Appeals Tribunal erred in concluding that s. 11(1) of the Workers’ Compensation Act barred the claimant’s tort action against a hospital. The legal question posed was whether a worker who was injured while receiving medical treatment for a work-related injury was acting within the course of his or her employment at the time of the subsequent injury. A positive response to that question has the effect of barring any tort claim that the claimant may have against either employer, provided both employers are covered by the legislation, which they were in Keddy. As a matter of interpretation and policy, the Appeals Tribunal answered the question in the positive and, therefore, the tort claim against the nurse and hospital was barred under s. 11(1). This Court ruled that the issue was a question of law for which the review standard was correctness, but that the Tribunal had not erred in its determination. Note that Keddy was not a case in which it had to be determined whether the employee had sought medical treatment for the work injury or whether the medical injury arose from the treatment of the work injury. Rather, the Keddy case raised a broad policy issue of precedential significance and one that other tribunals and courts have had to address (see Kovach v. Workers’ Compensation Board, [1996] B.C.J. No. 2388 (C.A.) (QL) and Pasiechnyk v. Saskatchewan (Workers’ Compensation Board), [1997] 2 S.C.R. 890). As explained in Canada (Director of Investigation and Research) v. Southam Inc., [1997] 1 S.C.R. 748 at para. 37 the fact that a case is of true precedential significance is a litmus test for deciding whether the issue being addressed involves a question of law.  

 

[16]                                   In the present case, the disability for which the appellant claims compensation is rooted in her workplace. The poor working conditions, together with the appellant’s encounter with her supervisor, are the factors underscoring her disability claim. Specifically, the appellant argued that the confrontational encounter with her supervisor led to her inability to work. In the words of her doctor, that incident was the “straw that broke the camel’s back.” While both the Commission and the Appeals Tribunal rejected the idea that the encounter qualified as a traumatic event, it is agreed that the stress for which the appellant is seeking compensation was work-related. In short, no one denies that the appellant suffered stress “arising out of and in the course of employment.” Hence, the issue of factual causation does not present a barrier to her compensation claim. However, that is not sufficient for purposes of establishing a valid claim.  

 

[17]                                   The causation issue arises twice in the assessment of stress-related claims. On the first occasion the decision-maker is required to decide whether the stress (the alleged traumatic event) arose out of and in the course of employment. Assuming this to be so, the decision-maker must then decide whether the work-related incident qualifies as a traumatic event. If the incident so qualifies, the causation issue arises a second time. The decision-maker must now decide whether the traumatic event caused an acute reaction leading to the claimant’s inability to work.

 

[18]                                   When I read the Appeals Tribunal’s reasons, reproduced at paragraph 9 of these reasons,  I am left with the impression that the appellant’s appeal was dismissed because her inability to work was the cumulative effect of a number of stressors being experienced within and outside the workplace, rather than a single incident which caused an acute reaction. In short, the Appeals Tribunal held that the appellant’s confrontation with her supervisor was not a traumatic event. Rather, her claim was for chronic stress, a topic addressed below.  In these circumstances, the respondents’ argument that the issue before this Court relates to causation and, therefore, a question of fact, is, with great respect, wrong. This leaves for consideration the appellant’s argument.

 

[19]                                   Counsel for the appellant recognizes that the terms “traumatic event” and “acute reaction” are subject to interpretation. Therefore, their meaning qualifies as a question of law. Specifically, counsel seized on the fact that the appellant’s claim was rejected after the adjudicator applied the Commission’s policy pertaining to stress-related claims. When one looks at Policy 21-103 (“Conditions for Entitlement-Stress”) it is evident that the Commission applies a tri-partite test when assessing stress-related claims. Moreover, the Commission has adopted its own definitions for the terms “traumatic event”, “acute reaction” and “post-traumatic stress disorder.” Policy 21-103 also speaks of an objective test for assessing stress claims. Finally, Policy 21-103.01 provides Commission staff with guidelines to be considered in cases involving post-traumatic stress syndrome.

 

[20]                                   Counsel for the appellant argues that the application of these policies constitutes an error of law in the sense that they contradict the proper meaning of the expression “acute reaction to a traumatic event.” Counsel also contends that the Appeals Tribunal applied those policies when deciding whether the appellant’s disability came within the exception to the general rule. With respect to the latter submission, the Appeals Tribunal’s reasons do not support the argument that the Tribunal expressly or implicitly accepted and applied either of the two policies in question. It is clear to me that the Tribunal considered that a determination as to what qualifies as a traumatic event is a question of personal judgment more akin to a question of fact. There is a reason why this is so and one that underscores a fundamental problem in the administrative appeals process.

 

[21]                                   When one looks at the record below (“Statement of Facts”), it is evident that the Commission did not participate in the hearing, thus leaving carriage of the appeal to the appellant’s employer, Via Rail. At that hearing, the appellant was represented by a “labour relations consultant”, who apparently has no formal legal training. The same appears to be true of the two representatives appearing on behalf of Via Rail: a claims officer and the local manager. In these circumstances, it is not surprising that the Appeals Tribunal was not presented with any legal arguments, save one, dealing with the interpretation of the expression “acute reaction to a traumatic event.” To the credit of the appellant’s advisor, he did question the validity of the policy in question and cited case law to the effect that the Appeals Tribunal was not bound by the Commission’s interpretation of the Act as reflected in Commission policy directives. Be that as it may, the Appeals Tribunal did not address this issue. This is so even though the right of appeal to the Appeals Tribunal, provided for under s. 21(1) of the Workplace Health, Safety and Compensation Commission Act, is from a decision of the Commission. This is not to deny that s. 21(3) of that Act effectively provides for a de novo hearing of the matter. However, even though claimants are entitled to a rehearing on the merits, including the right to adduce fresh evidence in support of their claims, that reality does not undermine the fact that the claim may have been rejected, in part or in full, because of the Commission’s erroneous interpretation of the enabling legislation or some other flawed determination of law.

 

[22]                                   In brief, I recognize the Appeals Tribunal’s right to conduct a de novo hearing and to make independent findings of fact based on evidence not presented to the Commission. However, that does not mean that the Appeals Tribunal is free to disregard the Commission’s reasons for rejecting a claim. Those reasons may raise questions of law. It makes no sense, for example, that the Commission would formulate and apply a tri-partite test, together with its own definitions of critical statutory terms, when assessing disability claims and then turn around and abandon that legal framework on an appeal to the Appeals Tribunal or to this Court by re-characterizing the issue as a question of fact.

 

[23]                                   In future, it is incumbent on the Appeals Tribunal to review the Commission’s reasons for rejecting a claim to determine whether the appeal raises any question of law, including the interpretation of any pertinent legislation. That obligation places the Commission on notice that it may want to participate in the hearing before the Appeals Tribunal if it wishes to defend its interpretation or application of the relevant legislation. After all, there can be no assurances that the claimant’s employer will be able to address legal questions effectively. The present case is on point.

 

[24]                                   Once the Appeals Tribunal has identified and resolved any question of law, it may turn to the evidence adduced by the parties, make the necessary findings of fact and apply those facts against the relevant legal framework. Even in cases where the Commission’s ruling hinges on factual matters, the Appeals Tribunal may well find it instructive to understand the general basis on which the Commission rejected a claimant’s application for compensation benefits.    

 

[25]                                   Returning to the present case, it is undeniable that the Commission’s published policies governing work-related stress lay down a framework for assessing such claims. That framework consists of a tri-partite objective test and definitions of key terms. Whether or not that framework is in keeping with the legislative intent underscoring the exception to the general rule is of critical significance. Had the Appeals Tribunal dealt with this issue, this Court would have had to identify the proper review standard by applying the pragmatic and functional approach outlined in the Supreme Court jurisprudence. As this Court has stated on numerous occasions, the review standard applicable to decisions of the Appeals Tribunal involving questions of law is generally correctness (see Gallant v. Workplace Health, Safety and Compensation Commission (N.B.) (2000), 228 N.B.R. (2d) 98 (C.A.) at para. 27, and Keddy). Parenthetically, I am not aware of any Supreme Court decision in which that Court has applied a review standard other than correctness in cases involving a statutory right of appeal on a question of law alone. At the same time, the Supreme Court continues to recognize that the existence of a statutory right of appeal does not of itself mean that correctness is the proper review standard. The other factors that comprise the pragmatic and functional approach have to be considered and in particular the nature of the legal issue (see Dr. Q v. College of Physicians and Surgeons of British Columbia, [2003] 1 S.C.R. 226 at paras. 17, 21-25, and 33, and Law Society of New Brunswick v. Ryan, [2003] 1 S.C.R. 247 at para. 21).

 

[26]                                   There is nothing in the record before us to suggest that a standard other than correctness should be applied to the interpretative issue at hand. Moreover, the failure of the Appeals Tribunal to address a relevant issue constitutes a clear error of law which this Court must address. Bluntly stated, one cannot accord deference to a tribunal’s failure to address a pertinent interpretative issue. This leads one to ask whether the matter can and should be remitted to the Appeals Tribunal. Arguably, s. 23(8) of the Workplace Health, Safety and Compensation Commission Act and Rule 62.21(10) of the Rules of Court permit this Court to remit a case back to the Appeals Tribunal in cases requiring factual determinations. In the present case, no factual issues remain outstanding and, therefore, there is no need to remit the matter back to the Appeals Tribunal. As s. 21(12) of the Act states that a tribunal decision is subject to an appeal to this Court on a question of law or jurisdiction, I am prepared to address the legal issues and make a determination based on the accepted facts.

 

IV.       The Interpretative Issue – A Legal Framework

 

[27]                                   The starting point of any analysis is the definition of “accident” found in s. 1 of the Workers’ Compensation Act. It reads as follows:  

 

"accident" includes a wilful and intentional act, not being the act of a worker, and also includes a chance event occasioned by a physical or natural cause, as well as a disablement caused by an occupational disease and any other disablement arising out of and in the course of employment, but does not include the disablement of mental stress or a disablement caused by mental stress, other than as an acute reaction to a traumatic event;

 

 

« accident » comprend un acte volontaire et intentionnel autre que celui du travailleur; il comprend aussi un événement fortuit dû à une cause physique ou naturelle de même que l'incapacité causée par une maladie professionnelle et tout autre incapacité survenant par le fait et à l'occasion de l'emploi, mais ne comprend pas l'incapacité de la tension mentale ni l'incapacité causée par la tension mentale, sauf en tant que réaction violente à un événement traumatique;

 

 

[28]                                       In brief, the right to compensation benefits is dependent on the worker establishing that he or she was involved in an accident that arose out of and in the course of employment. In turn, accident is defined to include: (1) a wilful and intentional act, not being the act of a worker; (2) a chance event occasioned by a physical or natural cause; (3) a disablement caused by an occupational disease; and (4) any other disablement arising out of and in the course of employment. To this point the definition of accident is arguably broad enough to encompass a disablement resulting from mental stress, provided the stress was work-related; that is to say the stress arose out of and in the course of employment. However, in 1992 the definition of “accident” was amended by An Act to Amend the Workers’ Compensation Act, S.N.B. 1992, c. 34 to exclude “the disablement of mental stress or a disablement caused by mental stress, other than as an acute reaction to a traumatic event.” [Emphasis added.]

 

[29]                                   It is not difficult to identify the historical reasons underscoring the decision of legislatures to address the question of whether disability claims tied to work-related stress would be compensable. During the late 1980s the Appeals Tribunal of Ontario took the bold step of including such claims: a position that was at odds with the policy being applied by the board. The historical background is discussed in Douglas Gilbert’s text, Workers’ Compensation In Ontario. It helps explain why, in 1992, the New Brunswick Legislature decided to amend the definition of “accident” so as to exclude stress-related claims, subject to one exception. 

 

[30]                                   Prior to 1998 the Ontario board had no explicit policy dealing with compensation for mental stress alone. It did have a policy covering “psychotraumatic disability” which applied in situations where mental stress resulted from a physical injury or disability. There was no policy in place for cases in which stress originating in the workplace lead to an employee’s inability to work. The Board’s practice was to award benefits only where the stress claim was connected to a specific traumatic event in the workplace. The board did not recognize stress claims associated with typical workplace pressures. Subsequently, the Appeals Tribunal expanded the boundaries of entitlement by allowing claims for stress reactions to normal workplace events based on a modified reasonable person test. In the tribunal’s view, chronic stress claims should be compensated in the same way as other gradual process injuries. For example, in 717/88, [1992] O.W.C.A.T.D. No. 713 (QL) the worker claimed that a diagnosed psychiatric condition of phobic anxiety and severe clinical depression was the result of work-induced stress. In allowing the appeal, the Appeals Tribunal held that the workplace stressors need not be unusual or unexpected and that the test would be based on objective evidence of work stressors that a “reasonable person” could plausibly find stressful. The majority concluded that while there were personal stressors and a disposition to a stress-related disability, the evidence did not negate the fact that work was a “significant contributing factor.” In a later decision, 363/91, [1994] O.W.C.A.T.D. No. 83 (QL), the tribunal adopted a modified objective reasonable person test which required balancing objective evidence of stress in the workplace against the worker’s perceptions of and subjective reaction to workplace conditions.

 

[31]                                   Understandably, the divergent approaches taken by the Ontario board and the Appeals Tribunal generated uncertainty, leading to substantial litigation. In 1996, the Ontario government commissioned a report covering the issues of chronic stress and chronic pain. The report recommended exclusion of compensation for chronic stress claims for the reason that “there can be little assurance that, in any one case, the employment has in fact caused a psychological disability that has so many other potential non-work causes” (see Cam Jackson, New Directions for Workers’ Compensation Reform: A Discussion Paper (Toronto: Minister Without Portfolio Responsible for Workers’ Compensation Reform, 1996)). In 1997, the Ontario government responded by amending its legislation to exclude claims for mental stress, except where the disability arose from an acute reaction to a traumatic event (see An Act to Secure the Financial Stability of the Compensation System for Injured Workers, to Promote the Prevention of Injury and Disease in Ontario Workplaces and to Revise the Workers’ Compensation Act and Make Related Amendments to Other Acts, S.O. 1997, c. 16). However, the amendments go further. They state that a worker is not entitled to benefits for mental stress caused by the employer’s decisions or actions relating to the employment. The amendment refers specifically to management decisions involving a change of work or work conditions, a decision to discipline an employee or to terminate the employment (see Workplace Safety and Insurance Act, S.O. 1997, c. 16, Sched. A, s. 13(5)).  Together, the amended provisions confirmed the Ontario board’s traditional approach to compensation for mental stress.

 

[32]                                   With the amendments to the Ontario legislation the board adopted a new policy which distinguishes between gradual reactions to general workplace conditions, which are not compensable, and delayed acute reactions to sudden and unexpected traumatic events. For example, an employee who, weeks after the event, feels stress related to witnessing a serious work accident would be entitled to benefits, while an employee who becomes disabled by stress following weeks of severe pressure in the workplace does not have an entitlement. The policy also calls for a “reasonable person” standard to be applied in determining whether an event is “traumatic.” The policy goes on to state that such events are “usually horrific, or have elements of actual or threatened violence to the worker.” The event must be uncommon in the normal course of the worker’s employment. The “thin skull” principle does not apply. The event must be traumatic on an objective basis. A worker who, due to personality or psychological history, reacts strongly to a relatively innocuous incident has no entitlement under the policy. On the other hand, an event may still be considered traumatic even if others in the workplace do not share the worker’s reaction, or if the worker had witnessed similar events in the past without experiencing a stress reaction (e.g. ambulance driver). In 2002, the board adopted a policy with respect to workplace harassment. Under that policy, harassment may be viewed as a series of traumatic events giving rise to injury. In a related policy change, persons working in stressful jobs such as health care, firefighting and policing are entitled to claim a stress injury due to the cumulative effects of events which occur on the job.

 

[33]                                   Manitoba was the first province to amend its workers’ compensation legislation for purposes of adopting a general rule excluding stress claims. In 1991, its legislation was amended to define “occupational disease” to exclude stress other than an acute reaction to a traumatic event (see The Workers Compensation Amendment and Consequential Amendments Act, S.M. 1991, c. 36).  New Brunswick followed suit in 1992, but the wording of the amendment differs from the other provinces in that the exclusion clause states that the term “accident” does not include “the disablement of mental stress or a disablement caused by mental stress” before acknowledging the traumatic event exception. Newfoundland amended its legislation in 1998 to define “injury” as excluding stress other than stress which qualifies as an acute reaction to a traumatic event (see Act to Amend the Workers’ Compensation Act, S.N. 1998, c. 19).  By contrast, the other Acts simply exclude “stress”, subject to the traumatic event exception. This is true of the Nova Scotia amendment adopted in 1995 (see An Act to Reform the Law Respecting Compensation for Workers, S.N.S. 1995, c. 10). Prince Edward Island’s amending legislation of 2001 uses the same language as does the British Columbia amendment adopted in 2002 (see An Act to Amend the Workers Compensation Act, S.P.E.I. 2001, c. 20 and Workers Compensation Amendment Act, S.B.C. 2002, c. 56). However, the British Columbia amendment goes further than any other. It imposes a requirement that stress claims be supported by a physician’s or psychologist’s diagnosis that the mental stress fell within the American Psychiatric Association's Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 4th ed. (Washington, D.C.: American Psychiatric Association, 1994) (DSM-IV). Finally, Saskatchewan and Alberta are the only Provinces that have not adopted amending legislation to deal with stress-related claims.   

 

[34]                                   I agree that the question of whether the claimant experienced an “acute reaction to a traumatic event” should not be approached as though it were a question of fact only. To persist with the view that the decision-maker is entitled to exercise an informed but personal judgment as to whether an event is traumatic and the reaction acute is to invite arbitrariness. There is no assurance that similarly situated claimants will be treated alike or that future claimants will be able to evaluate the merits of pursuing a claim. The Commission’s two policies covering stress-related claims commend themselves, if only for the reason that they introduce an element of objectivity and certainty into the decision-making process. This is true for both the Commission’s adjudicators and claimants. However, the law is clear that neither this Court nor the Appeals Tribunal is bound by Commission policies (see Green v. Workplace Health, Safety and Compensation Commission (N.B.) (1998), 201 N.B.R. (2d) 93 (C.A.) at para. 14; Melanson v. Workers’ Compensation Board (N.B.) (1994), 146 N.B.R. (2d) 294 (C.A.); Dwyer v. Workplace Health, Safety and Compensation Commission (N.B.) (1996), 179 N.B.R. (2d) 348 (C.A.) and Myles v. Workplace Health, Safety and Compensation Commission (N.B.) (1996), 181 N.B.R. (2d) 183 (C.A.)). Ultimately, it is the responsibility of this Court to interpret the provisions of the legislation in a manner that is in keeping with interpretative principles.

 

[35]                                   The first of the two Commission policies dealing with stress-related claims is Policy No. 21-103. It has the stated purpose of establishing “guidelines” for the adjudication of stress-related claims. It begins with definitions for the following terms: acute reaction, traumatic event and post-traumatic stress disorder:    

 

Acute Reaction – An acute reaction is one that can be established as being sharp in effect, serious or critical. It is not chronic.

 

Traumatic event – A ‘traumatic’ event involves a physical injury or wound caused by external force or violence, or an emotional or psychological shock that may produce disordered feeling or behaviour. 

 

Post-traumatic stress disorder – According to the American Psychiatric Association, “Post-traumatic Stress Disorder is a disorder which arises following a psychologically distressing event that is outside the range of usual human experience (i.e. outside the range of such common experiences as simple bereavement, chronic illness, business losses and marital conflict). The stressor producing this syndrome would be markedly distressing to anyone, and is usually, [but not always], experienced with intense fear, terror and helplessness.”

 

 

[36]                                   The policy goes on to  outline three criteria to be applied by staff when assessing mental stress claims: (1) the disability must not be due to chronic stress; (2) the event must directly produce a condition which meets established diagnostic criteria; and (3) the event (the accident) arose out of and in the course of employment. The policy goes on to exclude stress-related claims caused by ongoing personal or employment conditions or normal workplace discipline and management labour relations. In turn, the policy’s definition of acute reaction excludes a chronic or gradual onset reaction. A traumatic event is defined to involve a physical injury or wound caused by external force or violence or an emotional shock that may produce disordered feelings or behaviour. The requirement that the event must produce a condition which meets established diagnostic criteria is further addressed in Policy No. 21-103.01. That policy provides Commission staff with “guidelines” to be considered in cases involving post-traumatic stress disorder as described in the third revised edition of the American Psychiatric Association's Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 3rd ed. (Washington, D.C.: American Psychiatric Association, 1987). 

 

[37]                                   The second of the two Commission policies dealing with stress-related claims is Policy 21-103.01. It describes the essential feature of a post-traumatic stress disorder as “the development of characteristic symptoms following a psychologically distressing event that is outside the range of usual experience.” It goes on to state that for compensation purposes the event must be acute, not cumulative, and extraordinary in nature in comparison with those events in the normal scope of the worker’s employment. The disability must not arise out of the usual activities of employment or events inherent in the job. Policy 21-103.01 also states that a traumatic event must be demonstrated by “clear and concise evidence” and is to be measured by “objective standards and actual events”, rather than the worker’s perceptions. That requirement is followed by the direction to Commission staff that the event must directly produce a condition which meets established diagnostic criteria and, in turn, the diagnosed condition must produce a disability which is demonstrable based on appropriate testing. The policy outlines in detail the symptoms of one suffering from post-traumatic stress disorder.  

 

[38]                                   In summary, the Commission’s policies lay down the following directives for assessing stress-related claims. First, the traumatic event must have arisen out of and in the course of employment. Second, a traumatic event is defined as an event that is sudden and unexpected: one that is outside the range of usual human experience. Third, an employment decision affecting the terms and conditions of employment does not qualify as a traumatic event. Fourth, cases of chronic or gradual onset stress, being cumulative in nature, do not qualify as a traumatic event. Fifth, what is or is not a traumatic event must be measured objectively. Sixth, a claim will only succeed if the worker is diagnosed as having the symptoms of one who suffers from a post-traumatic stress disorder. I am in general agreement with all of the propositions, save the last. My formal reasoning is as follows.

 

[39]                                     It goes without saying that the traumatic event which precipitates the employee’s inability to work must arise out of and in the course of the employment, as do all injury claims. As the causation issue was addressed above, I will say no more of this requirement.

 

[40]                                   It is safe to conclude that the 1992 amendment to the Act’s definition of accident was intended to curtail the willingness of administrative tribunals to grant benefits for what are commonly referred to as either “chronic” or “gradual onset” stress claims. Prior to the amendment, it was open to argument that if a worker is entitled to compensation benefits because of a gradual onset physical injury so too should that worker be entitled to compensation in circumstances where work-related stress culminates in his or her inability to work. This explains why the 1992 amendment begins with the general rule that compensation is not available for “the disablement of mental stress or a disablement caused by mental stress.” As noted, this aspect of the Act’s definition of accident differs from that found in the other Provinces mentioned above.

 

[41]                                   It is easy to grasp the significance of a “disablement caused by mental stress.” Stress is the product of the psychological or emotional pressure that we experience in both our personal and work lives. Often it is difficult to isolate the stress in one situation from that in the other and to determine the impact that stress in one domain has on stress in the other. But there may come a point that the “stressors” encountered in the workplace lead to the inability to function in a work environment. The clearest manifestation of this involves employees who suffer what is commonly referred to as “burnout.” A claim for compensation benefits in such circumstances is outside the purview of the definition of accident. This view is reinforced when one turns to the exception to the general rule outlined in the definition of accident. The inability to work must arise from a traumatic event which produces an “acute reaction.” The notion that the event must induce an “acute” reaction displaces the notion that a compensation claim based on “chronic stress” qualifies as a compensable accident.  

 

[42]                                   As easy as it may be to grasp the concept of a disablement resulting from stress, the same cannot be said of the expression “disablement of mental stress.” No where have I been able to find any reference to the meaning of that expression. Perhaps the draftsperson was contemplating the situation in which “chronic stress” would be advanced as the reason for the inability to work. Perhaps, it was intended to reinforce the notion that work-related stress claims are outside the purview of the definition of accident. Otherwise, it is difficult to give meaning to this expression. This leads us to consider the scope of the exception to the general rule, namely, an acute reaction to a traumatic event. 

 

[43]                                   What is a traumatic event within the meaning of s. 1 of the Workers’ Compensation Act? Is that meaning consistent with the interpretation set out in the Commission’s policy which is reproduced above? Frankly, the Commission’s formal definition is unhelpful (“psychological shock that may produce disordered feeling or behaviour”). However, the descriptive portion of the policy refers to a traumatic event in terms of one that is sudden and outside the realm of what is expected or usual within the workplace. Applying that understanding, it necessarily follows that management decisions involving changes to terms and conditions of employment do not qualify as traumatic events. The same is true with respect to the decision to impose a lay-off or to terminate employment, with or without just cause. Such decisions may well generate stress, but that is to be expected.  

 

[44]                                   As easy as it is to conclude that management decisions do not qualify as traumatic events, it is universally accepted that employees who suffer from a post-traumatic stress disorder qualify for compensation benefits, provided of course the traumatic event arose out of and in the course of employment. Inevitably, such a diagnosis is made according to the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, 4th ed. (DSM-IV). According to that manual the essential feature of a post-traumatic stress disorder is that the distressing event must be outside the range of usual human experience. It is one that would be markedly distressing to any person and is usually, but not always, experienced with intense fear, terror and helplessness. The worker may be directly involved in the traumatic event or a witness thereto. The event will be sudden as well as a frightening or shocking experience. Invariably, a traumatic event involves actual or threatened death or serious injury or a threat to one’s physical integrity. Examples of a traumatic event are the employee who is a victim of a robbery or hostage-taking incident; the employee who witnesses the death or severe injury of a co-worker or who is the first to respond to victims of severe physical trauma or fatalities. 

 

[45]                                   I pause here to note that the Commission’s Policy Directive 21-103.01 states that: “[m]erely being a witness to an event does not qualify the occurrence as being a traumatic event.” If the intent of that statement is to establish the proposition that a witness to a traumatic event cannot be diagnosed with a post-traumatic stress disorder, that statement is in error. As well, the following statement from that policy directive must be read with caution: “[f]or compensation purposes, the event must be acute, not cumulative, and extraordinary in nature in comparison with those events in the normal scope of the worker’s employment.” First, it is clear that it is the “event” that must be “traumatic” and the reaction to the traumatic event which must be “acute.” Second, the notion of cumulative events must be approached with caution. It is true that the cumulative effects of stressors experienced within the workplace do not qualify as a traumatic event. The cumulative effect of a series of work-related events may lead to a worker’s inability to continue with employment, in which case there is no right to compensation benefits. On the other hand, there are cases where the worker is exposed to a series of traumatic events which over time lead to a diagnosis of post-traumatic stress syndrome. Delayed onset of the syndrome is not unusual for those who are required, for example, to provide emergency care to accident victims. Thus, the cumulative effect of a series of events is relevant, provided each occurrence qualifies as a traumatic event.

 

[46]                                     To this point, I have identified the two categories of cases for which it is a relatively simple task to predict whether or not the employee’s stress-related claim will succeed. On the one hand, there are those cases where the employee receives a professional diagnosis of a post-traumatic stress disorder or syndrome, using the DSM-IV. On the other hand, stress claims tied to the implementation of typical management decisions are doomed to failure. But not all cases will fall neatly within either category. For example, case law reveals that stress claims involving “harassment” in the workplace require further consideration (see Canada Post Corp. v. Nova Scotia (Workers’ Compensation Appeals Tribunal), [2004] N.S.J. No. 242 (C.A.) (QL)).  

 

[47]                                   With respect to the indeterminate cases, the crucial issue is whether the notion of a traumatic event should be measured by either a subjective or objective test.  The argument is as follows.  The appellant does not accept the Commission’s definition of traumatic event. That is to say, she does not agree that the event must be one that is sudden and outside the realm of what is expected and usual in the workplace. The appellant maintains that such a definition embraces an objective test for gauging whether an event is or is not traumatic when, in law, the test should be a subjective one. The appellant insists that a traumatic event must be measured in terms of what is traumatic to the claimant and not what the reasonable person would regard as traumatic. There is no doubt that if we adopt the subjective test we will be effectively applying the “thin skull” principle. What may be a traumatic event for one employee may not be for another. An employee with a pre-existing emotional or psychological condition may react differently to a precipitous event than an employee who is not so burdened. In the present case, the appellant maintains that her pre-existing mental condition arose from the poor working conditions which she had been enduring for several months. The alleged traumatic event was the confrontational exchange between the appellant and her supervisor. 

 

[48]                                   The law should not readily embrace an entirely subjective test when adjudicating on compensation claims. Typically what happens in the law is that an objective test is adopted with a subjective component. At some point, the reaction of the employee to the precipitous event must be measured against the reasonable person standard. If that is so then the test would have to be reformulated as follows: Would a reasonable person standing in the shoes of the claimant, and possessing the same psychological profile, have reacted in the same way.  In law, this reformulation is labeled the “modified objective test.” By contrast, a purely objective test imposes the reasonable person standard without reference, for example, to the reasonable fears, concerns and circumstances of the claimant. See generally Arndt v. Smith, [1997] 2 S.C.R. 539.

 

[49]                                   In support of her argument, the appellant cites three administrative appeal decisions emanating from Ontario. However, two of the cases were decided prior to the adoption of the amending legislation in 1997. The third case is not on point as it deals with the problem of psychotraumatic disability (psychological injury arising from a physical injury) discussed earlier in these reasons (see 916/94, [1995] O.W.C.A.T.D. No. 1116 (QL); 1763/99, [1999] O.W.S.I.A.T.D. No. 2233 (QL) and 576 02, [2002] O.W.S.I.A.T.D. No. 1064 (QL)).

 

[50]                                   The appellant also cites a number of court decisions but recognizes that at the time the cases were decided the legislation in the relevant provinces did not have the same restrictive definition of accident as is found in New Brunswick’s Act (see Saunders v. Workers’ Compensation Commission (Nfld.) et al. (1998), 166 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 227 (Nfld. S.C.); Smith v. Newfoundland (Workplace Health, Safety and Compensation Review Commission), [2002] N.J. No. 150 (S.C.) (QL); Harry v. Alberta (Workers’ Compensation Board), [2001] A.J. No. 1384 (Q.B.) (QL); and Dowling v. Workers’ Compensation Board (P.E.I.) (1994), 124 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 358 (P.E.I.S.C.(A.D.))).   

 

[51]                                   In my view, the test for assessing whether an event is traumatic must be an objective one. If it were a purely subjective test or even a modified objective test, the most innocuous of management decisions could support a claim for psychological injury. It would not be difficult for the skilled advocate to turn a case of “chronic” or “gradual onset” stress into a claim of psychological injury by focusing on a single incident; the one that broke the camel’s back, so to speak. The overly sensitive employee who is experiencing a severely stressful home or work life might well suffer an acute reaction to a critical management decision. A decision to lay an employee off work or to terminate employment, with or without just cause, may well lead to depression and the inability to find alternative work. But are these the types of claims for which the Legislature intended that compensation benefits would be available?  I think not. To hold otherwise would be to sanction a regime in which the exception to the rule would become the rule. In my view, a subjective or modified objective test would be incompatible with the object of the 1992 amendment and its wording. For these reasons, the Commission was correct in imposing an objective test for deciding whether an event is a traumatic one. The question properly formulated is whether the reasonable person would regard the precipitous event as a traumatic one (out of the usual, expected or ordinary) because it is the type of occurrence that could realistically result in an employee being unable to continue with his or her employment.

 

[52]                                   There is one other aspect of the Commission’s policies that requires consideration. The appellant challenges the understanding that the claimant’s acute reaction to the traumatic event must manifest itself in the form of a post-traumatic stress disorder. Commission Policy 21-103.01 supports the understanding that a stress-related claim will only succeed if the claimant is able to lead evidence that he or she has the symptoms of post-traumatic stress syndrome. This leads me to ask whether a diagnosis of a post-traumatic stress disorder, as set out in the DSM-IV, is a condition precedent to establishing a stress-related claim under the Workers’ Compensation Act. I am not prepared to go this far. Were that the intention of the Legislature it would have been easy for it to adopt a provision to this effect as was done in British Columbia. In the absence of such an express provision, I am willing to accept that, in theory, a worker may suffer an acute reaction to a traumatic event even though he or she has not been diagnosed as suffering from a post-traumatic stress disorder or syndrome. This approach leaves open, for example, the possibility that the intentional infliction of mental harm through harassment in the workplace may support a stress-related claim whether or not it falls within the DSM-IV. Perhaps the question of whether a DSM-IV diagnosis is a condition precedent to a claim for compensation benefits for psychological injury is more of a theoretical construct than a pragmatic one, once it is accepted that the test to be applied in assessing whether an event is traumatic is an objective test. At the same time, none of this should be taken to discourage claimants from seeking a professional opinion as to whether they are suffering from a post-traumatic stress disorder diagnosed in accordance with the criteria set out in the DSM-IV. This is a question that all physicians and psychologists should address when formulating their professional opinion as to whether the claimant suffered a psychological injury following a work incident.   

 

V.        Application of Legal Framework to Facts of Case

 

[53]                                   Applying the legal framework outlined above, it is not difficult to conclude on the facts of this case that neither the Commission nor the Appeals Tribunal erred in dismissing the appellant’s claim for stress-related benefits. My reasons are as follows. 

 

[54]                                       To establish a right to stress-related benefits the appellant had to show that the alleged traumatic event arose out of and in the course of employment. On the facts of this case that condition precedent has been satisfied.

 

[55]                                   The next issue is whether the event in question qualifies as a traumatic event in the objective sense. Accepting that a traumatic event is one that is sudden and unexpected or outside the usual range of human experience, it must be asked whether a reasonable person would regard a non-violent confrontation between the claimant and her supervisor as capable of inducing an acute reaction leading to the employee’s inability to work. I accept that confrontations in which the employee is the recipient of a verbal rant are unacceptable. It is one thing to wrongly discipline employees and quite another to do it in a manner that infringes their sense of dignity and personal worth. But are such confrontations outside the range of human experience in the workplace?  I think not.

 

[56]                                   Objectively speaking, I do not believe that the reasonable person would regard the incident in question as one that could realistically or plausibly lead to a psychological injury that would prevent the employee from continuing with his or her work. An isolated instance of management yelling at an employee does not even support an allegation of harassment, which in appropriate circumstances, might well support a claim of constructive dismissal. Admittedly, the employee may feel ill at ease at returning to the workplace so long as the offending supervisor remains in authority. But even if there are valid reasons for refusing to return to work, those reasons cannot be transformed into a psychological injury. Of course, if the real reason for not returning to work is to avoid future confrontations, the claim for work-related stress benefits has not been made out. The claimant should seek unemployment benefits under the federal unemployment insurance regime or possibly file a grievance under a collective agreement.

 

[57]                                   If there is doubt as to whether an incident qualifies as a traumatic event, it is proper to ask whether it is more probable than not that the facts of the case support a claim of chronic stress. In the present, the appellant’s psychiatrist stated in his report that the appellant had been suffering from a “Major Depression” prior to the incident in question and that the problem with “her mood” started with the change to her new work environment and the problems encountered with respect to the poor lighting and noise conditions. The report goes on to state that as the appellant had no history of receiving psychiatric care, “work-related stress is one of the major contributing factors toward her problem with her mood.” In my view, this evidence supports the inference that the appellant was suffering from chronic stress and that the confrontation with her supervisor was simply the last of a series of cumulative events that triggered the appellant’s inability to continue with her employment. In my opinion, this is the true basis on which the Appeals Tribunal dismissed the appellant’s appeal.

 

[58]                                   Finally, I was able to find one Nova Scotia decision in which the Appeals Tribunal of that Province held that a claimant was entitled to benefits following an “intense meeting” with a supervisor. However, the Appeals Tribunal found that the meeting was “personal”, “violent” and “aggressive.” There was also evidence to support the Appeals Tribunal’s inference that there was “a real and imminent threat that the meeting was about to become physically violent.” The claimant also adduced a medical opinion that he had suffered a work-related post-traumatic stress syndrome. The facts of that case are materially different from those under consideration (see Children’s Aid Society of Cape Breton-Victoria v. Nova Scotia (Workers’ Compensation Appeals Tribunal), [2005] N.S.J. No. 75 (C.A.) (QL)).

 

[59]                                   In conclusion, this is not a case where the claimant has established that she comes within the exception to the general rule.  In brief, the verbal tirade directed at the appellant by her supervisor does not qualify as a traumatic event. In these circumstances, it is unnecessary to determine whether the event caused an acute reaction leading to her inability to work.

 

VI.       Two Issues That Were Not Raised

 

[60]                                   I have identified two issues that were not raised on this appeal that warrant a passing comment or two. First, I am acutely aware of the Supreme Court’s ruling in Nova Scotia (Workers' Compensation Board) v. Martin; Nova Scotia (Workers' Compensation Board) v. Laseur, [2003] 2 S.C.R. 504. In that case, it was held that a provision of the Nova Scotia legislation denying workers’ compensation benefits for “chronic pain” offended s. 15 of the Charter and was not saved under s. 1. Specifically, it was found that those workers who suffered from chronic pain following a physical and job-related injury were being discriminated against, on the basis of disability, when compared to those injured workers who did not suffer from chronic pain. This leads one to ask whether the exclusion of “chronic stress” claims from the definition of accident found in s. 1 of our Workers’ Compensation Act offends the Charter. The difficulty I would have with such a challenge is in identifying the relevant comparator group. The idea that employees who suffer from work-related chronic stress are being discriminated against when compared to those who do not suffer such an affliction appears nonsensical. Those who do not suffer from chronic stress will not be in receipt of benefits and, hence, it is difficult, if not impossible, to see how the issue of discrimination can arise.

 

[61]                                   The second non-issue stems from the fact that Via Rail is a federal employer. As I understand the legislative scheme, federal employees may be entitled to file a claim for workers’ compensation benefits under a provincial scheme pursuant to the Government Employees Compensation Act, R.S.C. 1985, c. G-5. The general purpose of GECA is to provide compensation to federal workers on the same terms and conditions as their provincial counterparts. For example, in Nova Scotia, the provincial scheme governs claims submitted under GECA, provided two conditions are met. One of those conditions is that a provision of the provincial legislation must not be in conflict with GECA. In that regard the federal legislation does not expressly exclude chronic or gradual onset stress from the definition of “accident.” This leads one to ask whether their exclusion under the provincial legislation applies to claims made under GECA, an issue touched on by the Nova Scotia Court of Appeal in two decisions (see Morrison (Estate) v. Cape Breton Development Corp., [2003] N.S.J. No. 353 (C.A.) (QL) and Canada Post v. Nova Scotia). 

 

[62]                                   The applicability of GECA and the potential conflict with the Workers’ Compensation Act did not escape the appellant’s representative. Before appealing the Commission’s decision to the Appeals Tribunal, he wrote to the Commission asking whether the appellant’s eligibility for benefits arose under the provincial legislation or under a tri-partite agreement entered into by the federal and provincial governments and Via Rail [Whether or not such an agreement exists is another question]. The Commission responded that as the appellant worked for Via Rail, she was not considered a federal employee governed by GECA. The Commission opined that as the appellant was a resident of and worked in New Brunswick she was covered under the provincial legislation. With great respect, the logic employed eludes me. Furthermore, the Commission’s position appears to be at odds with its position in Canada Post Corp. v. New Brunswick (Workplace Health, Safety and Compensation Commission), [2003] N.B.J. No. 151 (C.A.). As the issue was not raised before the Appeals Tribunal, or this Court, I will refrain from further comment.

 

VII.      Disposition

 

[63]                                   I would dismiss the appeal. This is not a case where costs should be awarded. Were it not for the appellant, the correctness of the Commission’s policy directives would never have been questioned.
