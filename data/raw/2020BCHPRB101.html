Review of Inquiry Committee Disposition – Summary:

 

Stage 2 hearing of an application for review of an Inquiry Committee complaint disposition under s. 50.6 HPA – Matter sent back to the Inquiry Committee with directions. The Complainant complained to the College about the professional conduct of the Registrant, a chiropractor who owned and operated a multidisciplinary clinic where the Complainant was employed as a contracted massage therapist. The Complainant claimed that the Registrant did not honour their verbal agreements, changed agreements to benefit himself, made up debts incurred and withheld pay from the Complainant’s cheques. The Complainant also alleged that the Registrant did not secure patient records and that he refused to release patient records when the Complainant left the clinic. According to the Complainant, the Registrant had a clear lack of understanding of College Bylaws and Regulations as they pertain to the College of Chiropractors of BC as well as other Colleges regulating the contracted registrants practicing in the clinic. The College Registrar responded to the Complainant that the College is a regulatory body established under the Health Professions Act to regulate chiropractors in their practice as it relates to patient care and the interest of the public. The Registrar concluded that the matter between the Complainant and the Registrant pertained to business and/or contractual issues, making it a civil matter, and not for a regulatory body to contend with. The Registrar disposed of the compliant without consideration from the Inquiry Committee. The Review Board found that the Disposition was not reasonable as the Deputy Registrar did not recognize, investigate or consider elements of the complaint that ranged from unprofessional conduct to management of patient records, a responsibility that the College is delegated to oversee under the Act. The Review Board concluded that the Investigation was not adequate because the College dismissed the complaints without investigating whatsoever. The Review Board made an order sending the matter back to the Inquiry Committee for reconsideration with directions and authorization for the Inquiry Committee to consider any submissions made by the Complainant and the Registrant for the purpose of this review.

 

DECISION

I               INTRODUCTION

[1]          This is an application to review the decision of the Deputy Registrar of the College to dismiss a complaint under s.32(3)(b) of the Act.

[2]          Section 32(3)(b) of the Act authorizes the Registrar to dismiss a complaint on the ground that it “does not contain allegations that, if admitted or proven, would constitute a matter subject to investigation by the inquiry committee under section 33(4).”

[3]          The Registrant is a registrant of the College and runs a multi-disciplinary clinic (the "Clinic"). The Complainant is a massage therapist who treated patients at the Clinic two days per week under a contract with the Registrant (the "Contract") starting in July 2017. The relationship between the two quickly broke down and the Complainant stopped working at the Clinic at the end of October 2017.

[4]          The Complainant complained to the College on August 22, 2019, alleging among other things that the Registrant breached the College's bylaws and its Code of Ethics pertaining to record keeping and privacy, professional conduct and ethics, and office sharing arrangements. He also complained that the Registrant's ongoing behaviour prevents the Complainant from complying with the bylaws of his own governing body, the College of Massage Therapists of British Columbia (the "CMTBC").

[5]          On August 26, 2019, the Deputy Registrar of the College advised the Complainant that he had dismissed the complaint on the basis that the complaint was about “business or contractual issues” and “there does not appear to be any evidence of patient care issues…” (the "Disposition"). On the same date, the Deputy Registrar notified the Registrant that the complaint had been dismissed.

[6]          In response to the Deputy Registrar’s August 26, 2019 letter, the Complainant wrote back the same day alleging that he had raised several practice issues, related to consent to share records, records storage, the withholding of patient records, the impact of withholding on the Complainant’s ability to meet his professional obligations as a massage therapist, and possible fraudulent action by the Registrant. The Deputy Registrar responded in a letter dated August 28, 2019 providing more detail for his decision (the “Follow-up Letter").

[7]          The Disposition was confirmed by the College's Inquiry Committee on September 11, 2019.

[8]          The Complainant applied to the Review Board for a review of the Disposition on September 19, 2019.

II             ROLE OF REVIEW BOARD

[9]          Upon an application for review by a Complainant pursuant to s.50.6(5) of the Act, the Review Board provides independent reviews of complaint dispositions of the inquiry committees of the health profession colleges of British Columbia. These are reviews of investigations and dispositions of complaints made by, or considered to be made by, inquiry committees; they are not fresh investigations or dispositions.

III            LEGISLATION CONCERNING REVIEW

[10]       Section 50.6(5) of the Act defines what the Review Board must consider:

50.6(5) On receipt of an application under subsection (1), the review board must conduct a review of the disposition and must consider one or both of the following:

(a)  the adequacy of the investigation conducted respecting the complaint;

(b)  the reasonableness of the disposition.

[11]       Upon completion of the review, I may make one of the orders pursuant to s.50.6(8) of the Act:

(a)  confirming the disposition of the inquiry committee

(b)  directing the inquiry committee to make a disposition that could have been made by the inquiry committee in the matter; or

(c)  sending the matter back to the inquiry committee for reconsideration with directions.

IV           STAGE 1 OR STAGE 2 HEARING

[12]       As set out in Rule 44 of its Rules of Practice and Procedure for Reviews under the Health Professions Act, R.S.B.C. 1996, c. 183, the Review Board will conduct a Stage 1 hearing before determining whether to conduct a Stage 2 hearing. In a Stage 1 hearing the Review Board's review is based solely on the record of investigation provided by the College (the "Record") and submissions from the Complainant.

[13]       On December 11, 2019, I directed that this matter proceed as a Stage 2 hearing both as to the adequacy of the investigation and the reasonableness of the disposition. I received submissions from the College, Registrant and Complainant.

V             THE ALLEGATIONS

[14]       To dispose of a complaint under s.32(3)(b) of the Act, the Deputy Registrar must be satisfied that even if the complaint allegations were admitted or proven, they would not fall under any of the categories that can be investigated under s.33(4):

33(4) The inquiry committee may, on its own motion, investigate a registrant regarding any of the following matters:

(a)     contravention of this Act, the regulations or the bylaws;

(a.1) a conviction for an indictable offence;

(b)     a failure to comply with a standard, limit or condition imposed under this Act;

(c)     professional misconduct or unprofessional conduct;

(d)     competence to practise the designated health profession;

(e)     a physical or mental ailment, an emotional disturbance or an addiction to alcohol or drugs that impairs his or her ability to practise the designated health profession.

[15]       It follows that for the Review Board to meaningfully review the reasonableness of the Deputy Registrar’s decision that this complaint fell outside the scope of professional regulation, the starting point must be a review of the complaint allegations, which he had to take as “admitted or proven” when acting under s.32(3)(b) of the Act.

[16]       The complaint alleged that in June and July 2017, the Complainant and the Registrant discussed the possibility of the Complainant working in the Clinic and the terms of a Contract. The Complainant started training on July 19, 2017 and signed the Contract on August 15, 2017. He says that he trusted that the Contract would accurately reflect the terms he had agreed on with the Registrant, but this turned out to not be the case.

[17]       The Complainant alleged that he told the Registrant during an interview that the intake form for registered massage therapist services at the Clinic was "not completely safe" as it did not provide for a patient to consent to different therapists looking at each other's charts for the same patient and did not state that records made by each therapist would remain at the Clinic if the therapist left, rather than the files being taken by the therapist who created them. The Complainant noted that the CMTBC bylaws require each therapist to maintain his or her own records and the records are to remain with the therapist who created them unless the patient consents to someone else keeping the records. He alleged that the Clinic has two files for each patient, one for massage therapy and one for chiropractic services and the Registered Massage Therapists (“RMTs”) or chiropractors share the patient's file between them accordingly.

[18]       The Complainant alleged that when he asked the Registrant early on whether he could add a line to the consent form for this practice to continue, the Registrant stated that this had never been a problem in the past “and that if it ever became a problem, the clinic would be in trouble, not me as the practitioner, and the clinic’s lawyers would take care of it…”

[19]       The Complainant alleged that conflicts arose in September 2017, when he took a day off due to the flu and needed to reschedule patients. The Complainant alleged that the Registrant demanded that he give free massage treatments and purchase gift cards for patients who were inconvenienced. This was not a term of the Contract. The Complainant refused to provide free treatments and says the Registrant deducted money from his pay cheque to compensate patients, without the Complainant's permission.

[20]       There were further problems when one of the Complainant's patients broke a shelf and when another was inadvertently overcharged. The Complainant alleged that the Registrant underpaid him and was extremely condescending and abusive, verbally and in writing.

[21]       The two agreed that they should no longer work together and that the Complainant's last day at the Clinic would be November 11, 2017. However, the Complainant alleged that on October 28, 2017, he saw the Registrant removing patients from his schedule, including for entire days, without asking his permission or notifying him. The Complainant says that this cost him money and interfered with the treatment plans of patients who had specifically requested him as their primary care provider at the Clinic and patients who returned to the Complainant regularly were unable to receive treatment with him.

[22]       Despite agreeing his last day would be November 11, 2017, October 28, 2017 was the Complainant's last day working at the Clinic. On that date, the Registrant took issue with the Complainant writing down on a sticky note the names of his patients, the time and date of their treatment and amount charged, as required by the CMTBC Bylaws, to transcribe into a notebook when he got home. He explained this to the Registrant, but the Registrant was threatening and hostile, accused him of stealing patients' contact information so that he could contact them after he left and told him that the front desk was monitored with surveillance cameras.

[23]       The Complaint also referred to further communications, in which the Registrant “persisted with rudeness, harassment, verbal abuse and condescending remarks,” which he alleged was conduct unbecoming of a chiropractor and clinic owner.

[24]       The Complainant alleged that on November 8, 2017, after he had stopped working at the Clinic, he told the Registrant that he needed to confirm the names of patients and their appointment times and wanted to check this information with the Clinic records. The Registrant responded in a “WhatsApp” group text (so others could see it) stating that he could not breach patient confidentiality by "giving a non-contracted person patient information."

[25]       The Complaint alleged that on November 17, 2017, there was a printed warning attached to the clinic window with a photo of two male trespassers unattended in the clinic. The Complainant felt this highlighted a patient record security issue and alleged:

The clinic stores their patient records on open shelves behind the reception desk which cannot be locked and they are clearly visible from the street.  The only line of defense is the front door.  This raises concerns about the security of my patients’ records and confidentiality.  Reception is only staffed part time and therapists often leave the waiting area unattended while in treatment with the front door to the clinic unlocked or, in the summer, entirely propped open. This lack of security clearly breaches the CCBC Bylaws regarding security of record storage (Part 7.73 – 1(b) and (d)).

[26]       The Complainant alleged that in January 2018, legal counsel for his Professional Association advised him that it was a breach of the Registered Massage Therapists’ Association of British Columbia (“RMTBC”) Bylaws for him not to have his client files and advised him to demand their return from the Registrant. The Complainant alleges that the Registrant did not respond to requests from the Complainant or legal counsel for the Association.

[27]       The Complaint alleged that in February 2018, the Complainant received what he considered to be threatening demand letters from the Registrant, delivered by a process server to the Complainant's place of business. The Complainant alleged that one of the letters contained “four pages of defamation,” including false allegations of insurance fraud, personal insults, false professional misconduct allegations and “many mentions of how he had discussed me in a bad light with coworkers of mine, patients, owners of other clinics and even the police.” The letters included “threats of further harassment, litigation and demands for rent payments” the Complainant considered to be unfair and inappropriate. In March 2018, the Registrant sent demands for payment for the broken shelf and for legal fees. The Complainant responded to the Registrant’s allegations and demands by way of letter on July 20, 2018, to which the Registrant did not respond.

[28]       On July 23, 2018 the Complainant received a letter from the CMTBC informing him that the Registrant had made a formal complaint against him. That complaint was dismissed by the CMTBC on February 7, 2019.

[29]       On September 24, 2018, the Registrant again demanded payment of debts the Complainant considered to be false and told the Complainant that he had submitted the debts to a collection agency (which has never contacted him), and this would adversely affect the Complainant's credit rating.

[30]       The Complainant advised the College that he had copies of all written communications and would be happy to forward them along. His complaint also identified the name of another RMT who gave permission to use her name “in case CCBC wanted to reach out to her and investigate further.”

[31]       The Complaint summarized the allegations as follows:

To summarize, [the Registrant] has failed to honour verbal agreements, changed agreements to the detriment of patients and coworkers for his own benefit, and both fabricated debts (which might qualify as fraud) and withheld pay. He has failed to maintain adequate security of patient records and has assumed custody of records he has no claim to. [The Registrant] deliberately interfered with patient treatment plans by removing days and patients from my schedule without any communication with me. He has refused to communicate professionally, or communicate at all in some cases. [The Registrant] has harassed me and made strong attempts against my professional reputation and personal credit score. [The Registrant] also shows a clear lack of understanding of the bylaws governing both his own practice and those governing practitioners he contracts within his business. I believe having some familiarity with the bylaws which govern most of his contracted workforce (RMTs) would be a rudimentary responsibility to operating a multidisciplinary clinic.

[32]       The complaint attached excerpts from the College's bylaws, Professional Conduct Handbook, Code of Ethics and Standards, Limits and Conditions of Practice.

[33]       The complaint ends with the statement: "I hope to hear from the college soon to discuss this matter in more depth. Thank you for taking the time to look into these allegations."

VI           DISPOSITION OF COMPLAINT

[34]       As noted above, the College received the complaint on August 22, 2019. Four days later, on August 26, 2019, the Deputy Registrar of the College sent the Complainant the Disposition by email advising that he had dismissed the complaint and of the Complainant's right to apply for a review to the Review Board within 30 days.

[35]       The Disposition states:

You raise concerns about your business relationship with [the Registrant] during your time as an independent contractor at the office from June to November 2017.

The issues involve the business relationship, a contract between yourself and [the Registrant] and events that occurred after you left the office.

The CCCBC is a regulatory body established under the Health Professions Act. The Act sets out the duties of a College, in part:

Duty and objects of a college

16

(1)  It is the duty of a college at all times

(a)  to serve and protect the public, and

(b)  to exercise its powers and discharge its responsibilities under all enactments in the public interest.

(2)  A college has the following objects:

(a)  to superintend the practice of the profession;

We regulate chiropractors in their practice in relation to patient care and in the interests of the public. Yours are business and/or contractual issues. The contents of a contract are not a matter for a regulatory College to review, the CCBC or the CMTBC. A contract is a civil matter and any breach or enforcement is a matter for the civil courts.

In your letter, you mention defamation, fees, charges & payments of money issues with [the Registrant]. These too are civil matters.

There does not appear to be any evidence of patient care issues concerning [the Registrant], only your business relationship with him.

Therefore, having reviewed the complaint, I have determined that the allegations raised do not warrant disciplinary action.

Accordingly, I have decided to dismiss the complaint.

[36]       The Deputy Registrar sent the Registrant a letter on the same day, August 26, 2019, advising him that he had decided to dismiss the complaint because he had "determined that even if admitted or proved, the allegations raised would not constitute a matter subject to investigation by the College's Inquiry Committee."

[37]       Also, on August 26, 2019, after sending the emails to the Complainant and the Registrant advising of the Disposition, the Deputy Registrar sent an email to the Inquiry Committee:

I have dismissed this under HPA. 32(3): the complaint does not contain allegations that, if admitted or proven, would constitute a matter subject to investigation by the Inquiry Committee.

This email serves as notice to the IQC required under s. 32(4) and (5):

(4) If a complaint is disposed of under subsection (3), the registrar must deliver a written report to the inquiry committee about the circumstances of the disposition.

(5) A disposition under subsection (3) is considered to be a disposition by the inquiry committee unless the inquiry committee gives the registrar written direction to proceed under subsection (2).

We will review this at the next meeting.

However, as stated in 32(5). the IQC may now direct this matter be referred to the IQC for investigation. Please advise if you wish that to be done. [emphasis in original]

[38]       There is no record of the Deputy Registrar receiving a response to this email.

[39]       Also, on August 26, 2019, the Complainant wrote the Deputy Registrar a further letter asking for clarification as to why issues he raised relating to the Registrant's practice would fall outside the jurisdiction of the College.  Specifically, the Complainant stated:

In your response you only mentioned specifics of my conflict with [the Registrant] outside of his practice regarding his behavior towards me.  Do you have any interpretation of the issues I raised in the other aspects of [the Registrant’s] practice?

The matters I am referring to, specifically regarding [the Registrant’s] practice, include:

•         consent for records to be shared, which he did not have during my time at [the clinic];

•         [The Registrant’s] records storage system which would seem to be insufficient to meet the requirements established in the legislature;

•         [The Registrant’s] withholding of patient records from parties legally entitled to them and required to obtain them;

•         [The Registrant] may be committing fraudulent actions, which I outlined in the original complaint;

•         and by [the Registrant] withholding patient records, he is stopping me from upholding my requirements to PIPA and HPA as another registered professional.

Although I have no legal training, it would seem to me that these matters directly affect patient care and do not fall into the category of a contractual disagreement or a civil matter.

You also mentioned that my claims that [the Registrant] had harassed and defamed me would only be civil matters. I find this surprising because there is a code of ethics and a professional conduct handbook which outlines and mandates a certain level of professionalism and ethical uprightness be maintained by Chiropractors…

[40]       The Deputy Registrar responded in the Follow-up Letter of August 28, 2019 and advised that the matter was closed with the College. In his Follow-up Letter, the Deputy Registrar listed issues raised by the complaint with his response to each. He stated, "I do not question your motives in filing this complaint; rather I have determined that the allegations raised do not warrant disciplinary action."

[41]       I will refer to the Follow-up Letter in more detail later in these reasons.

[42]       The Inquiry Committee confirmed the Deputy Registrar's Disposition on September 11, 2019.

VII          APPLICATION FOR REVIEW

[43]       On September 19, 2019, the Complainant applied to the Review Board for a review of the disposition of his complaint. His application refers to the date of the disposition as August 28, 2019, the date of the Follow-up Letter.

[44]       However, it appears from the letter the Deputy Registrar wrote to the Review Board when enclosing the Record that, when the Inquiry Committee confirmed the decision of the Deputy Registrar, it was not provided with the Complainant's letter dated August 26, 2019 or the Deputy Registrar's Follow-Up Letter of August 28, 2019.

[45]       Therefore, strictly speaking, the Disposition for my review is the letter from the Deputy Registrar dated August 26, 2019, as confirmed by the Inquiry Committee on September 11, 2019.

[46]       It is unfortunate that the Inquiry Committee was not provided with the Complainant’s August 26, 2019, letter and the Deputy Registrar’s August 28, 2019, response. It is possible that those letters might have prompted the Inquiry Committee to use its power under s. 32(5) to direct an investigation. Be that as it may, and in order to avoid prolonging these proceedings unnecessarily, I will consider the reasons for the dismissal of the complaint given in both the Disposition and in the Follow-up Letter. I am prepared to read the Follow-up letter as setting out additional “justification” for the Disposition. In his Application for Review, the Complainant requests a review of the reasonableness of the Disposition because:

No questions were asked of me, nor was an investigation opened before my complaint was dismissed.  I believe I have written proof of professional misconduct & evidence to support other claims against a registrant, yet my complaint was disregarded….

I would like my complaint & evidences to be read & considered for their validity.

[47]       The Complainant asks the Review Board to direct the Inquiry Committee to take appropriate action to resolve the matter between him and the Registrant and to request in writing that the Registrant undertake not to repeat the conduct which is the subject of the complaint.

[49]       As set out in s.50.6(7) of the Act, the Review Board may hear evidence that is not part of the record as reasonably required for a full and fair disclosure of all matters related to the issues under review.

A.           College

[51]       The College delivered a brief affidavit (4 paragraphs) of the Deputy Registrar. The affidavit describes his authority and states:

[52]       The Deputy Registrar's affidavit also states:

[56]       With regard to para. [4] of the Affidavit, there is no statement that the Deputy Registrar actually considered the Registrant’s answers to the “self-review” questions regarding security of patient records. To refuse to investigate complaint allegations based on periodic self-reports would be to abdicate the complaints investigation function under the Act. I will therefore take into consideration para. [4] only as evidence that there is a Quality Assurance Program relating to requirements for patient file storage.

IX           DISCUSSION AND ANALYSIS

A.           Adequacy of Investigation

[68]       The Complainant's application for review does not challenge the adequacy of the investigation. However, it does state:

No questions were asked of me, nor was an investigation opened before my complaint was dismissed.  I believe I have written proof of professional misconduct & evidence to support other claims against a registrant, yet my complaint was disregarded.

[69]       The Complainant asks the Review Board to send his complaint to the Inquiry Committee to "perform an investigation into my allegations so they can gather the appropriate evidence to deliberate on disciplinary actions."

[70]       As already noted, the Registrant was not asked to respond to the complaint and was not sent a copy of the complaint until he was notified that it had been dismissed.  The Deputy Registrar did not review any documents, interview any witnesses, ask the Complainant to clarify his complaint or provide additional documents or information. 

[71]       The duty of the Review Board to intervene because there was an inadequate investigation was considered in Moore v. College of Physicians and Surgeons of British Columbia, 2013 BCSC 2081, affirmed for reasons of Supreme Court, 2014 BCCA 466, leave to appeal to SCC dismissed 2015 SCC 36272:

[121]    The Board must defer to the College in cases where the investigation, given the context of the complaint and the disposition of the College fall within a range of outcomes that are reasonable and rational. The Board must intervene where there is either no investigation or only a cursory investigation that is inconsistent with the nature of the complaint, or where even though there has been a proper, full investigation the disposition of the College is unreasonable.

[72]       In Complainant v. College of Chiropractors of British Columbia, 2014 BCHPRB 25 the Review Board stated:

[34]      It is clear that an investigation is inadequate if key information is missing, but equally it will be found to be inadequate if a decision is not defensible based upon lack of probing to determine what is key information. It is difficult for speculation to exist within an adequate investigation.

[35]      To be defensible an inquiry committee does not have to prove that no more key information exists (i.e. prove a negative) but it must have sought information to the point that it is probable that there is no other material information available that would affect its assessment.  What is “key information” is relative to the matter being investigated. An inquiry committee must take steps to obtain sufficient information so that it knows that on the balance of probabilities the key information is in hand and that there is no information that may be key but which has been left out. Without that conscious exercise it becomes speculative as to what information was missed, whether the committee was adequately informed and what would or would not have affected the assessment.

[73]       As set out in The College of Physicians and Surgeons of British Columbia v The Health Professions Review Board, 2018 BCSC 2021, the question for me to consider is "whether the College exercised its investigative discretion reasonably having regard to all the circumstances of the complaint."

[74]       Although the Disposition does not expressly cite “s. 32(3)(b),” the Deputy Registrar’s letter to the Complainant makes clear that he viewed the complaint as being outside the College’s mandate. In his letter to the Registrant and email to the Inquiry Committee, his reliance on s.32(3)(b) of the Act is clear, as he uses its precise language.  In both communications, he stated:

To the Registrant: “I determined that even if admitted or proved, the allegations would not constitute a matter subject to investigation by the College’s Inquiry Committee.”

To the Inquiry Committee: “I have dismissed this under HPA 32(3): the complaint does not contain allegations that, if admitted or proven, would constitute a matter subject to investigation by the Inquiry Committee”.

[75]       If it was reasonable for the Deputy Registrar to dismiss the complaint pursuant to s.32(3)(b) of the Act – if it was reasonable to conclude that the complaint allegations were not within the scope of regulatory concerns of the College - then there was no need for an investigation and the investigation was “adequate.” If this was not a reasonable decision, then there may have been a need for an investigation unless it could be determined on the face of the complaint, without the need for any investigation, that it was trivial, frivolous, vexatious or made in bad faith under s.32(3)(a) of the Act: Complainant v. College of Physicians and Surgeons of British Columbia, 2012 BCHPRB 2 at para. [54]. 

[76]       Therefore, and consistent with the focus of the Application for Review, I will consider the reasonableness of the Disposition before completing my analysis as to the adequacy of the investigation.

B.           Reasonableness of Disposition

[77]       To determine the reasonableness of the disposition, I draw guidance from the decision of the Supreme Court of Canada in Canada (Minister of Citizenship and Immigration) v. Vavilov, 2019 SCC 65 .

[78]       The Deputy Registrar had the responsibility to justify his decision to the parties in a manner that is transparent and intelligible. I must defer to the decision if "it is based on an internally coherent and rational chain of analysis that is justified in relation to the facts and law that constrain the decision maker:" Vavilov paras 85 and 96.

[79]       I must not assess the decision of the Inquiry Committee against a standard of perfection and may only set it aside if I am satisfied "that there are sufficiently serious shortcomings in the decision such that it cannot be said to exhibit the requisite degree of justification, intelligibility and transparency. Any alleged flaws or shortcomings must be more than merely superficial or peripheral to the merits of the decision": Vavilov at paras 91 and 100.

[80]       The use of s.32(3)(b) of the Act was discussed in 2012 BCHPRB 2:

[56]      Subject to any inquiries the Registrar needs to make to clarify allegations in a complaint for this purpose, we agree with Decision No. 2009-HPA-0045(a) (2011 BCHPRB 31) that, based on the wording of s.32(3)(b), a dismissal under this subsection must be based solely on the complaint allegations. Its purpose is to allow the dismissal of complaints that would not be subject to investigation, because they are not within the jurisdiction of the Inquiry Committee to investigate. Section 33(4), in effect, sets forth the matters in respect of which a complaint has to be based in order to be successful.

[81]       The matters set out in s.33(4) of the Act are:

(a)        a contravention of this Act, the regulations or the bylaws;

(a.1)     a conviction for an indictable offence;

(b)        a failure to comply with a standard, limit or condition imposed under this Act;

(c)        professional misconduct or unprofessional conduct;

(d)        competence to practise the designated health profession;

(e)        a physical or mental ailment, an emotional disturbance or an addiction to alcohol or drugs that impairs his or her ability to practise the designated health profession;

[82]       In addition to establishing bylaws, the College has established practice standards by publishing a Handbook under section 19(1)(k), (l) and (z) of the Act and section 82 of its bylaws. The Handbook contains a Code of Ethics.  In his complaint, the Complainant referred to the bylaws and sections of the Handbook and Code of Ethics he believed the Registrant had contravened. 

[83]       The Complainant's list of complaints vary somewhat between his complaint, correspondence with the College and submissions to the Review Board. In its submissions, the College has endeavoured to address all issues raised by the Complainant.

[84]       The College adds that "given the nature of the allegations made in the complaint, the Deputy Registrar might also have dismissed the Complaint under s.32(3)(a) of the HPA on the basis that it was trivial, frivolous, vexatious, or made in bad faith.” However, that was not the stated basis for the Deputy Registrar's Disposition.

[85]       My task is to review the reasonableness of the Disposition based on the complaint actually made and the justification actually given: Vavilov, para. [96]. I cannot consider complaints made for the first time on this review or disregard the flawed basis for a decision and substitute another justification for the outcome that is not explicitly or implicitly reflected in the reasons. The issue is whether the Deputy Registrar reasonably captured the complaint allegations actually made and whether his justification for dismissing them all under s.32(3)(b) of the Act was reasonable.

C.           Business Dispute – Allegations that were reasonably dismissed under s.32(3)(b) of the Act

[86]       As noted, the Deputy Registrar dismissed the complaint pursuant to s.32(3)(b) of the Act giving the following reasons in the Disposition:

There does not appear to be any evidence of patient care issues concerning [the Registrant], only your business relationship with him.

[87]       In its Statement of Points, the College submits that the reasoning in Complainant v. College of Physicians and Surgeons of British Columbia, 2010 BCHPRB 10 and Complainant v. College of Physicians and Surgeons of British Columbia, 2011 BCHPRB 65 is "directly applicable to, and conclusive of, the allegations from the complaint wholly related to the business dispute between the Complainant and the Registrant, including the Complainant's allegations regarding pay deductions, demands for reimbursement, inappropriate communications, accusations of stealing patient information, failure to cooperate in resolving the issues between them, defamation, and making regulatory complaints."

[88]       In my view, this submission relates to some, but not all, of the allegations made by the Complainant.

[89]       In 2010 BCHPRB 10, the Review Board found that a complaint made by one registrant against another, the registrant's former business partner, did not fall within the focus of the complaint process in the Act. The panel held:

[11]      I agree with the College that its regulatory focus (and the purpose of this Review Board) is properly understood in light of the governing statutory direction that the College is to be concerned with the service and protection of the public: Act, s. 16(1)(a).  The Complainant does not in this case allege that any patient or member of the public was harmed, directly or indirectly, by any medical treatment, or by the conduct he alleges.  The statutory direction that the College must exercise its mandate “in the public interest” (Act, s. 16(1)(b)) does not foist upon the College the duty to resolve business disputes between physicians involving claims of “recklessness” in dealings with billing agencies, rudeness as between partners, or “suspicion” involving a stethoscope.

[12]      This is self-evidently a financial dispute between two former business associates who have other remedies available to address any financial losses one seeks to claim against the other.  The courts, not this Review Board, are the proper forum for that sort of litigation.

[90]       In 2011 BCHPRB 65, the Review Board considered a complaint brought by a former employee of a registrant:

[15]      The interaction between the Complainant and Registrant was clearly in connection with an employer/employee relationship.  The Complainant says she was a patient of the Registrant on some occasions but she does not raise any complaints about the professional conduct or competence of the Registrant.  Her complaints all relate to the interactions which followed when her employment was terminated.  The College declined to accept jurisdiction over the complaint taking the position that there were other forums for such disputes which is in accord with other decisions of the Review Board in a generic sense, see Review Board Decision No. 2009-HPA-0057(a) (2010 BCHPRB 10) and Review Board Decision No. 2010-HPA-0073(a) (2010 BCHPRB 25).  I concur.

[91]       Not all issues arising in the context of a “business dispute” fall outside the purview of the college complaint process.  For example, in Complainant v. College of Dental Surgeons of British Columbia, 2011 BCHPRB 18 (which must be read with Complainant v. College of Dental Surgeons of British Columbia, 2011 BCHPRB 8), a complainant alleged professional ethical breaches in the context of a business dispute. The Review Board stated:

[4]        One can contemplate situations where certain financial arrangements by a dentist could constitute professional misconduct or unprofessional conduct.]

[92]       In Complainant v. College of Physicians and Surgeons of British Columbia, 2013 BCHPRB 106, the Review Board considered a complaint involving a longstanding dispute between two physicians who had practiced together and then parted ways acrimoniously. The Complainant alleged the Registrant made false statements and insinuations concerning him to other physicians and hospital administrators in letters. The Review Board disagreed with the Inquiry Committee's finding that the complaint did not engage the jurisdiction of the college because it did not concern the Registrant's clinical care or medical judgment and no patients had been affected. It found that the investigation of the College was inadequate:

[19]      I find that the Inquiry Committee failed to address this complaint as within the College’s statutory mandate and duty to investigate breaches of professional standards pursuant to s. 16(2)(g) of the Act, namely, “to establish, monitor and enforce standards of professional ethics amongst registrants.”

[20]      I further find the Inquiry Committee interpreted and dismissed this complaint as a disagreement between professionals and failed to acknowledge the complaint as an allegation of professional misconduct, and as such, within the jurisdiction of the College. The Inquiry Committee is mandated to investigate a Registrant regarding professional misconduct or unprofessional conduct as set out in s. 33(4)(c) of the Act.

[93]       These decisions reflect that the fundamental issue for college decision-makers in these kinds of cases is whether the particular alleged “private” or “business” misconduct falls within or has a sufficient connection to the matters referenced in s. 33(4) that it should be a regulatory concern of the College.  The College is entitled to deference in determining what constitutes “unprofessional conduct:” Salway v. Association of Professional Engineers and Geoscientists of British Columbia, 2010 BCCA 94.

[94]       I find that it was reasonable for the Deputy Registrar to conclude that the following complaint allegations related to the parties' business dispute and were too remotely connected to the mandate and objects of the College to warrant investigation:

(a)  that there were discrepancies between the written Contract the Complainant signed to work at the Clinic and what he was told by the Registrant when he was interviewed for the position;

(b)  that the Registrant made inappropriate pay deductions;

(c)  that the Registrant fabricated debts that he claimed from the Complainant;

(d)  that the Registrant made inappropriate and\or fraudulent demands for reimbursement from the Complainant; and

(e)  harassment.

[95]       I will explain my decision relating to the allegations of fraudulent demands for reimbursement and harassment in further detail below.

(i)   Fraud

[96]       The Complainant's August 26, 2019 letter to the Deputy Registrar asked for "any interpretation of the issues he raised in his complaint that related to the Registrant's practice including his allegation that the Registrant ‘may be committing fraudulent actions’, which the Complainant had outlined in the original complaint."

[97]       The complaint alleged that the Registrant "failed to honour verbal agreements, changed agreements to the detriment of patients and coworkers for his own benefit, and both fabricated debts (which might qualify as fraud) and withheld pay." The Complainant also alleged that the Registrant made an unjustified demand for payment of rent stating: "this was a dishonest act breaching s.12.1 of the Professional Conduct Handbook as his actions did not demonstrate integrity." He sought to characterize all these allegations as follows:

What I believe [the Registrant] has done here is falsified debts to intimidate and harass me, then he sold or wrote off these debts for financial gain, all while directly making an attempt to harm my personal credit score, which he threatens outright in his letter.

[98]       In his Follow-up Letter, the Deputy Registrar responded that: "Fraud is a criminal act and if you have documented evidence of fraud, you should forward such evidence to the police and the CCBC for a separate investigation."

[99]       The College submits that the alleged fraudulent actions are efforts to collect money and are wholly associated with the parties' business dispute.

[100]    The Complainant has confirmed in his reply that he is not seeking financial compensation for his losses but that his complaint is that the Registrant acted unethically and unprofessionally in "'the fabrication of debts and fraudulent accounting actions taken subsequently in an attempt to write off these false debts as losses, constituting tax fraud." The allegation that the Registrant has engaged in tax fraud is an expansion of the initial complaint, made for the first time on review, so I will not consider it on this review. 

[101]    The Complainant characterized the actions of Registrant as being "possibly fraudulent," that the Registrant "fabricated debts (which might qualify as fraud)" and committed a "dishonest act" by making a false claim for rent. For the purposes of s.32(3)(b) of the Act, it is my view that the Deputy Registrar was to look at the underlying allegations, not the Complainant’s characterization of the legal outcome of those allegations. I agree that the facts underlying these allegations were reasonably seen as relating to the Registrant's attempts to collect money from the Complainant, falling within the parameters of the parties' business dispute that need not be investigated by the College and did not fall within the scope of s.33(4) of the Act. The Deputy Registrar's disposition of this allegation was reasonable.

(ii)  Harassment

[102]    In his letter of August 27, 2019, the Complainant questioned why the Deputy Registrar considered his claims of harassment and defamation to be civil matters only, when the College's Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct Handbook require a certain level of professionalism and ethical uprightness. The College submits that this aspect of the complaint relates solely to the business dispute and no public interest is engaged.

[103]    In my view, the allegations relating to harassment and defamation are distinct.

[104]    The Complainant's allegations of harassment relate to the demands for payment made by the Registrant; the Registrant's responses to the Complainant's requests for his patient's files and the Registrant making regulatory complaints against the Complainant. I will deal with the Complainant's requests for patient files below. The Registrant's complaints to the CMTBC were dealt with by that body. It was reasonable for the College to not make its own decision as to the legitimacy of those complaints.

[105]    While the Complainant has characterized the Registrant's demands for payment and collection efforts as harassment, I find that it was reasonable for the Deputy Registrar to conclude that this alleged conduct occurred in the context of the business relationship between the parties and was too remotely connected to the mandate and objects of the College to warrant investigation under s.33(4) of the Act. The Deputy Registrar's dismissal of this allegation was reasonable.

D.           Business Dispute: Allegations that were not reasonably dismissed under s. 32(3)(b) of the Act

(i)   Allegations underlying the claim of “defamation”

[106]    The Complainant alleged that one of the letters sent to him by the Registrant contained “four pages of defamation,” including "false allegations of insurance fraud, personal insults, false professional misconduct allegations and many mentions of how he had discussed me in a bad light with coworkers of mine, patients, owners of other clinics and even the police. Beyond breaching ethics and professionalism bylaw, this is yet another addition to the wealth of defamation [the Registrant] has committed against me..." [emphasis added]

[107]    In his initial Disposition the Deputy Registrar stated:

In your letter, you mention defamation, fees, charges & payments of money issues with [the Registrant].  These too are civil matters.

[108]    As noted, in his letter of August 27, 2019, the Complainant noted that the College's Code of Ethics and Professional Conduct Handbook requires a certain level of professionalism and ethical uprightness.

[109]    While a conclusion of “defamation” is a civil matter falling outside the College’s mandate, the allegations that the Registrant admitted discussing the Complainant in a bad light with coworkers and patients, and that there is correspondence displaying “unprofessional and confrontational” communications, were not reasonably swept aside as being part of a “business dispute.”

[110]    Without at least looking into the allegations and obtaining the clarification and documents the Complainant was prepared to offer, it was not possible for the Deputy Registrar to make a determination that these allegations fell outside the ethical requirements of the Handbook and s.33(4)(c) of Act. I find that the Deputy Registrar's decision to reject all aspects of these allegations under the general rubric of “a business decision” and a “civil matter” was not reasonable.

(ii)  Allegation of interfering with patient treatment plans

[111]    The Complainant alleges that the Registrant inappropriately removed patient appointments from the Complainant's calendar, interfering with 20-30 treatments. This allegation was not addressed in the Follow-up Letter.

[112]    The College now submits that due to timing, the Complainant would not have been able to treat these patients at the Clinic. The Complainant disputes this.

[113]    There is no explanation of why this aspect of the complaint was dismissed, other than the initial blanket Disposition on the basis that it is related to the business dispute between the parties. While it does relate to the Complainant leaving the Clinic, the allegation is that patients were adversely affected. I find that the Deputy Registrar's dismissal of this aspect of the complaint was not reasonable because it was not justified, logical or transparent.

(iii)         Consent for Records to be shared

[114]    The complaint alleged that the Clinic has two files for each patient, one for massage therapy and one for chiropractic services, that these are “shared” and that the intake form did not provide for a patient to consent to different therapists looking at each other’s charts.

[115]    The Deputy Registrar dealt with this complaint in the Follow-up Letter as follows:

Your contract with [the Registrant] may have clauses or language that affect or limit you in regards to clinical records. The CCBC is unable to enforce or act on a civil contract between two parties.

[116]    The Deputy Registrar made this speculative statement without seeing the relevant contract language or referencing the College’s own standards.

[117]    On this review, the Registrant provided a version of the Contract which includes clauses indicating that the Registrant has sole ownership of documents, including patient records created or produced by the Complainant, and that the Registrant has the sole discretion as to whether to allow the Complainant to make copies.

[118]    The Registrant submits that patients can choose who keeps their confidential records. He states that the majority of patients have indicated that they want their records kept at the Clinic and do not want the Complainant to have possession of their records for any reason and have not authorized the release of files to the Complainant. The Registrant refers to the Clinic consent forms attached to his submissions, which permit sharing of patient files.

[119]    The College submits that Clinic patients are not parties to the Contract, and there is no suggestion that they are bound by it. The College submits that the Complainant is wrong when he says that the sharing of records between a patient’s treating professionals is a violation of the Personal Information Protection Act (“PIPA”), submitting that there is nothing in the College’s legislation or standards that precludes the sharing of records between treating professionals.

[120]    Section 12.4 of the College's Handbook permits a chiropractor to enter into office sharing arrangements with other health care practitioners in accordance with guidelines set out in Appendix “E” to the Handbook. The complaint attaches a copy of section 3 of Appendix "E", "Guidelines for Office Sharing," which provides:

3. All registrants who violate these guidelines do so at their own risk. If a complaint is received about a registrant's office sharing arrangements and if the Board finds that the office sharing arrangements violate these guidelines, then the registrant may be subject to discipline."

[121]    Sections 7 and 9 of Appendix "E" provide:

7. The health practices of the registrant and of the other health care practitioner(s) must be independent of each other. The registrant and the other health practitioner(s) must not be in an employment relationship where one is the employer of the other.

9. Separate and distinct patient records must be maintained by the registrant and the other healthcare practitioner(s).

[122]    These sections appear to at least support an argument, which needs to be addressed directly, that patient consent is required before the “separate and distinct patient records” that must be maintained" can be shared. Instead, the Deputy Registrar was diverted into an irrelevant speculation about the Complainant’s and Registrant’s “civil contract.”

[123]    Even if there is no express written standard dealing with records sharing in a shared office, the Deputy Registrar is still required to assess whether there is an implicit or unwritten standard in this regard. And if, in the end, it is the Deputy Registrar’s view that practitioners in a shared office can share treatment records which must otherwise be kept separate and distinct, or that the specific contract demands a particular result, he needs to address that point directly and justify it in his decision. It is not appropriate for me to make such a finding for the first time on this application for review.

[124]    I find that the reasons given in the Follow-up Letter relating to this issue were unreasonable. 

(iv) Patient File Storage

[125]    The Complainant alleged that the Clinic patient files are kept on open shelves that cannot be locked in a reception area that is often unattended, clearly visible from the street, that the front door of the clinic is often unlocked and sometimes propped open and that he saw a warning sign in the Clinic window with a picture of two trespassers unattended in the Clinic. The complaint attached copies of College Bylaws 72(1) and 73 which set out a Registrant's duty to take reasonable measures to ensure that all patient personal information is safely and securely stored.

[126]    In his Follow-up Letter, the Deputy Registrar referred to the provisions of the Handbook discussing requirements for file security arrangements but rejected this aspect of the complaint as follows:

As noted in our Professional Conduct Handbook, The Personal Information Protection Act ("PIPA") requires chiropractors and chiropractic corporations to protect personal information in their control, such as patient files, "by making reasonable security arrangements to prevent unauthorized access, collection, use, disclosure, copying, modification or disposal".

While PIPA does not identify what "reasonable security arrangements" are, various privacy organizations recommend adopting physical security measures (for example, locked filing cabinets, and alarm systems) technological tools (passwords, encryption, firewalls) and organizational controls (security clearances, restricted access to information, appropriate destruction of outdated information). In addition, it is necessary to ensure staff are aware of the need to protect personal information and trained in the safeguards used for that purpose.

Your concern arises almost 2 years after your works at [the Registrant's] office terminated and is your opinion at this stage. Members of the public have not raised an issue with the CCBC. We have not been made aware of any issues with the OIPC. It appears your concern is speculative at best.

[127]    Counsel for the College echoes these reasons, submitting that "considering its lack of currency, this allegation is speculative at best."

[128]    Although the Registrant was not asked by the College to respond to the complaint, in his submission on this Review, the Registrant described the security system at the Clinic.

[129]    The Deputy Registrar’s justification for dismissing the complaint under s.32(3)(b) of the Act on this issue was unreasonable. The Complainant did not just express his “opinion”, he offered his observations, which the Deputy Registrar was required to accept as being true for the purposes of s.32(3)(b) of the Act. The issue for the Deputy Registrar was whether, if those observations were admitted or proven, this constituted “reasonable security arrangements” in all the circumstances. The Deputy Registrar did not answer that question “yes.” Instead, he focused on the length of time that had passed, and that neither a member of the public or the Office of Information and Privacy Commissioner (OIPC) had raised any issues with the College.

[130]    The length of time that passed since the complaint was made is not relevant to the jurisdiction of the College under s.32(3)(b) of the Act. Nor is it relevant for jurisdictional purposes that the complaint was made by the registrant of another College. There is no requirement in s.32(1) of the Act that only “a member of the public” may make a complaint; there is nothing in ss.32.2, 32.3 and 32.4 that would exclude such a complaint. To do so would undermine the public interest, as someone who works in the office will in many cases have unique knowledge of regulatory concerns and the relevant professional standards. Therefore, the Deputy Registrar’s decision on this issue was unreasonable, because it was based on irrelevant considerations and it failed to address the key issue: whether the allegations, if admitted or proven, would be subject to investigation.

(v)  Withholding Patient Records from the Complainant

[131]    The Complainant alleged that the Registrant was withholding patient records when the Complainant was legally entitled to them and required to obtain them. In his Follow-up Letter, the Deputy Registrar rejected this aspect of the complaint on the following basis:

You are the party you refer to, and this is in relation to your contract. Again, this is a civil matter, not a regulatory matter.

[132]    The Deputy Registrar dealt with the related complaint that the Registrant was preventing the Complainant from complying with his own professional obligations by withholding his patients' files as follows:

It is not within the CCBC's mandate to ensure you are in compliance with your College's regulations, with or without a contract in place.

[133]    The College submits that the College might have a role to play if the Registrant was blocking patient access to massage records but submits that the arrangements between the Complainant and Registrant concerning patient records are part of their business dispute.

[134]    The Follow-up Letter and College submissions do not address Appendix "E" section 9 of its Professional Conduct Handbook, set out above, requiring registrants to maintain separate files from other healthcare practitioners.

[135]    One of the objects of the College set out in s.16(2)(k) of the Act is to promote or enhance "(ii) interprofessional collaborative practice between its registrants and persons practising another health profession."

[136]    I find that the Deputy Registrar’s justification for dismissing this complaint was unreasonable. While the Deputy Registrar was reasonable in noting that the Complainant was the one claiming an “entitlement” to the treatment records, he unreasonably characterized this as being solely a civil rather than a regulatory matter.

[137]    The Complainant was seeking the records as a regulatory requirement of his regulatory college. The Deputy Registrar stated: “It is not within CCBC’s mandate to ensure you are in compliance with your College’s regulations, with or without a contract in place.”

[138]    The proposition that this College has no mandate to assess whether a registrant has shown any consideration for the ethical obligations of a registrant of another college working in the same office is unreasonable because it runs counter to the fact that the College has itself addressed the subject matter of “office sharing” in its Professional Conduct Handbook. It also unreasonably ignores the legislature’s direction that the statutory objects of all the colleges include to “promote and enhance…interprofessional collaborative practice between its registrants and persons practising another health profession: Act, s.16(2)(k). It is unreasonable to conclude that the allegation here is merely a “civil matter” and is a subject that cannot be investigated under the Act.

[139]    It is possible that the Deputy Registrar’s reference to the “contract” was meant to suggest that any professional obligation of the Registrant to take into account the Complainant’s duties as a massage therapist might have been modified by their agreement. However, as noted, the Deputy Registrar did not have a copy of the contract so his statement regarding the Contract is speculative and an unreasonable basis for his decision.

[140]    I am not purporting to answer the question whether this allegation was valid. The limited point I make is that it was unreasonable for the Registrar to simply dismiss this allegation without investigation or assessment on its merits.

[141]    I find that the decision of the Deputy Registrar that the issue of retention of patient records was not within the mandate of the College to investigate was unreasonable because the Deputy Registrar did not recognize that it fell within one of the objects of the College as set out above and was regulated by its Handbook but rather based his decision on speculation as to the contents of the Contract.

E.           Conclusion on Reasonableness of the Disposition

[142]     I find that the Disposition was not reasonable with respect to the following allegations in the complaint because the Deputy Registrar failed to recognize, investigate and consider on their merits, the aspects of the complaint which engaged the College's mandate to regulate its profession, namely:

(a)  allegations of unprofessional conduct summarized at para.[106];

(b)  interfering with patient treatment plans;

(c)  consent for records to be shared;

(d)  patient file storage; and

(e)  withholding patient records from complainant;

F.            Conclusion on Adequacy of Investigation

[143]    It is common ground that the Deputy Registrar did not investigate any allegation made in the complaint.

[144]    To the extent that the Deputy Registrar reasonably dismissed several of the complaint allegations (as I have found above), no investigation was necessary.

[145]    To the extent that the Deputy Registrar unreasonably dismissed several of the complaint allegations (as I have also found above), the absence of any investigation whatsoever rendered the investigation inadequate.

[146]    I will not purport to dictate to the Deputy Registrar what investigative steps he or the Inquiry Committee ought to take to investigate the complaint. With the benefit of this decision, they can make those decisions, though I would expect that they would at least review the “new evidence” tendered on this review.

X             COLLEGE PROCESS

[147]    As noted above, the Deputy Registrar issued the Disposition without direction from the Inquiry Committee and prior to advising the Inquiry Committee of the complaint and his proposed disposition.

[148]    There is a question as to whether a Registrar’s failure to give the Inquiry Committee the opportunity to direct an investigation based on the Complainant’s letter of August 26, 2019, renders the Registrar’s disposition unreasonable - as being a failure to complete the statutory process before the Disposition was “issued.” I need not answer that question in this case, as my remittal in this case will allow that defect to be remedied regardless of whether the reconsidered disposition is made by the Registrar under s.32 of the Act, or by the Inquiry Committee under s.33 of the Act.

[149]    There is also a question as to whether the Deputy Registrar unreasonably interpreted the test in s. 32(3)(c) when his August 26, 2019 letter to Complainant stated that “I have determined that the allegations do not warrant disciplinary action.” This is a different and more stringent test than that set out in s.32(3)(b) of the Act.

[150]    While the Deputy Registrar's misstating of the test he was required to apply does not affect my decision in this case, I bring this to matter to the attention of the College so that in the future its letters and deliberations reflect the mandate it has been given.

XI           ORDER

[151]    Pursuant to s.50.6(8)(c) of the Act, I order that the complaint be sent back to the Inquiry Committee with the following directions:

(a)  The Inquiry Committee is to investigate the complaint with respect to the allegations of:

(i)    allegations of unprofessional conduct summarized at paragraph [106];

(ii)  interfering with patient treatment plans;

(iii) consent for records to be shared;

(iv) patient file storage; and

(v)  withholding patient records from the Complainant;

(b)  In their investigations, the Inquiry Committee may consider any of the information the Complainant or Registrant tendered as new evidence before the Review Board;

(c)  The Inquiry Committee is to issue a disposition that explains its outcome with reference to the relevant standards of practice on each issue raised by the complaint set out above.
