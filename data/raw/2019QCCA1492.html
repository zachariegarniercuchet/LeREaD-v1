1.   PRELIMINARY QUESTIONS

[4]           On April 26, 2018, the chief justices of the Superior Court made a written application to dismiss Parts 1 and 2 of the expert report of Donald Fyson.[6] They argued that these two parts of the report are primarily an exercise in interpreting legislation in force from 1846 to 1867, an exercise that falls within the expertise of the Court, not that of the report’s author. They also asked that Part 3 of the report be considered mere documentary evidence, because it does not contain an expert opinion.

[5]           Although no representations on this matter were made at the hearing, the Court must first rule on the Demande des juges en chef de la Cour supérieure en irrecevabilité du document Fyson à titre d’expertise [Application of the chief justices of the Superior Court to dismiss the Fyson document as an expert report], which was referred to it by the Chief Justice.

[6]           The application is dismissed for the following reasons.

[7]           Donald Fyson, a historian and full professor in the Department of History at the Université Laval, is a specialist in the history of 18th, 19th and 20th century Quebec, particularly its social, sociolegal and sociopolitical aspects.

[8]           His report, dated April 10, 2018, was filed by the Attorney General of Quebec. It is entitled Civil Justice in Mid-Nineteen-Century British North America: Court Structure, Judges and Recourse to the Courts in Lower Canada, Upper Canada, New Brunswick and Nova Scotia and is divided into three parts.

[9]           Part 1 describes the various courts of original civil jurisdiction in pre-Confederation times. It covers the provinces of Lower Canada, Upper Canada, New Brunswick and Nova Scotia and deals with the composition of those courts and the scope of their respective jurisdiction. It is based, notably, on an analysis of the statutes then in force and on certain scholarly publications.

[10]        In Part 2, Professor Fyson describes the process by which judges and magistrates were appointed to those courts, based, once again, on pre-Confederation statutes and on scholarly articles.

[11]        Lastly, in Part 3, he presents the vast amount of statistical and factual data he compiled, on the basis of which he estimates the number of civil suits launched in each court, the amount at stake in those suits, the percentage of the population involved and the number of judgments rendered by each court.

[12]        In essence, as indicated in paragraph [4] hereinabove, the chief justices submit that the first two parts of the report are inadmissible because the expert interprets and opines on the jurisdiction of the courts of justice in 1867, a task they argue is reserved to the Court in connection with this reference. They also ask that the third part be considered to be mere documentary, rather than expert, evidence, arguing that the author of the report only reproduces data he has compiled, but does not express an opinion.

***

[13]        It is true that the task of interpreting the law usually belongs, not to experts, but to judges, who have judicial notice of the law.[7] There are, however, exceptions to this rule, both as regards the law currently in force and as it existed in the past.[8]

[14]        Thus, the courts will allow expert evidence if it contains a complex contextual discussion of the former law,[9] examines legislative facts on reasonably debatable points, particularly when those points deal with a matter that could be decisive,[10] or establishes the social and historical context of the issue under consideration.[11]

[15]        Statutes are sources of historical knowledge and historians often use them to establish and understand certain facts. Indeed, it would be difficult to draw a picture of the public institutions that existed at a given time without referring to the legislation that created them.

[16]        This is likely why Chief Justice Dickson used the expression “historical inquiry” to refer to the first step of the three-step process that must generally be followed when applying section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867, which suggests that certain findings to be made at that stage are factual and historical.[12]

[17]        That being said, recently, in R. v. Comeau,[13] the Supreme Court pointed out that these exceptions must not result in judges abdicating their role in favour of expert opinions.

[18]        How does the foregoing apply to Professor Fyson’s report?

[19]        As mentioned above, Parts 1 and 2 of the report identify the courts of civil jurisdiction prior to 1867 and the manner in which judges and magistrates of those courts were appointed. Professor Fyson consulted and analyzed numerous statutes in order to draw a comprehensive picture of the justice system at that time. He did not express a legal opinion per se or analyze the impact of his findings on the legality of the current system. His discussion was limited to the situation that existed at the time. Although his analysis was based, among other things, on the statutes then in force, his report was essentially intended to establish historical facts.

[20]        It can easily be seen that he did not in any manner usurp the role of the Court, but merely stated historical facts without drawing any legal conclusions therefrom.

[21]        The chief justices fault him for having consulted factums filed in the Supreme Court in cases dealing with the scope of s. 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867 and contend that this shows that his report borders on a legal opinion.

[22]        This Court does not share this view.

[23]        Professor Fyson explained that he consulted these factums to ensure he had not missed any major elements, while recognizing that these are “evidently neither primary sources (for the state of the courts in the mid-nineteenth century) nor scholarly publications subject to normal peer review, and thus must be treated with caution”.[14] He therefore consulted these factums in an effort to be thorough, so as to substantiate the exhaustive nature of his approach and its outcome.

[24]        Under these circumstances, although Professor Fyson’s report relies on statutes, articles and other scholarly works and, incidentally, on factums filed in the Supreme Court of Canada, the Court finds that it is admissible because it establishes legislative facts and paints a picture of the prevailing situation in pre-Confederation times, without encroaching on the role of the Court.

***

[25]        The chief justices of the Superior Court also asked the Court to order the Minister of Justice of Quebec and the Minister of Justice of Canada to pay them a “provision for costs” intended to pay the extrajudicial fees and the disbursements they incurred to participate in the reference. They agreed, however, that this application would not be dealt with at the hearing on the reference itself. Although the Court has now rendered its opinion on the reference questions, it is still seized of the chief justices’ application, which application will be referred to another panel of the Court for hearing and adjudication.

2.   CONTEXT

[26]        Before delving into the analysis of the two questions before the Court, it is useful first to examine the context, that is, the origin and purpose of the provisions of the Constitution Act, 1867 dealing with the courts and the organization of justice, the circumstances under which the first and second reference questions were brought before the Court and, briefly, the current functioning of the Superior Court and the Court of Québec.

2.1. The origin and purpose of the relevant constitutional provisions

[27]        As stated in the preamble to the Constitution Act, 1867, the purpose pursued at Confederation was to establish a constitution for Canada similar in principle to that of the United Kingdom.[15] The essentially unitary nature of the United Kingdom’s constitution, however, presented certain challenges when developing a constitution for a federal state. These challenges are reflected in several aspects of the Constitution Act, 1867, the division of powers between the central government and the provinces being the most striking example. Shaping Canada’s constitution based on that of the United Kingdom was just as delicate a task when addressing the organization and administration of the various judicial systems of the pre-Confederation provinces, which also originated from the English system or were informed by it.

[28]        The English justice system has long been based on a dichotomy between the royally appointed courts, referred to as the “superior courts”, and the inferior courts, referred to as the “subordinate magistrates”. As Blackstone stated as early as 1765, the English superior courts had a guaranteed general jurisdiction and their judges had the necessary privileges to ensure their impartiality, including an immutable appointment and guaranteed salary:

[…] But at present, by the long and uniform usage of many ages, our kings have delegated their whole judicial power to the judges of their several courts; which are the grand depositary of the fundamental laws of the kingdom, and have gained a known and stated jurisdiction, regulated by certain and established rules, which the crown itself cannot now alter but by act of parliament. […] And now, by the noble improvement of that law […] the judges are continued in their offices during their good behaviour […] and their full salaries are absolutely secured to them during the continuance of their commissions; his Majesty having been pleased to declare, that “he looked upon the independence and uprightness of the judges, as essential to the impartial administration of justice; as one of the best securities of the rights and liberties of his subjects; and as most conducive to the honour of the crown.” [16] 

[29]        By contrast with the royally appointed “superior” courts of justice, there were and continue to be so-called “subordinate” or “inferior” courts in England.[17] At the end of the 18th century, Blackstone listed a number of them, the principal ones being the sheriffs, coroners, justices of the peace and overseers of the poor.[18] These officers exercised functions we would refer to today as ministerial or administrative, while playing a role in local criminal and civil justice. For purposes of the reference, the Court need not deal with their jurisdiction over criminal matters.[19] In civil matters, the sheriffs, within their respective counties, decided, notably, matters of 40 shillings or less, while the coroners had jurisdiction over matters involving shipwrecks and treasure-troves, justices of the peace handled certain civil cases based on the commissions under which they had been appointed and overseers of the poor were charged with ensuring the well-being of the indigent and making the necessary decisions in that regard, including those relating to their rights of settlement.[20]

[30]        All inferior courts in England were, since at least the 18th century, subject to the supervision of the royal superior courts, primarily by means of the writ of certiorari:

[…] The broad notion was that the king’s courts should keep all lesser authorities within the procedural and jurisdictional bounds set by the law, and provide the subject with a remedy if tribunals or officials exceeded their legal authority or gave orders which were patently contrary to law. […] [By the] eighteenth century, […] the proper course was to state cases formally for the King’s Bench.

[…] By the end of the seventeenth century the King’s Bench had a flourishing jurisdiction as a court of review for both summary convictions and orders of quarter sessions relating to such matters as public works, licensing, and the settlement of the poor. The essential features of the jurisdiction were settled by Holt CJ: certiorari would lie to any body created by statute which acted judicially, even if it was not a court of common law […][21]

[31]        In Les tribunaux administratifs et la Constitution, Professor Gilles Pépin cites Halsbury in order to specify the fundamental difference between a superior court and an inferior court:

[translation]

The courts of justice in each Canadian province can be grouped into two classes, in accordance with the Anglo-Saxon tradition which has had a profound impact on them: the superior courts and the inferior courts. This classification should not be confused with the more universal classification that distinguishes trial courts from appellate courts. In differentiating between superior courts and inferior courts, the jurisprudence usually refers to Halsbury:

818. The origin of the inferior courts, of which there are many varieties, may be traced back to the principle that justice should be taken to every man’s door by constituting as many courts as there were manors in the Kingdom…

They derived their general title of inferior courts because they were and are, in the great majority of cases, subject to the control and supervision of the Court of King’s Bench or King’s Bench Division as a superior court. A part of the original inherent jurisdiction of the Court of King’s Bench was to examine and correct all errors committed by the inferior courts, whether in matter of law or in exceeding the jurisdiction that had been conferred upon them.

819. The jurisdiction of an inferior court is defined by its constitution… Such jurisdiction is generally limited to matters between residents in a certain locality, or to causes of action arising within prescribed metes and bounds, or to actions where the amount claimed is under a certain specified limit.

820. It is in connection with jurisdiction that we find the chief distinctions between superior and inferior courts. The jurisdiction of the superior courts… extends in civil cases over the whole of England and Wales… Prima facie, no matter is deemed to be beyond the jurisdiction of a superior court unless it is expressly shown to be so, while nothing is within the jurisdiction of an inferior court unless it is expressly shown on the face of the proceedings that the particular matter is within the cognizance of the particular court. An objection to the jurisdiction of one of the superior courts of general jurisdiction must show what other court has jurisdiction, so as to make it clear jurisdiction is unnecessary. [22] 

[32]        This fundamental dichotomy between the superior and inferior courts was still characteristic of the justice system in England during the 19th century, including when the Constitution Act, 1867 was enacted. At that time, the principal debate regarding judicial administration in England was focused on the unification of the various superior courts, resulting in the 1873 Judicature Act, which, notably, created a High Court of Justice that was invested with the jurisdiction of various courts which the statute abolished.[23]

[33]        This same dichotomy existed in the judicial organization of the territories that were to become Canada.[24] Although judicial administration in each pre-Confederation province reflects the history of that province, a common thread emerges: the existence of superior courts tasked with applying the law and overseeing the inferior courts that had varying mandates and dispensed criminal and civil justice in a summary manner or according to equity.

[34]        Professor Fyson’s description, in his expert report, of pre-Confederation judicial administration in the four founding Canadian provinces confirms this duality. He specifies that it was, in fact, a “three-tiered system” consisting of a superior court, intermediate courts and inferior courts:

1.1 LOWER CANADA

After having been established in 1764 as a simplified version of the court system of England, the court structure of Quebec and then of Lower Canada underwent a number of fairly dramatic changes over the decades that followed, with a final major reorganization in 1849. This established a three-tiered system of courts with general civil jurisdiction: a Superior Court for the most important civil cases (such as those involving large sums of money, or title to land), a Circuit Court for intermediate and smaller cases, and Commissioners’ Courts for cases involving smaller amounts. While the overall court structure did not change much thereafter until after Confederation, a major territorial reorganization was undertaken in 1857-1858, when the colony’s seven existing judicial districts were replaced by 20 districts. The new or newly-defined districts were mostly made up of one, two or three of the counties into which the colony was already divided for the purposes of political representation. Each new district received a full complement of the usual courts of civil jurisdiction: a Superior Court, a Circuit Court or Courts, and a Commissioners’ Court. Each district also had a considerable number of justices of the peace. There were also specialized courts in the colony with focused civil jurisdiction: Recorder’s Courts and Police Courts in Montreal and Quebec City, Trinity Houses in both cities as well, and a Court of Vice-Admiralty in Quebec City.[25]

1.2. UPPER CANADA

The court system of Upper Canada in the 1850s and 1860s was the product of multiple and progressive reforms that had been undertaken since the establishment of the colony in 1791, culminating in major reforms in the late 1830s and early 1840s and again in 1849. The system established from that point onwards, and which remained in place until 1867 and beyond, was three-tiered. There was a pair of superior law courts, the Court of Queen’s Bench and the Court of Common Pleas, with original jurisdiction over all civil matters; there were intermediate County and District Courts, with monetarily-limited jurisdiction; and there were local Division Courts, with even more limited jurisdiction. There were also specialized courts: by the 1860s, the Court of Chancery and the Surrogate Courts. Upper-Canadian justices of the peace and municipal courts did have some civil jurisdiction, but less than in the other three colonies.[26]

1.3 NEW BRUNSWICK

Until the very end of the period under examination here, New Brunswick maintained a three-tiered court system, with a Supreme Court with unlimited original civil jurisdiction, Inferior Courts of Common Pleas with intermediate jurisdiction, and justices of the peace and municipal courts in Saint-John and in Portland with jurisdiction over smaller civil claims. Up to 1867, the only notable change came in 1854, with the abolition of the Court of Chancery. The colony also had specialized courts for particular types of cases, notably Probate Courts, a Court of Divorce and Matrimonial Causes, and a Court of Vice-Admiralty. […][27]

1.4 NOVA SCOTIA

Up until 1841, Nova Scotia had a three-tiered system of common law courts with original jurisdiction in common law civil cases, much like New Brunswick’s. There was a Supreme Court based in Halifax hearing the most important cases, which also went on circuit into the counties; Inferior Courts of Common Pleas established in each of the counties; and justices of the peace throughout the colony with summary civil jurisdiction. In 1841, the Inferior Courts of Common Pleas were abolished and their business taken over by the Supreme Court in circuit and the JPs, giving the colony a two-tiered court system. In 1841, the colony also had a range of specialized courts: a Court of Chancery for equity jurisdiction, a Court of Marriage and Divorce for divorce hearings, Probate Courts in each county for matters relating to estates, and a Court of Vice-Admiralty with jurisdiction over Admiralty cases. There was also a Mayor’s Court or City Court in Halifax with summary civil jurisdiction. The court system as it stood after 1841 remained largely in place through to 1867, with the exception of the abolition of the Court of Chancery in 1855 and the transfer of its equity jurisdiction to the Supreme Court. [35]        It is therefore with reference to this historical and factual context that we must examine the provisions of the Constitution Act, 1867 dealing with the organization of the judicial system in Canada, which – as stated above – seeks to reflect the United Kingdom’s judicial system.[29]

[36]        The organization of the courts is one of the greatest constitutional challenges for any federal state.[30] A number of approaches may be considered, including a purely central or federal model, a purely local or provincial model or a model in which each government, central and local, creates its own courts to interpret and apply its own laws, this last model being the American model and the one that was best known at the time of the constitutional discussions that led to the enactment of the Constitution Act, 1867. According to this model, the federal state and each constituent state administers its own separate judicial system tasked with applying either the federal law or state law, depending on the nature of the dispute. It is precisely this American model that the Canadian drafters rejected.[31]

[37]        Instead, they opted for a unique hybrid model, inspired by pragmatic considerations: each province administers justice within its borders, but the central government appoints all the judges of the superior courts and intermediate courts,[32] which includes all the judges who, according to the English model, state the law, develop the common law, and control and supervise the inferior courts. By having both the central government and the governments of the provinces participate in organizing the superior courts (including the intermediate courts), the Canadian drafters avoided copying the American system, which is characterized by federal courts and state courts that are independent of one another. The Canadian approach was closer to the English unitary judicial system, which was marked by the dichotomy between the superior courts and the inferior courts, and was a better reflection of the existing judicial organization in each province at the time of Confederation. The central government could therefore entrust the interpretation and application of its own laws to so-called “provincial” courts, because decisions would be made by federally appointed judges in each province.

[38]        As Peter H. Russell noted:

Comparatively speaking, the Canadian judicial system ranks as one of the most integrated, or least federalized. The judicial provisions of the Canadian Constitution lean strongly in the direction of the judicial system of a unitary state. Unlike the American Constitution, which refers to the “judicial Power of the United States” and vests that jurisdiction in the Supreme Court of the United States and such federal courts as Congress may establish, and the Australian Constitution, which similarly vests “the judicial power of the Commonwealth’ in the High Court of Australia and such federal courts as the national Parliament creates,” the Canadian Constitution does not refer to a federal judicial power. The only judicial arrangements specifically provided for in the Constitution are the very essence of an integrated federal-provincial system: federally appointed judges of provincial superior and intermediate courts. No other federation has this element of judicial integration. [33]

[Emphasis added]

[39]        Unlike the distinction it establishes for institutions within the legislative and executive branches in Canada, the Constitution Act, 1867 does not create a clear distinction between institutions within the judicial branch; it does not establish federal judicial institutions exercising their jurisdiction throughout Canada within the federal sphere and provincial judicial institutions exercising their jurisdiction in each province within the provincial sphere.[34] Indeed, the rejection of the American model of administration of justice is a hallmark of the Canadian constitution[35].

[40]        As a result, it is up to the superior courts (which are administered by the provinces, but whose members are appointed by the central government) to interpret and apply all of the rules of law and all legislation, whether federal or provincial, within each province. Indeed, Resolution 32 of the October 1864 Quebec Conference provided that all courts and judges of the various provinces were required to assist the central government in the exercise of its powers and, as such, would be considered to be courts and judges of the central government.[36] As for Resolution 33, it stated that the central government would appoint and pay the judges of the superior courts in each province and of the county courts in Ontario.[37] These resolutions gave rise to s. 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867 (as well as s. 100).

[41]        The courts quickly decided that Parliament need not establish new courts to apply federal legislation, because it is entitled to entrust such duties to the “provincial” superior courts, whose judges are federally appointed, without trenching on the provincial jurisdiction over the administration of justice. Ritchie, J. confirmed this arrangement in the early days of Confederation in the 1879 judgment in Valin v. Langlois:

[…] while they have clearly the power of establishing a new Dominion Court, they have likewise the power, when legislating within their jurisdiction, to require the established courts of the respective Provinces, and the judges thereof, who are appointed by the Dominion, paid out of the treasury of the Dominion, and removable only by address of the House of Commons and Senate of the Parliament of the Dominion, to enforce their legislation. [38] 

[42]        As a result of their jurisdiction over all legislation, whether federal or provincial, the judges of the superior courts play a unifying role in Canada.[39] The provisions of the Constitution Act, 1867, however, go beyond merely permitting the superior courts to interpret and apply federal legislation. They also reflect the notions of independence and impartiality of the superior courts which originate from the United Kingdom’s unwritten constitution, and incorporate them into the Canadian constitution as immutable principles. In doing so, they also ensure that superior courts in each province will always enjoy this guarantee of independence and impartiality, without being subject to the political whims of the governments of the day, be they federal or provincial, as the Supreme Court pointed out in McEvoy:

The traditional independence of English Superior Court judges has been raised to the level of a fundamental principle of our federal system by the Constitution Act, 1867 and cannot have less importance and force in the administration of criminal law than in the case of civil matters. Under the Canadian constitution the Superior Courts are independent of both levels of government. […][40]

[43]        Author Luc Huppé explains that this is a fundamental aspect of the Canadian constitution:

[translation]

[…] From then on, one source – the country’s Constitution rather than the sparse provisions of colonial legislation – gave them this [immutable] status, which does not depend on the favour of any of the public bodies created by the Constitution Act, 1867. Compared with their English counterparts under the Act of Settlement (1700), as of that moment Canadian judges had an enviable status. Even more than the parliamentarians and members of the various governments, Canada’s superior court judges found, in the provisions of the Constitutional Act, 1867, the foundations for their cohesion, which the fragmentation of the Canadian territory into separate colonies did not yet permit.[41]

[44]        Federally appointed judges therefore have a particular status within the Canadian constitutional order, just like their counterparts in English superior courts. Their core jurisdiction and the crucial role they play in the justice system within each province are constitutionally guaranteed, as Lamer, C.J. stated in MacMillan Bloedel:

[…] The superior courts have a core or inherent jurisdiction which is integral to their operations. The jurisdiction which forms this core cannot be removed from the superior courts by either level of government, without amending the Constitution. Without this core jurisdiction, s. 96 could not be said either to ensure uniformity in the judicial system throughout the country or to protect the independence of the judiciary. […][42]

[45]        This core jurisdiction extends particularly and minimally to the following elements:

(1)        the preservation of the rule of law;[43]

(2)        the exercise of a superintending and reforming power over the provincial courts of inferior jurisdiction and provincial public bodies;[44]

(3)        the scrutiny of the constitutionality of federal and provincial legislation;[45]

(4)        the resolution of disputes on issues of private and public law;[46] and

(5)        the oversight of its own procedure, notably through the use of means to ensure a fair trial, prevent abuse of process and secure compliance with its orders.[47]

[46]        It goes without saying that, under the Constitution, it is not permissible to abolish the superior courts or remove their core powers.[48]

[47]        There is also a prohibition against creating courts with provincially appointed judges that exercise, in whole or in part, the jurisdiction of the superior courts as “shadows” or “mirrors” thereof.[49] In this regard, in civil matters, the Supreme Court of Canada generally applies a three-part test:[50]

1.  Does the power or jurisdiction broadly conform to the power or jurisdiction exercised by superior, district or county courts at the time of Confederation?

2. Is the function of the provincial tribunal within its institutional setting a judicial function, considered from the point of view of the nature of the question which the tribunal is called upon to decide or, to put it in other words, is the tribunal concerned with a private dispute which it is called upon to adjudicate through the application of a recognized body of rules and in a manner consistent with fairness and impartiality?

3. If the power or jurisdiction of the provincial tribunal is exercised in a judicial manner, does its function as a whole in its entire institutional context violate s. 96?

2.2. Context of the first reference question and circumstances under which the Court was seized thereof

[48]        The first reference question fits within a specific context and highlights the core jurisdiction of the superior courts over the resolution of judicial disputes in matters of private law. As McLachlin, C.J. pointed out in Trial Lawyers Association:

[32]      The historic task of the superior courts is to resolve disputes between individuals and decide questions of private and public law. Measures that prevent people from coming to the courts to have those issues resolved are at odds with this basic judicial function. The resolution of these disputes and resulting determination of issues of private and public law, viewed in the institutional context of the Canadian justice system, are central to what the superior courts do. Indeed, it is their very book of business. To prevent this business being done strikes at the core of the jurisdiction of the superior courts protected by s. 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867. […][51]

[Emphasis added]

[49]        Both before and after Confederation, the superior courts acted, throughout Canada, as courts of original general jurisdiction in civil matters. In this regard, Quebec is no different: the Superior Court was expressly given this role as of 1849 by a statute providing for the replacement of the Court of Queen’s Bench with a “Superior Court”.[52] As of then, the Superior Court exercised general civil jurisdiction across Lower Canada[53] (subject to matters falling under the admiralty jurisdiction or the jurisdiction of the Circuit Court), as well as a superintending and reforming power over public bodies and inferior courts.[54]

[50]        It is also important to note the existence, prior to Confederation, of the Circuit Court (an “intermediate” court), which determined all matters of lesser importance in a summary manner (fifty pounds or less) or according to equity (six pounds five shillings or less).[55]

[51]        In contrast, the pre-Confederation inferior courts in Quebec did not have original civil jurisdiction; their jurisdiction was limited by the amount and location in question and, moreover, by the type of case.

[52]        In 1866, the Code of Civil Procedure of Lower Canada[56] adopted this judicial structure. This is an important legislative text, because it was enacted contemporaneously with the Constitution Act, 1867 and in anticipation thereof. It is therefore a reflection of the political vision of the post-Confederation judicial structure.

[53]        This code confirmed the Superior Court’s status as the court of original civil jurisdiction as well as its superintending and reforming role.[57]

[54]        It also conferred exclusive jurisdiction on the Circuit Court – the intermediate court – over cases where the value in dispute was less than $100, which cases were decided in a summary manner, and over those for $100 and more, but not more than $200, which could not be appealed.[58] Under the Code, the Circuit Court also had a superintending and reforming power, concurrently with the Superior Court, over certain inferior courts.[59] It is worth remembering that the Circuit Court is a court included in the list set out in section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867 and is considered a superior court.[60]

[55]        By contrast, under the 1866 Code of Civil Procedure of Lower Canada, the inferior courts, namely, the commissioners’ courts, the justices of the peace and the recorders’ courts, did not have general civil jurisdiction, even less so over the entire territory of the province. They were not courts of original general jurisdiction, because their civil jurisdiction was subject to a geographic and monetary limit and a limit on the subject matters they could adjudicate; moreover, their decisions were essentially rendered in a summary manner or in equity, and they were subject to the superintending power of the superior courts.[61]

[56]        The history of the so-called “judicial” courts with provincially appointed judges in Quebec was described by this Court in 1964, in Renvoi concernant la constitutionnalité de la Cour de magistrat,[62] particularly in the reasons of Choquette, J.A. It is not necessary to repeat the lengthy analysis in that judgment, but a summary of its main points is useful.

[57]        In 1869, the province created the magistrate’s courts, whose judges were provincially appointed, and conferred upon them the criminal and penal jurisdiction of the judges of the sessions of the peace, the recorders, the stipendiary magistrates and any two justices of the peace.[63] This statute also allowed the provincial government to confer upon a magistrate’s court, in place of the former commissioners’ court, the jurisdiction to hear and determine civil suits whose value did not exceed $25,[64] which jurisdiction was increased to $50 in 1871.[65] This was essentially a reorganization of the civil jurisdiction of the province’s inferior courts.

[58]        In 1888, however, the province attempted to abolish the Circuit Court for the district of Montreal and replace it with a magistrate’s court.[66] The federal government disallowed this statute on the ground that it violated section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867.[67] A statute enacted in 1889[68] to establish a magistrate’s court in the district of Montreal, without abolishing the Circuit Court, was also disallowed.[69] This crisis was resolved in 1893 by the creation of a special Circuit Court of the District of Montreal, presided by federally appointed judges other than judges of the Superior Court.[70]

[59]        Be that as it may, the civil jurisdiction of the magistrate’s courts very gradually increased, reaching nearly $100 in 1921.[71] As of 1922, the civil jurisdiction of the Circuit Court was transferred to a magistrate’s court in the districts in which such a court existed.[72] The civil jurisdiction of the magistrate’s courts increased to $200 in 1946.[73] In 1953, the province abolished the Circuit Court, without a court challenge or disallowance,[74] and conferred exclusive jurisdiction upon the Magistrate’s Court to hear and determine civil disputes where the value in dispute was less than $200.[75]

[60]        In 1963, the province enacted a statute to increase the civil jurisdiction of the Magistrate’s Court to $500.[76] Given the uncertainty regarding the constitutionality of the broadened jurisdiction, the Government of Quebec proceeded by way of reference to this Court, which unanimously found the statute to be unconstitutional.[77] It was of the opinion that the Magistrate’s Court was the equivalent of the Circuit Court and that its judges could not act unless appointed by the federal government.

[61]        The matter went to the Supreme Court of Canada. While Fauteux, C.J. did not disavow the Court’s conclusions, he found that it had not answered the question it had been asked:

[translation]

I would therefore say, with the greatest respect for the Court of Appeal, that, in my view, the text of the preamble on which the order in council is based, as well as the text of the question defined in the order show that the opinion the executive council seeks concerns the statute specifically mentioned therein, and no other statute, and has no purpose other than to know whether, given its particular object – which is to change the monetary limit – the statute is unconstitutional so long as the Judges of the Court contemplated in the statute are not appointed in accordance with s. 96 of the British North America Act. […]

[…]

In the present case, to which the scope of my opinion is limited, the broadening of the jurisdiction of the Magistrate’s Court, by increasing the number of dollars, viewed in light of the current value of the dollar, does not, in and of itself, have the effect, when added to the jurisdiction that is peculiar to it as an Inferior Court not contemplated in s. 96, of making it a s. 96 Court. It follows that the statute under consideration is not unconstitutional. From this finding, one must not infer that I consider the various provincial statutes that extend the jurisdiction of the Magistrate’s Court – other than through a monetary increase – to be constitutionally valid, which statutes the Court of Appeal considered. As for the opinions expressed by the Court of Appeal on this matter, I neither dissent nor concur. [78]

[Underlining and bold added]

[62]        Shortly thereafter, the Magistrate’s Court was renamed the “Provincial Court”.[79] In 1966, its civil jurisdiction was increased to $1,000 with the enactment of the new Code of Civil Procedure.[80] In 1969, it increased to $3,000,[81] and then to $6,000 in 1979,[82] to $10,000 in 1982[83] and to $15,000 in 1984.[84]

[63]        It should be noted that as of the 1960s, every Canadian province had established provincial courts with broad powers over criminal and penal matters, as well as limited civil jurisdiction.[85] This development of criminal and penal law created somewhat of a transformation in the Canadian judicial landscape.[86]

[64]        The enactment of the Canadian Charter of Rights and Freedoms[87] in 1982 led the Supreme Court of Canada to define the independence of the judges of the provincial courts hearing criminal or penal matters, based on s. 11(d) of the Canadian Charter.[88] The constitutional basis for the independence of the provincial courts was reinforced in 1997 in the Reference re Remuneration of Judges of the Provincial Court of Prince Edward Island,[89] where Lamer, C.J., speaking for the majority, based his remarks on the preamble to the Constitution Act, 1867, the unwritten constitutional rules and s. 11(d) of the Canadian Charter to find that provincial courts enjoy constitutional guarantees similar to those of the superior courts.[90]

[65]        In 1988, the Court of Québec was created by merging the Provincial Court, the Court of Sessions of the Peace and the Youth Court.[91] The civil jurisdiction of this new court was increased to $30,000 in 1995,[92] and then to $70,000 in 2002.[93] Finally, on January 1, 2016, when the new Code of Civil Procedure came into force, the civil jurisdiction of the Court of Québec was increased to $85,000.[94]

[66]        In summary, since the Supreme Court of Canada’s 1965 judgment, the civil jurisdiction of the Magistrate’s Court and its provincial successors grew from $500 to $85,000, a very significant increase at first sight.

[67]        Moreover, save for so-called “small claims” files with a value of no more than $15,000, which are decided in a summary manner,[95] all the other civil matters before the Court of Québec are, for the most part, governed by the same scheme as matters coming before the Superior Court: the Court of Québec applies the same rules of procedure and evidence as the Superior Court, gives reasons for its judgments in the same manner, sits in the same courthouses and shares the same courtrooms. Similarly, in all these respects, the functioning of the Court of Québec in the exercise of the jurisdiction exclusively conferred upon it (such as in matters of adoption or recovery of property taxes) is the same as that of the Superior Court.

[68]        Faced with this situation, the chief justices of the Superior Court asked the government of the province not to increase the ceiling for the Court of Québec’s civil jurisdiction to $85,000, but their request was denied. They also asked, again unsuccessfully, that the constitutionality of the Court of Québec’s civil jurisdiction be decided by means of a reference to this Court or to the Supreme Court of Canada prior to the enactment or coming into force of the legislative amendment.

[69]        Given these refusals, on July 19, 2017, the chief justices of the Superior Court instituted proceedings for a declaratory judgment challenging the civil jurisdiction of the Court of Québec over matters other than those in which the value in dispute does not exceed $10,000. Subsequent to those legal proceedings, the provincial government adopted Order in Council 880-2017, dated August 30, 2017, referring the first question to the Court.

2.3. Context of the second reference question and circumstances under which the Court was seized thereof

[70]        The context of the second question, which deals with the Court of Québec’s appellate jurisdiction, is somewhat different.

[71]        The twentieth century was characterized by a phenomenal increase in the role of governments and public administration bureaucracy. This gave rise to the development of a multitude of so-called “administrative” bodies and tribunals to adjudicate the numerous disputes arising from the various government programs that are inherent in an interventionist state.[96] Authors Slapper and Kelly provide a summary, which applies just as well to Canada, of the origin and scope of this phenomenon in the United Kingdom:

Although attention tends to be focused on the operation of the courts as the forum within which legal decisions are taken, it is no longer the case that the bulk of legal and quasi-legal questions are determined within that court structure. There are, as an alternative to the court system, a large number of tribunals that have been set up under various acts of Parliament to rule on the operation of the particular schemes established under those Acts. Almost one million cases are dealt with by tribunals each year, and as the Royal Commission on Legal Services (Cmnd 7648) pointed out in 1979, the number of cases then being heard by tribunals was six times greater than the number of contested civil cases dealt with by the High Court and county court combined. It is evident, therefore, that tribunals are of major significance as alternatives to traditional courts in dealing with disputes.

The generally accepted explanation for the establishment and growth of tribunals in Britain since 1945 was to provide a specialist forum to deal with cases involving conflicts between an increasingly interventionist welfare state, its functionaries and the rights of private citizens. It is certainly true that, since 1945, the welfare state has intervened more and more in every aspect of people’s lives. The intention may have been to extend the various social benefits to a wider constituency, but in so doing, the machinery of the welfare state, and in reality those who operate that machinery, have been granted powers to control access to its benefits, and as a consequence have been given the power to interfere in and control the lives of individual subjects of the State. By its nature, welfare provision tends to be discretionary and dependent on the particular circumstances of a given case. As a consequence, State functionaries were extended discretionary power over the supply/withdrawal of welfare benefits. As the interventionist State replaced the completely free market as the source of welfare for many people, so access to the provisions made by the State became a matter of fundamental importance, and a focus for potential contention, especially given the discretionary nature of its provision. At the same time as welfare state provisions were being extended, the view was articulated that such provisions and projects should not be under the purview and control of the ordinary courts. It was felt that the judiciary reflected a culture that tended to favour a more market-centred, individualistic approach to the provision of rights and welfare and that their essentially formalistic approach to the resolution of disputes would not fit with the operation of the new projects. [97]

[72]        The superintending and reforming role of the superior courts with respect to these numerous new public bodies and administrative tribunals therefore needed adjustment. As a result, the Supreme Court of Canada rendered a series of judgments in which it opened the door for the development of Canadian administrative law while, at the same time, establishing the boundaries required to maintain the superintending and reforming power of the superior courts, a power which, it must be noted, is constitutionally guaranteed to litigants. In order to establish the context within which the second reference question was formulated, two judgments merit particular consideration: Farrah[98] and Crevier.[99]

[73]        The 1978 judgment in Farrah dealt with the constitutionality of a provincial regulatory agency in respect of public and private transport, the Transport Tribunal, comprised of three judges of the Provincial Court and tasked with hearing and determining appeals from decisions of the Quebec Transport Commission. The Transport Tribunal had, in particular, “jurisdiction, to the exclusion of any other court, to hear and dispose of […] in appeal, on any question of law, any decision of the [Quebec Transport] Commission which terminates a matter”;[100] this grant of jurisdiction was coupled with an absolute privative clause insulating the decisions of that tribunal from the Superior Court’s superintending and reforming power. All the justices of the Supreme Court of Canada expressed the opinion that this jurisdiction was unconstitutional in that it did not respect the superintending and reforming power of the Superior Court guaranteed under section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867. While recognizing that a provincial legislature is, in principle, competent to reduce the scope of the supervisory power of a superior court by precluding the review by that court of the decisions of an inferior tribunal which are tainted with certain irregularities, [101] Pratte, J., speaking for the majority, concluded that the statute could not, however, entirely exclude that supervisory power. He explained that “[t]he net combined effect of s. 58(a) and of the privative clauses (ss. 24 and 72 of the Transport Act) [was] therefore to transfer to the Transport Tribunal part of the inherent supervisory authority that was vested in the Superior Court at the time of Confederation”,[102] which was contrary to section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867.[103]

[74]        For its part, the 1981 judgment in Crevier considered the constitutionality of the Quebec Professions Tribunal, which was also comprised of judges of the Provincial Court charged with hearing appeals from any decision of a disciplinary committee of a professional order governed by the Quebec Professional Code.[104] In a unanimous decision, the Supreme Court of Canada declared the tribunal, as then constituted, unconstitutional. To arrive at this finding, the Supreme Court considered three questions.

[75]        The first concerned the integration of the appeal within the administrative scheme of the disciplinary system under the Professional Code. The Supreme Court concluded that since “[t]he Professions Tribunal is given no function other than that of a general tribunal of appeal […] and it was, therefore, impossible to see its final appellate jurisdiction as part of an institutional arrangement by way of a regulatory scheme for governance of the various professions”.[105] In that regard, Laskin, C.J. referred to the remarks of Dickson, J. in Re Residential Tenancies Act, 1979,[106] made shortly before, in holding that if the adjudicative function is the only – or central – function of a tribunal, so that the tribunal can be said to be operating like a court provided for in section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867,[107] the administrative appellate function is no longer merely subsidiary, ancillary or necessarily incidental to the achievement of an administrative policy objective; that is, the function is no longer exercised as part of the administrative scheme, but as a substitute to the superintending and reforming power of a superior court. Here are the specific remarks of Dickson, J.:

[…] It is no longer sufficient simply to examine the particular power or function of a tribunal and ask whether this power or function was once exercised by s. 96 courts. This would be examining the power or function in a “detached” manner, contrary to the reasoning in Tomko. What must be considered is the “context” in which this power is exercised. Tomko leads to the following result: it is possible for administrative tribunals to exercise powers and jurisdiction which once were exercised by the s. 96 courts. It will all depend on the context of the exercise of the power. It may be that the impugned “judicial powers” are merely subsidiary or ancillary to general administrative functions assigned to the tribunal (John East; Tomko) or the powers may be necessarily incidental to the achievement of a broader policy goal of the legislature (Mississauga). In such a situation, the grant of judicial power to provincial appointees is valid. The scheme is only invalid when the adjudicative function is a sole or central function of the tribunal (Farrah) so that the tribunal can be said to be operating “like a s. 96 court”.[108]

[76]        The second question in Crevier considered the effect of a privative clause on section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867. The Supreme Court concluded that such a privative clause can validly exclude judicial review by a superior court on questions of law, but not on questions of jurisdiction.[109] Laskin, C.J. stated the following:

It is true that this is the first time that this Court has declared unequivocally that a provincially-constituted statutory tribunal cannot constitutionally be immunized from review of decisions on questions of jurisdiction. In my opinion, this limitation, arising by virtue of s. 96, stands on the same footing as the well-accepted limitation on the power of provincial statutory tribunals to make unreviewable determinations of constitutionality. There may be differences of opinion as to what are questions of jurisdiction but, in my lexicon, they rise above and are different from errors of law, whether involving statutory construction or evidentiary matters or other matters. It is now unquestioned that privative clauses may, when properly framed, effectively oust judicial review on questions of law and, indeed, on other issues not touching jurisdiction. […][110]

[77]        Lastly, the third question concerned the impact of the judgment in Farrah, which Laskin, C.J. described as follows: “In short, what the Farrah case decided was that to give a provincially-constituted statutory tribunal a jurisdiction in appeal on questions of law without limitation, and to reinforce this appellate authority by excluding any supervisory recourse to the Quebec Superior Court, was to create a s. 96 court.”[111]

[78]        Following that decision, the provincial legislature amended the Professional Code in order to replace the absolute privative clauses contained therein[112] and add the words “except on a question of jurisdiction”,[113] thereby confirming that the Professions Tribunal is subject to the superintending and reforming power of the Superior Court on such questions of jurisdiction. It is worth noting that, in general, privative clauses contained in Quebec statutes from that point forward have contained this same exception regarding questions of jurisdiction. 

[79]        Farrah and Crevier raised certain doubts regarding the possibility, constitutionally speaking, for a provincial legislature to confer an appellate function on questions of law to a tribunal that was neither a superior court contemplated in section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867 nor an administrative appellate body forming an integral part of a provincial administrative scheme. At least that was the conclusion of author Jean-François Jobin in his book L’article 96 de la Loi constitutionnelle de 1867 et les organismes inférieurs d’appel:

[translation]

In this context, it seems arguable that an appeal limited to questions of law is, at least as regards its purpose, akin to the inherent superintending and reforming power of the superior courts insofar as it seeks primarily to oversee the legality of the lower body’s decision rather than its merits. Consequently, such a power should only be granted to judges appointed by the Governor General, or, as the minority in Farrah seems to have accepted, to a “provincial” body whose primary functions are administrative.

[…]

The decisive element in the ratio in Farrah should therefore be the indisputable fact that the appellate jurisdiction conferred in that case upon the Transport Tribunal was limited to questions of law, as Pratte, J. pointed out. That being said, such an appeal may, rightly so, be likened to the superintending and reforming power exercised by the superior courts; in both situations, the crux of the matter under supervision concerns the legality of the decisions of the inferior body. The object of the power exercised by the inferior body is therefore of little import, because the appellate power conferred is, in and of itself, akin to an analogous power exercised in 1867 by section 96 courts.

The attribution of an appellate jurisdiction limited to questions of law, therefore, does not pass the first step of the jurisprudential test, that is, the historical step. Thus, such appeals will have been validly entrusted to a body whose members are appointed by a province only if they truly appear to be merely ancillary to the overall general administrative functions assigned to that body. […][114]

[Emphasis added]

[80]        It was, in fact, for this reason that the 1987 Ouellette report, which dealt with Quebec’s administrative tribunals, recommended to the Government of Quebec that it abolish appeals of decisions of administrative bodies to the Provincial Court on questions of law or jurisdiction.[115] A similar recommendation was made in 1994, albeit for different reasons, in the Garant report, which led to the creation of the Administrative Tribunal of Québec as the appellate tribunal for a number of the province’s administrative bodies.[116]

[81]        Nonetheless, subsequent to the Farrah and Crevier judgments, and notwithstanding the recommendations of the Ouellette and Garant reports, the Quebec legislature decided instead to give the Court of Québec a greater role within the province’s administrative justice system.[117] This spectacular growth in the Court of Québec’s jurisdiction led Professor Garant to conclude that [translation] “the Quebec legislature had shown great favour towards it”,[118] so much so that, in his view, this raised the question: [translation] “Is there a difference, in our legal tradition and in Quebec culture, between the mission of the Superior Court and that of the Court of Québec, particularly as superintendent of the administrative apparatus?”[119] Professor Garant’s answer was a resolute “no”, and he noted that in [translation] “the context of a sophisticated administrative justice tailored to contemporary reality, it seems obvious that the state does not need two superior courts with substantially the same mission. There is one too many. Which one?”[120] It must be noted, however, that Professor Garant’s comments were made after the Supreme Court judgment in Proprio Direct[121] and can be largely explained by that judgment, which we will now address.

[82]        Indeed, there is no better illustration of the problem related to the respective roles of the Superior Court and the Court of Québec within the administrative justice system than the development of the jurisprudence dealing with standards of review. According to this Court’s most recent jurisprudence, set out in a series of judgments in which the constitutional issue was not raised, appeals from decisions of administrative tribunals to the Court of Québec are, in principle, subject to the standards of review applicable to judicial review.[122] This jurisprudence was cited by the Supreme Court of Canada in Proprio Direct[123] and subsequently by this Court, but not without certain questions being raised.[124]

[83]        It should be noted that the jurisprudence of the Supreme Court of Canada addressing the standard of review applicable to appeals from decisions of administrative tribunals to the courts was developed in the context of appeals to superior courts, be it a provincial superior court,[125] a provincial court of appeal[126] or a federal court.[127] Indeed, no province other than Quebec, with its Court of Québec, has allowed a court of justice with provincially appointed judges (as opposed to an administrative appellate tribunal) to hear and determine appeals.

[84]        As this Court noted in Parizeau, it has been acknowledged that appeals of administrative decisions to administrative appellate tribunals are not governed by standards of judicial review.[128] Thus, insofar as the constitutionality of the Court of Québec’s appellate jurisdiction relies on the integration of that appeal within the administrative scheme in question, one may well query why the Court of Québec sitting in appeal of such an administrative decision should act like the equivalent of a court of judicial review.[129] In so doing, would it not be usurping the Superior Court’s review jurisdiction?

[85]        This is, in fact, the conclusion asserted by the chief justices of the Superior Court in their July 17, 2017 application for a declaratory judgment.[130] In it, they seek judicial declarations confirming that the Court of Québec has no jurisdiction to exercise a power of judicial review whether by way of appeal or otherwise. They contend that only the Superior Court can review decisions rendered by Quebec’s judicial and administrative tribunals, notably, under section 100 of the Real Estate Brokerage Act,[131] section 159 of the Act respecting administrative justice[132] and section 147 of the Act respecting access to documents held by public bodies and the protection of personal information.[133]

[86]        The conclusions of that application are what prompted the second reference question.

2.4. Summary description of the Superior Court and the Court of Québec

[87]        For a more complete picture of the situation and the role of each court in Quebec’s judicial landscape, we will now briefly discuss the current structure and functions of the Superior Court and the Court of Québec.

[88]        According to the Courts of Justice Act, the Superior Court comprises 157 judicial positions (of which 154 are currently funded by the federal government[134]), including a Chief Justice position, a Senior Associate Chief Justice position and an Associate Chief Justice position.[135] It is also composed of supernumerary judges, that is, judges who are eligible for retirement, but less than 75 years old, and have chosen to continue to hold office, with a reduced workdload.[136] As at September 9, 2019, there were 41 supernumerary judges, or the equivalent of 20.5 full-time positions.[137] Superior Court judges can hold office until the age of 75.[138] Like their counterparts across Canada, they receive the same remuneration, which is established by a statute of Parliament.[139]

[89]        The Superior Court is the court of original general jurisdiction in Quebec.[140] Its jurisdiction covers the entire territory of the province,[141] in 36 judicial districts grouped into two major administrative divisions, Montreal and Quebec.[142]

[90]        In civil matters, it hears and determines all applications not exclusively assigned by law to another tribunal.[143] In addition to its jurisdiction over applications in which the value in dispute is $85,000 or more, this means that, for example, it has jurisdiction over disputes involving the ownership of immovable property and its dismemberments, matters involving wills and successions, a variety of family law matters (nullity of marriage or separation from bed and board, custody of children), and others matters. It also has exclusive jurisdiction over class actions and applications for injunctions.[144] Moreover, it hears and determines a number of civil matters provided for in certain federal statutes, including applications for divorce[145] and those related to bankruptcy and insolvency.[146]

[91]        In criminal matters, it has exclusive jurisdiction with respect to serious indictable offences such as murder, treason and judicial corruption, and which often involve a jury.[147] It has jurisdiction over matters of habeas corpus; it also exercises superintending and reforming powers over other tribunals with criminal or penal jurisdiction; it hears appeals, provided for by law, from certain judgments of these other tribunals.[148]

[92]        The Superior Court is also vested with a general power of judicial review, which is the subject matter of the second reference question. It exercises this power over all courts (other than the Court of Appeal) and administrative tribunals in Quebec, as well as over public bodies, legal persons established in the public interest or for a private interest, and partnerships and associations and other groups not endowed with juridical personality.[149] This power, however, cannot be exercised in cases declared by law to be under the exclusive purview of a court, person, body or group, or in cases excluded by law, except where there is lack or excess of jurisdiction. Thus, where the Superior Court is seized of an application for judicial review and where there has been an excess of jurisdiction or the procedure followed has been affected by some serious irregularity, it can, in particular, review or quash the judgment or decision of the inferior tribunal.[150]

[93]        As for the Court of Québec, it has jurisdiction in civil, criminal and penal matters, and matters involving youth protection. It also sits in appeal of decisions of certain administrative tribunals. In all cases, its jurisdiction over a particular matter must be provided for by statute.[151] It exercises this jurisdiction throughout Quebec.[152]

[94]        The Court of Québec is composed of 306 judges.[153] This includes a Chief Judge, a Senior Associate Chief Judge and 4 Associate Chief Judges,[154] each appointed for a non-renewable term of 7 years,[155] as well as 10 coordinating judges (to which there may be added a maximum of 12 associate coordinating judges), each appointed for a renewable term of no more than 3 years.[156] Judges of the Court of Québec are remunerated by the province[157] and must cease to hold office upon attaining the age of 70 years.[158]

[95]        The Court of Québec consists of three divisions: the Civil Division, the Criminal and Penal Division, and the Youth Division.[159] The Chief Judge may choose to assign a judge to a particular division,[160] but each judge retains jurisdiction throughout Quebec and for all matters under the jurisdiction of the Court of Québec (save for a few exceptions involving labour laws[161]), regardless of the division to which the judge has been assigned.[162]

[96]        The Civil Division hears and determines, notably, applications where the value in dispute is less than $85,000,[163] the jurisdiction that is the subject of the first question of this reference. It hears and determines appeals from decisions made by a number of administrative tribunals and bodies, as well as certain tax matters. Lastly, the Small Claims Division of the Civil Division decides matters where the value in dispute does not exceed $15,000.[164]

[97]        The Criminal and Penal Division hears and determines all cases involving criminal offences over which judges of the Court of Québec have jurisdiction under the Criminal Code[165] and, insofar as the accused so elects, those involving criminal offences not exclusively reserved to the Superior Court.[166] It also hears and determines cases involving provincial penal offences, insofar as jurisdiction over the matter is not conferred upon a municipal court.[167]

[98]        Lastly, judges in the Youth Division exercise the powers of the youth justice court in accordance with the Youth Criminal Justice Act[168] and have jurisdiction over applications under the Youth Protection Act,[169] adoption matters, and penal proceedings brought under the Code of Penal Procedure where the defendant is a minor.[170]

[99]        The table reproduced in the Second Schedule to this opinion sets out the number of files opened in the Court of Québec and in the Superior Court, all matters combined, for the 2012-13 to 2017-18 judicial years. Overall, as can be seen, most of the court files in Quebec, that is, over 85%, are opened in the Court of Québec (without counting the considerable number of files opened in the municipal courts), which can be explained primarily by the large number of criminal and penal matters.

[100]     In civil matters, the Court of Québec (civil division, voluntary deposit and small claims) opens on average nearly 55% of the total files, while the Superior Court (civil division, commercial division and family division), receives approximately 45%, half of which involve matrimonial and family law.

[101]     Having described the context for the reference, we now turn to the questions referred to the Court.

3.   THE FIRST REFERENCE QUESTION

[102]     The first reference question seeks to determine whether the monetary ceiling imposed by article 35 C.C.P. falls within the powers of the legislature under section 92(14) of the Constitution Act, 1867, without infringing on the core jurisdiction of the Superior Court of Quebec which is protected under section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867.

3.1. Preliminary Comments

[103]     It is well established that provinces have great latitude in matters of administration of justice within their territory, provided they respect the limits imposed by sections 96 and 101 of the Constitution Act, 1867, reproduced above:

[39]      Legislatures have the power and responsibility to legislate in relation to the administration of justice (Constitution Act, 1867, ss. 92(14), 96 and 101). This includes the power to create, transform and abolish judicial offices. Provincial legislatures also have power over the jurisdiction of the courts they create (MacMillan Bloedel Ltd. v. Simpson, [1995] 4 S.C.R. 725, at paras. 10-12). Reforms can contribute to public confidence in the administration of justice. Indeed, by implementing reforms to the justice system, governments and legislatures often play an active role in ensuring and enhancing public confidence in the judiciary. Reforms can be driven by a number of reasons: improving the independence and qualifications of judicial officers, adapting to new realities, and increasing access to justice. However, all reforms must respect the constitutional requirements for judicial independence (paras. 36-37) and the core jurisdiction of the courts established under s. 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867.[171]

[104]     Where the attribution of jurisdiction to an inferior court satisfies the three-part test in Re Residential Tenancies Act, 1979,[172] those limits will have been respected. Moreover, based on the analysis proposed by the Supreme Court in MacMillan Bloedel,[173] it goes without saying that a province cannot remove from the Superior Court any part of its core jurisdiction.

[105]     The three-step test established by the Supreme Court in Re Residential Tenancies Act, 1979 was considered in a number of decisions and was refined over time. Before undertaking an analysis, it is important to characterize the impugned power. That characterization can have a serious impact on the historical analysis that must follow.[174] This therefore “require[s] a strict, that is to say a narrow, approach”[175] and an analysis focused on the type of dispute involved[176] rather than the particular remedy sought.[177]

[106]     The three-step test must be applied as follows:

[58]      […] In brief, the test asks (1) whether the power conferred broadly conforms to a power or jurisdiction exercised by a superior, district or county court at the time of Confederation; (2) if so, whether the power is a judicial power; and (3) if so, whether the power is either subsidiary or ancillary to a predominantly administrative function or necessarily incidental to such a function: Reference re Amendments to the Residential Tenancies Act (N.S.), [1996] 1 S.C.R. 186, at para. 74.[178]

[107]     The first step, the historical analysis, “involves consideration, in the light of the historical conditions existing in 1867, of the particular power or jurisdiction conferred upon the tribunal. The question here is whether the power or jurisdiction conforms to the power or jurisdiction exercised by superior, district or county courts at the time of Confederation”.[179] One must search for an “analogous, not precisely the same, jurisdiction”;[180] in other words, it “is not [suggested] that jurisdiction must have been entirely or even generally concurrent, for the nature of the inferior-superior court distinction will invariably mean that the former’s jurisdiction was limited in some way”.[181] Thus, the historical analysis must consider the conditions existing “reasonably contemporaneously with Confederation”, [182] in the four confederating provinces and, in the absence of a conclusive result, in the United Kingdom during that same period.[183] It may be that the analysis will lead to the finding that there is “a general shared involvement in a jurisdiction, not concurrent jurisdiction in all respects”,[184] to quote the words of Wilson, J., speaking for the majority in Sobeys.

[108]     As Dickson, J. wrote in Re Residential Tenancies Act, 1979, “[i]f the historical inquiry leads to the conclusion that the power or jurisdiction is not broadly conformable to jurisdiction formerly exercised by s. 96 courts, that is the end of the matter”.[185] The same is true if the analysis leads to the conclusion that there is a general shared involvement, as McLachlin, J. (as she then was) stated in Reference re Amendments to the Residential Tenancies Act (N.S.):[186]

75        [...] If the inferior courts before Confederation alone exercised the power, or shared it in a practical way with the future superior courts, s. 96 is not engaged and no further enquiry is required.

[109]     If, however, “the historical evidence indicates that the impugned power is identical or analogous to a power exercised by s. 96 courts at Confederation, then one must proceed to the second step of the inquiry”.[187]

[110]     The second step “involves consideration of the function within its institutional setting”.[188] The question is whether the function is judicial or administrative. If the function is administrative, the grant of jurisdiction is presumptively valid. If, however, the function is judicial, then one must proceed to the last step.

[111]     The third step consists in “review[ing] the tribunal’s function as a whole in order to appraise the impugned function in its entire institutional context”.[189] One must ask whether the impugned judicial functions are merely “subsidiary or ancillary to general administrative functions assigned to the tribunal (John East; Tomko)”[190] or “necessarily incidental to the achievement of a broader policy goal of the legislature (Mississauga)”.[191] In such a case, the grant of jurisdiction is presumptively valid. Conversely, “when the adjudicative function is a sole or central function of the tribunal (Farrah)”,[192]the grant of jurisdiction is unconstitutional, unless it forms part of a social policy goal based on a different notion of the subject area of the jurisdiction granted from that which existed at the time of Confederation.[193]

[112]     Next, even if the three-step test set out in Re Residential Tenancies Act, 1979 suggests that the grant is valid, because the challenged jurisdiction was generally exercised by the inferior courts at the time of Confederation, the function in question is merely administrative or the central function of the tribunal is not adjudicative or is based on a different conceptual or institutional notion, the constitutional analysis cannot automatically end there:

[27]      In the case of transfers of jurisdiction which are within the inherent powers of a superior court, the Residential Tenancies test does not provide a framework for analyzing the most important aspect of constitutional infringement. The true problem in this case is the exclusivity of the grant. The impugned section of the Young Offenders Act clearly states that exclusive jurisdiction over ex facie contempt of court is transferred to the youth court. […] As my colleague McLachlin J. notes, in most instances where a s. 96 analysis is employed, the grant of jurisdiction is exclusive. The requirement to consider whether the corresponding removal of jurisdiction is valid only arises when the core jurisdiction of superior courts is affected. In many instances, therefore, the Residential Tenancies test provides a complete answer to the constitutional query.[194]

[Underlining in the original; bold added]

 

[113]     Indeed, if the impugned jurisdiction removes a power forming part of the core jurisdiction of the superior courts and grants that power exclusively to an inferior court, it then becomes necessary to proceed to the analysis suggested in MacMillan Bloedel.[195]

[114]     While it may be difficult to specifically identify the powers forming part of that inherent jurisdiction, the core jurisdiction of the superior courts is defined as “[t]he full range of powers which comprise the inherent jurisdiction of a superior court [and which] are, together, its ‘essential character’ or ‘immanent attribute’”.[196] This notion “developed from the role of provincial superior courts in Canada’s legal system”[197] and guarantees them a “traditional core of superior court jurisdiction”.[198]

[115]     In the matter before us, however, there is no such identification problem. Indeed, in Trial Lawyers, the Supreme Court recognized that the power to “resolve disputes between individuals and decide questions of private [...] law”[199] forms part of the inherent jurisdiction of the superior courts. It must be pointed out, however, that Trial Lawyers did not deal with concurrent judicial powers, as is the case here.

[116]     That being so, the “doctrine of inherent jurisdiction does not operate without limits”[200] and may be circumscribed.[201] It is “limited by the institutional roles and capacities that emerge out of our constitutional framework and values”.[202] One must therefore “take into account the structure of our Constitution, which includes other fundamental principles, such as the rule of law and parliamentary supremacy”.[203] In other words, a province may legislate in matters of administration of justice, even if this power affects the core jurisdiction of the superior courts to adjudicate civil disputes. It must, however, do so in a manner that is consistent with that jurisdiction. Trial Lawyers is helpful on this point:

[36]      It follows that the province’s power to impose hearing fees cannot deny people the right to have their disputes resolved in the superior courts. To do so would be to impermissibly impinge on s. 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867. Rather, the province’s powers under s. 92(14) must be exercised in a manner that is consistent with the right of individuals to bring their cases to the superior courts and have them resolved there.[204]

[Emphasis added]

3.2. The respondents’ claims

[117]     Save for the chief justices of the Superior Court, the Attorney General of Quebec and all interveners on this first question are of the opinion that the first paragraph of article 35 C.C.P. is constitutional. In their opinion, the Quebec legislature merely exercised its jurisdiction over the administration of justice in the province when it enacted this provision. They argue that the exercise of that jurisdiction, within the context at hand, does not in any way infringe section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867.

[118]     The Attorney General of Quebec is of the view that the first paragraph of article 35 C.C.P. is constitutional under both the three-step test developed in Re Residential Tenancies Act, 1979 and under the analysis of the core jurisdiction of the superior courts set out in MacMillan Bloedel.

[119]     As regards the analysis under Re Residential Tenancies Act, 1979, the Attorney General of Quebec claims that, in 1867, the inferior courts of the four founding provinces had a general shared involvement in civil disputes based on contractual and extra-contractual obligations. Consequently, the challenged jurisdiction did not belong exclusively to the superior courts listed in section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867.

[120]     The Attorney General further argues that the first paragraph of article 35 C.C.P. does not affect the core jurisdiction of the Superior Court of Quebec to adjudicate private disputes, because the jurisdiction is not entirely or substantially removed. On the contrary, even if the Court of Québec has the task of ruling on civil disputes of less than $85,000, the Superior Court retains jurisdiction to adjudicate the same disputes over that threshold.

[121]     In the view of the Attorney General of Quebec, there is no legal requirement that the jurisdiction of the Court of Québec in civil matters have a monetary ceiling. In any event, it is argued, the existing $85,000 ceiling is below any conceivable limit and remains constitutional.

[122]     At the hearing, the Attorney General of Quebec stated that nothing requires that the general jurisdiction of the Superior Court of Quebec be exercised at first instance:

[translation]

[…] All I’m saying is that there is nothing there that establishes that a civil law dispute must first go through the Superior Court at first instance.

If the parties have access to an independent court to argue their case, I don’t see how there is a violation of the Trial Lawyers principles, because the parties have access to a court. […][205]

[123]     The Attorney General of Canada and the Attorney General of British Columbia are also of the opinion that article 35 C.C.P. is constitutional. However, their argument differs from that of the Attorney General of Quebec, because they are of the opinion that the three-step test discussed above is inappropriate in the present case. In their view, this is not a situation in which it is necessary to consider the transfer of a specialized jurisdiction from a section 96 court to an inferior court, but rather a situation in which it is necessary to determine whether granting a jurisdiction, particularly a monetary jurisdiction, to the Court of Québec changes the nature or character of the Superior Court. Recognizing that the civil jurisdiction of the Court of Québec must have a ceiling in order to respect the constitutional arrangement and guarantee the general jurisdiction reserved to the superior courts under section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867, they propose an analysis focused on an examination of the core jurisdiction of those courts and the effect of the monetary limit set by the Quebec legislature.[206]

[124]     The chief justices of the Superior Court are the only interveners who argue that the first paragraph of article 35 C.C.P. is unconstitutional. Since their arguments changed over the course of the proceedings, however, a brief summary thereof is useful.

[125]     In their application for a declaratory judgment dated July 17, 2017, they argued that, in 1867, the inferior courts had a concurrent jurisdiction with the courts listed in section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867 as regards civil disputes of $100 or less. Above that amount, the section 96 courts had exclusive jurisdiction to adjudicate such civil disputes.[207] Based on the current value of the dollar, they argued, the Quebec legislature could not, without impairing the core jurisdiction of the Superior Court,[208] grant the Court of Québec exclusive jurisdiction in civil matters if the value in dispute were to exceed $10,000.[209]

[126]     After the Attorney General of Quebec filed the notice of reference on October 5, 2017, the chief justices of the Superior Court modified their position. In a notice dated June 19, 2018[210], they henceforth indicated that as regards section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867, the upper limit of the exclusive monetary jurisdiction of the Court of Québec could not be fixed above $55,000.

[127]     Finally, their brief, dated September 17, 2018, brings further nuance to their position: while they initially stated that, in 1867, the inferior courts had a concurrent jurisdiction with the section 96 courts over civil disputes of $100 and less, they now assert that those inferior courts did not exercise any general jurisdiction over civil matters,[211] but only jurisdiction over certain specific civil matters.[212] They are therefore of the opinion that, in 1867, the section 96 courts had exclusive general civil jurisdiction throughout the entire territory of Quebec, regardless of the monetary limit,[213] contrary to the opinion they expressed earlier.

[128]     In their view, although they believe that the first paragraph of article 35 C.C.P. is incompatible with section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867, since only the courts listed therein had a general civil jurisdiction in 1867, they nevertheless propose that this Court recognize a limited compatibility between both provisions, based on a maximum monetary limit rooted in history, the law and the jurisprudence.[214]

[129]     This limit would be determined by updating the value of an amount of $100 in 1867, which was the maximum monetary jurisdiction of the inferior courts in Lower Canada over certain civil matters only. According to the report prepared by their expert, the economist Vincent Geloso[215], the conversion to the current value must be based on the nominal GDP per capita and, for the year 2017, is equal to approximately $55,000.[216]

[130]     The Conseil de la magistrature du Québec and the Canadian Association of Provincial Court Judges generally support the position of the Attorney General of Quebec on this point.

3.3. The appropriate analytical framework

[131]     In a preceding section discussing context, we referred to the opinion expressed in 1964 by this Court, in Renvoi concernant la constitutionnalité de la Cour de magistrat, to the effect that the Magistrate’s Court (the Court of Québec’s predecessor) was illegally constituted because its jurisdiction was broadly comparable to that exercised by the courts listed in section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867 at the time of Confederation.[217] Since then, that opinion has been neither confirmed nor contradicted by the Supreme Court,[218] which, at the time, answered the question as worded and recognized the constitutionality of the increase in the monetary jurisdiction of the Magistrate’s Court to the then current value of the dollar.[219]

[132]     It is worth highlighting once again the view of the chief justices of the Superior Court that the Quebec legislature can — in a manner consistent with section 96 — grant jurisdiction in civil matters to the Court of Québec for an amount equal to a monetary ceiling determined by history, the law and the jurisprudence, even if, as they argue, in 1867, the inferior courts did not have general jurisdiction over such matters.

[133]     The question submitted in this reference is similar to the one that was before the Court in 1964 in Renvoi concernant la constitutionnalité de la Cour de magistrat.[220] For ease of reference and comparison, we have reproduced both questions:

-     in 1964, the question was worded as follows:

      [translation]

      Is the Act respecting the jurisdiction of the Magistrate’s Court, 11-12 Eliz. II, c. 62, [which increases the civil jurisdiction of the Magistrate’s Court from $200 to $500] unconstitutional in whole or in part?[221]

-     in 2018, the question is the following:

      [translation provided by applicant’s counsel]

      Are the provisions of the first paragraph of article 35 of the Code of Civil Procedure (chapter C-25.01), setting at less than $85,000 the limit to the exclusive monetary jurisdiction of the Court of Québec, valid with regard to section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867, given the jurisdiction of Quebec over the administration of justice under paragraph 14 of section 92 of the Constitution Act, 1867?[222]

[134]     In 1964, this Court found that the Act respecting the jurisdiction of the Magistrate’s Court was unconstitutional because [translation] “the jurisdiction of the Magistrate’s Court [was] generally equivalent to the jurisdiction exercised at the time of Confederation by the county courts”,[223] which courts are listed in section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867 and whose judges should therefore be appointed by the Governor General of Canada.

[135]     In 1965, however, the Supreme Court, in a judgment written by Fauteux, J., considered that it was not [translation] “open” to it to make such a decision given the specific framework circumscribed by the question. He was of the opinion that the analysis should be limited to the following:

[translation]

On its merits, the only issue raised by the question, as delimited in the order in council, is that of determining whether the increase, from $200 to $500, of the monetary limit of the jurisdiction of the Magistrate’s Court – a Court that was indisputably not within those contemplated in s. 96 when it was created in 1869 and which has been viewed as such to this day – is a fact that, in and of itself, with nothing more, is liable to transform the nature of that Court into a Court within the meaning of s. 96 or one analogous to those mentioned therein. […][224]

[136]     As the Supreme Court stated in Reference Re Canada Assistance Plan (B.C.), one must be careful to avoid pitfalls arising from question as submitted, because the “court should avoid a ‘narrow literalism’, [but] care must [also] be taken that the interpretation of a question does not amount to a new question”.[225] Sopinka, J. added that “[i]f a question is clear, then the court is indeed bound to answer it on its terms, so long as it is justiciable”.[226]

[137]     The question submitted, therefore, does not invite the Court to determine whether the Court of Québec’s civil jurisdiction, as established by the Courts of Justice Act,[227] is compatible with section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867, but rather whether the $85,000 monetary limit set out in article 35 C.C.P. respects the core jurisdiction of the Superior Court of Québec established by section 96. In other words, the Court must determine whether the current limit is equivalent to the limit imposed on the inferior courts of the confederating provinces or whether it infringes on the core jurisdiction of the Superior Court, deprives it of that jurisdiction or unduly limits that jurisdiction.[228]

[138]     The question therefore assumes that the grant of civil jurisdiction to the Court of Québec is valid, which is consistent with the state of the law and which the chief justices of the Superior Court admit. Indeed, although they did not acknowledge that the inferior courts have a general civil jurisdiction, they nevertheless propose to the Court that it confirm the power of the legislature to grant civil jurisdiction to the Court of Québec up to a monetary limit, rooted in history, the law and the jurisprudence, of $55,000.

[139]     It follows that the appropriate analytical framework for answering the constitutional question before us must focus on assessing the core jurisdiction of the superior courts under section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867. The real question is therefore the following: By limiting the jurisdiction of the Superior Court of Quebec to adjudicate disputes in civil matters to cases in which the value in dispute is $85,000 and more, is the legislature infringing on the core jurisdiction of the Superior Court of Quebec to adjudicate private law disputes?

[140]     The legislature can certainly limit the power of the Superior Court to adjudicate civil disputes, provided the “nature” of the court is not thereby affected. Indeed, while the Supreme Court, in Trial Lawyers, confirmed that this power forms part of the core jurisdiction of the superior courts[229], this does not imply that they must absolutely adjudicate all civil disputes. This would have been surprising given, for example, the deciions in Grondin[230] and in Reference re Amendments to the Residential Tenancies Act (N.S.),[231] in which the Supreme Court acknowledged that the legislature can establish administrative tribunals with an adjudicative function to decide disputes involving residential lease matters, which are civil disputes. Nonetheless, as discussed earlier (see supra, para. [116]), the Supreme Court clearly indicated in Trial Lawyers that the power under section 92(14) of the Constitution Act, 1867 must be exercised “in a manner that is consistent with the right of individuals to bring their cases to the superior courts and have them resolved there”.[232] As explained in Scowby v. Glendinning by La Forest, J., who dissented, but not on this issue:[233]

In examining the matter, it is obvious that one should have an eye on what the Fathers must have had in mind, and this demands that one look to see if the jurisdiction sought to be vested in a tribunal is one that generally conforms to that exercised by a s. 96 court in 1867. But judicial functions, like other parts of the Constitution, cannot be frozen in an 1867 mold. Adaptations must be permitted to allow the legislatures scope to deal effectively with emerging social problems and concerns, and to develop new techniques of dispute resolution and the expeditious disposition of relatively minor disputes. At the same time, the s. 96 courts must continue their exclusive function of determining basic judicial questions of public order and public policy, or as Estey J. has put it, those “serious and frequently profound difficulties arising in the community” which the Constitution obviously contemplated should be their responsibility; see B.C. Family Relations Act, Re, supra, at p. 107.[234]

[Emphasis added]

[141]     In the case before us, the analysis must focus on the monetary limit set out in article 35 C.C.P. Indeed, pursuant to its power over the administration of justice, the legislature of Quebec can increase the monetary jurisdiction of the Court of Québec, but it must do so without altering the core jurisdiction of the superior courts to “resolve disputes between individuals and decide questions of private [...] law”.[235]

[142]     In that regard, when the Attorney General of Quebec was asked about its interpretation of article 35 C.C.P., given that the $85,000 limit exceeds the limit existing at the time of Confederation, as indexed, counsel replied that the civil jurisdiction formerly exercised by inferior courts was exercised by courts that did not have the requisite independence. Now that the provincial courts provide sufficient guarantees of independence, a monetary ceiling is no longer truly required, which would supports the view that the Superior Court does not necessarily have to exercise its original general civil jurisdiction.[236]

[143]     This argument is questionable. Such a restructuring of the civil jurisdiction of the inferior and superior courts would necessarily require the use of the constitutional amending formula. Indeed, at the time of Confederation, the general civil jurisdiction in first instance belonged to the Superior Court, and therefore to federally appointed judges. The 1866 Code of Civil Procedure echoed this principle.

[144]     As regards the civil jurisdiction limit of the inferior courts in 1867, the chief justices of the Superior Court have proposed an amount of $100, which was used as the basis for the expert reports filed by the various parties. The Court is of the opinion that this amount is an appropriate starting point for the analysis. This is the amount of the maximum monetary jurisdiction exercised in 1867 by some of the inferior courts charged with hearing certain civil matters.[237] It was also the limit for appeals as of right at that time. In Sobeys,[238] the Supreme Court also characterized this amount as “quite of a substantial monetary jurisdiction / compétence monétaire fort substantielle”, and spoke of a “substantial” jurisdiction in Reference re Amendments to the Residential Tenancies Act (N.S.).[239]

[145]     What, then, is the critical threshold today that would limit the core jurisdiction of the Superior Court to resolve disputes and decide questions of private law, in the words of the Supreme Court?

[146]     The 1866 Code of Civil Procedure established a judicial organizational model which is largely that which the other Canadian provinces adopted, subject to considerations specific to each of them. It is no coincidence that, throughout Canada, the superior courts act as courts of original civil jurisdiction, save for cases which, due to the lower amounts at stake, are under the jurisdiction of courts with provincially appointed judges and are generally decided in a summary manner:

(a)  In Ontario, the Ontario Court of Justice, with provincially appointed judges, has no civil jurisdiction, and the Small Claims Court is a division of the Superior Court of Justice which hears civil disputes whose value does not exceed $25,000, which disputes may be referred to deputy judges who are lawyers appointed for a 3-year renewable term;[240]

(b)  In New Brunswick, the Provincial Court comprises a Small Claims Court which hears civil disputes whose value is $20,000 or less and decides them according to flexible rules of evidence and procedure;[241]

(c)  In Nova Scotia, the Provincial Court of Nova Scotia comprises a Small Claims Court which hears civil disputes whose value does not exceed $25,000 and decides them according to flexible rules of evidence and procedure;[242]

(d)  In Prince Edward Island, the Provincial Court has no civil jurisdiction, and the Small Claims Court is a division of the Supreme Court of Prince Edward Island which hears civil disputes whose value does not exceed $16,000;[243]

(e)  In Newfoundland and Labrador, the Provincial Court of Newfoundland and Labrador comprises a Small Claims Court which hears civil disputes whose value does not exceed $25,000;[244]

(f)   In Manitoba, the Provincial Court of Manitoba has no civil jurisdiction, and the Court of Queen’s Bench of Manitoba comprises a Small Claims Court which hears civil disputes whose value does not exceed $10,000;[245]

(g)  In Saskatchewan, the Provincial Court of Saskatchewan comprises a Small Claims Court which hears civil disputes whose value does not exceed $30,000;[246]

(h)  In Alberta, the Provincial Court hears civil disputes whose value does not exceed $50,000 and decides them according to more flexible rules of evidence;[247]

(i)    Lastly, in British Columbia, applications where the disputed value is $5,000 or less are heard by the Civil Resolution Tribunal, while those not exceeding $35,000 are heard by the Provincial Court of British Columbia, which comprises a Small Claims Court, and are decided according to flexible rules of evidence and procedure.[248]

[147]     The uniformity of this judicial organization – which also reflects that of Quebec at the time of Confederation – is particularly telling as to the scope of the provisions of the Constitution Act, 1867 relating to the jurisdiction of the superior courts over civil disputes or, at the very least, about the understanding thereof by the various provincial governments since 1867.

[148]     In light of the historical context as well as the objectives tied to the rule of law and national unity arising from section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867, it seems appropriate to state that the Superior Court can retain its core jurisdiction to adjudicate civil disputes only if that jurisdiction applies to “substantial” claims of litigants.

[149]     Based on the principle of national unity, “s. 96 should apply in the same way across the country”.[249] Section 96 “is regarded as a means of protecting the ‘core’ jurisdiction of the superior courts so as to provide for some uniformity throughout the country in the judicial system”,[250] which is to say that the provincial superior courts must all have a similar purpose as courts of original general civil jurisdiction.

[150]     While the jurisprudence developed by the Court of Québec is of utmost importance for the evolution of the civil law, under the Canadian constitutional order it is the Superior Court that is entrusted with adjudicating substantial civil disputes, that is, "determining basic judicial questions of public order and public policy, or as Estey J. has put it, those 'serious and frequently profound difficulties arising in the community'".[251] The province’s judicial organization must therefore reflect this constitutional constraint. Otherwise, there would be an unexplained difference between the role of the superior courts across the country and that of the Quebec Superior Court, a difference that could not be justified by the particularity that Quebec is the only one to apply the civil law rather than the common law.

[151]     Whether a private or public law dispute is “substantial” for purposes of applying s. 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867, however, cannot be assessed in the abstract.

[152]     In 1965, in the decision on the reference regarding the Magistrate’s Court, Fauteux, J. was of the view that [translation] “the broadening of the jurisdiction of the Magistrate’s Court, by increasing the number of dollars, viewed in light of the current value of the dollar, does not, in and of itself, when added to the jurisdiction peculiar to it as an Inferior Court not contemplated in s. 96, have the effect of making it a s. 96 Court”.[252] In other words, in light of the question submitted by the provincial government in that reference, the mere updating of the monetary jurisdiction of the Magistrate’s Court was not unconstitutional in and of itself.

[153]     Updating the monetary jurisdiction, however, is only one of the elements for determining whether a claim is substantial for a present-day litigant.

[154]     Indeed, for a variety of reasons, increasing the monetary limit over and above the actual updated amount would not automatically infringe the core jurisdiction of the superior courts. First, the Attorney General of Quebec’s expert, Mr. Yves St-Maurice, warned the Court about the margin of error and the difficulties inherent in the task of converting into current dollars the amount of $100 which was the ceiling for the monetary jurisdiction of the inferior courts in 1867. Indeed, each updating methodology has more or less significant disadvantages (including the nominal GDP methodology ultimately favoured by both experts).[253] Second, it would be inconceivable that the issue of the respective jurisdictions of the Superior Court and the Court of Québec over civil matters could be resolved by merely applying a mathematical formula, since that was not the intent at the time of Confederation. Third, the question cannot be reduced to a debate between experts.[254] And, fourth, the provincial legislature must have a certain latitude in exercising its jurisdiction over the administration of justice.

[155]     It is therefore appropriate to consider other factors such as: (1) the monetary threshold for appeals as of right to the Court of Appeal; (2) the legislative objectives of the limits set on the jurisdiction of the Court of Québec and, consequently, of the Superior Court; and (3) empirical and statistical data.

[156]     Indeed, the provincial legislature can limit the right of litigants to appeal by establishing a monetary threshold for appeals de plano. In Quebec, the limit applies to judgments where the disputed value on appeal is $60,000 or more (s. 30 para. 2(1) C.C.P.).

[157]     When establishing a monetary threshold for appeals as of right of decisions of trial courts, the legislature weighs a number of factors, including access to justice, the role of the Court of Appeal in establishing the law and the possibility for litigants to contest a judgment that contains an error.[255] By choosing that threshold, in all likelihood the Quebec legislature considers that claims equal to or greater than that threshold are sufficiently substantial that they should go before the Court without requiring the filtering mechanism of an application for leave to appeal.

[158]     Authors Sopinka, Gelowitz and Rankin explain the purpose of the monetary threshold of the appellate courts in Ontario, Alberta, the Northwest Territories and Nunavut as follows:

§ 1.130            As is discussed above, in Ontario, the jurisdiction of the Divisional Court and the Court of Appeal for Ontario is conditional on the monetary value of the dispute. This condition ensures that the highest appellate court in the province focuses its effort on significant disputes [the monetary threshold being $50,000[256]]. Other provinces that do not have a Divisional Court address the same concern by imposing a leave requirement on parties seeking to appeal from orders with a monetary impact below a set threshold.

§ 1.131            In Alberta, the Alberta Rules of Court impose a $25,000 threshold, exclusive of costs, below which no appeal lies without leave.348 This limit applies only to matters that can be estimated in money. Similarly, the Northwest Territories and Nunavut impose a $1,000 threshold below which no appeal lies without leave.349[257]

[Emphasis added]

[159]     It should be noted that not all Canadian provinces subject appeals as of right to a monetary threshold. This seems to support the idea that each legislature must be able to assess what constitutes a substantial claim within its territory. This does not, however, imply that the provinces without a monetary threshold for appeals as of right consider that each claim by a litigant is substantial, because the right to appeal is but one indicator of that assessment.

[161]     Lastly, empirical data and statistics regarding the courts in question can also provide guidance in determining what constitutes a substantial claim. For example, in Trial Lawyers, McLachlin, C.J., in addition to considering the objectives of the statute in question, based her decision on economic evidence, including data regarding the value of the basket of goods and services, to conclude that the hearing fees infringed on the core jurisdiction of the superior courts.[261]

[162]     Such indicia, although not exhaustive, may be important, depending on the context in which they are invoked.

3.4. Analysis

[163]     The first paragraph of article 35 C.C.P. grants the Court of Québec exclusive jurisdiction to hear and determine applications “in which the value of the subject matter of the dispute or the amount claimed […] is less than $85,000 / dans lesquelles soit la valeur de l’objet du litige, soit la somme réclamée […] est inférieure à 85 000 $”. The Quebec Superior Court therefore exercises its jurisdiction to hear and decide disputes based on that threshold as the starting point.

[164]     The Attorney General of Quebec and the majority of the interveners are of the view that the jurisdiction comprises “contractual and extra-contractual obligations”, while the chief justices of the Superior Court, as noted above, are of the view that the jurisdiction covers only certain specific civil matters with a limited monetary value at stake.

[165]     It is true that, at first glance at least, article 35 C.C.P. grants fairly broad civil jurisdiction to the Court of Québec to decide disputes in which the value is less than $85,000. However, as discussed earlier (see supra, para. [90]), this civil jurisdiction does not extend to actions for declaratory judgments (art. 142 C.C.P.), nor to the recognition of a right of ownership in immovable property or a dismemberment of such right, which matter falls under the jurisdiction of the Superior Court, as this Court ruled in Gignac c. Marcotte.[262] Nor does it cover family law matters (except adoption and youth protection), class actions, bankruptcy or certain other matters. Moreover, the Court of Québec cannot grant injunctive relief, which is the exclusive prerogative of the Superior Court.[263] Thus, its “broad” civil jurisdiction, if we may say,[264] is exercised within certain legislative and constitutional limits and constraints.

[166]     In the present case, the Court must determine whether, by granting the Court of Québec civil jurisdiction for claims of less than $85,000, save for certain matters, including those just discussed, the legislature is infringing on the core jurisdiction of the Quebec Superior Court to decide civil disputes. Based on the factors and indicia outlined above, we must conclude that the first paragraph of article 35 C.C.P. is indeed inconsistent, in part, with section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867, because it removes some of the Superior Court’s core jurisdiction.

[167]     First, the 1867 amount of $100, updated using the nominal GDP per capita, falls under the $85,000 threshold, regardless of which expert report is retained.[265] The Court is also of the opinion that, for purposes of the analysis, the methods relying on the consumer price index (CPI), interest rates and nominal wages must be set aside.

[168]     Both experts agreed that the CPI is not an appropriate method;[266] there is no need to comment further thereupon.

[169]     With respect to the use of interest rates, the Court is of the view, as Mr. Geloso explained in his expert report, that it is not appropriate for purposes of updating an amount that represents the value of a dispute, because it seeks to determine the value of a hypothetical investment project[267] and, as such, does not provide any indication of what constitutes a significant amount for a litigant.[268] Moreover, the results obtained from such updating on the basis of the average yield of Government of Canada negotiable bonds, the interest rate applied by chartered banks and the 5% interest rate stipulated in the federal Interest Act[269] are, respectively, $230,500,[270] $85,400[271] and $150,798.[272] We must conclude, seeing the extreme variability of the results obtained and the relatively random nature of the starting hypothesis (that is, the investment project), that this method is of no assistance.

[170]     As for the nominal wage method, it establishes the average wages payable, in Canada as a whole, in 1867 and in 2017, in the manufacturing industry and in the field of education,[273] and sets the current monetary limit on the jurisdiction of the Court of Québec by reference to the ratio, in 1867, between those average wages and the upper limit of the jurisdiction of the inferior courts at that time. Thus, for the manufacturing industry, Mr. St-Maurice, in his expert report, obtained a result of $59,152,[274] while, in the field of education, he arrived at a result of $70,645.[275] At first sight, these are promising results. However, as Mr. Geloso noted in his expert report, wages are but one component of total household income; they do not take into consideration household assets and they are not a stable variable.[276] Mr. St-Maurice himself acknowledged the deficiencies or pitfalls of this method,[277] stating that the exercise here is an attempt to [translation] "compare two completely different worlds, the one that existed in 1867 and the one that existed in 2017".[278] Ultimately, the Court believes the best method is indeed that of the nominal GDP.[279]

[171]     Starting with the 1867 amount of $100 and applying the nominal GDP method for the year 2017, Mr. Yves St-Maurice, the Attorney General of Quebec’s expert, obtained an updated amount of $66,008 for Canada and $60,790 for Quebec.[280] For the same year, the expert of the chief justices of the Superior Court, Mr. Vincent Geloso, obtained an amount of $63,698 for Canada and $55,354 for Quebec.[281] Given that updating the amount provides but one indicator for identifying the critical threshold and that, for the reasons set out above, surpassing that amount is not necessarily fatal to constitutional validity, the Court is of the view there is no need to decide between the two expert reports, not to mention that there is only a relatively minor difference between the various amounts.

[185]     Access to justice is still one of the major challenges the Canadian judicial system faces. However, the pursuit of such an objective, as fundamental and significant as it may be — and that it certainly is — cannot serve to prevent the Quebec Superior Court from exercising the core jurisdiction conferred on it by the Canadian Constitution or justify the disregard of s. 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867. Moreover, none of the parties sought to provide evidence establishing that increasing the threshold of the Court of Québec’s civil jurisdiction to $85,000 facilitates access to justice.

[186]    

The Attorney General of Québec’s principal argument in this regard is to point out that there are few civil cases in the $70,000 to $85,000 range, such that the effect of the most recent amendment on the jurisdiction of the Quebec Superior Court is minimal. Such logic will inevitably lead to the Superior Court gradually being stripped of a significant component of its mission in civil matters. With the number of cases decreasing as the monetary ceiling increases, the core jurisdiction of this section 96 court would be eroded. The following scale clearly illustrates the exponential nature of recent increases in the Court of Québec’s civil jurisdiction: 

 

[187]     It is true that the criterion for delineating the respective jurisdictions of the Superior Court and the Court of Québec is qualitative rather than quantitative. Indeed, knowing how many cases the Superior Court hears compared with the Court of Québec is not, in and of itself, decisive. Instead, it is important to ensure that the jurisdiction of the Court of Québec does not encroach on the core jurisdiction of the Superior Court to adjudicate substantial civil disputes. That said, the most recent amendment, increasing the civil jurisdiction of the Court of Québec to $85,000, cannot be characterized as a catch-up measure. Rather, it is the extension of an ever-growing erosion of the constitutional jurisdiction of the Quebec Superior Court over civil matters.

[188]     While it is certainly the responsibility of the legislature to set a ceiling for the jurisdiction of the Court of Québec in civil matters, it must do so in compliance with the Constitution, particularly section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867. The Court must answer the first reference question as asked and take into consideration, inter alia, the following factors:

(1) the updated value of an amount of $100, which was a “substantial” [306] amount in 1867 and which, when updated to 2017, is equal to an approximate amount ranging from $50,000 to $61,000;

(2) the threshold of $60,000 established by the Quebec legislature for appeals as of right; and

(3) the legislative background regarding the determination of the $85,000 ceiling.

Accordingly, the Court concludes that article 35 C.C.P. violates section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867 because it infringes on the core jurisdiction of the Quebec Superior Court to adjudicate certain substantial civil disputes. Based on the evidence in the record and the applicable principles, in order to respect section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867, the maximum limit for the civil jurisdiction of the Court of Québec must fall between $55,000 and $70,000, but must not exceed the latter amount, subject, of course, to future updates.[307]

4.   THE SECOND REFERENCE QUESTION

4.1. Preliminary Comments

[189]     It should be noted that the second reference question differs from the question initially formulated by the chief justices of the Superior Court in their application for a declaratory judgment filed in the Superior Court on July 19, 2017.[308] For ease of reference, the following is the wording of the question:

[translation provided by applicant’s counsel]

2.  Is it compatible with section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867 to apply the obligation of judicial deference, which characterizes the application for judicial review, to the appeals to the Court of Québec provided for in sections 147 of the Act respecting access to documents held by public bodies and the protection of personal information (chapter A-2.1), 115.16 of the Act respecting the Autorité des marchés financiers (chapter A-33.2), 100 of the Real Estate Brokerage Act (chapter C-73.2), 379 of the Act respecting the distribution of financial products and services (chapter D-9.2), 159 of the Act respecting administrative justice (chapter J-3), 240 and 241 of the Police Act (chapter P-13.1), 91 of the Act respecting the Régie du logement (chapter R-8.1) and 61 of the Act respecting the protection of personal information in the private sector (chapter P-39.1)?

[190]     It also differs from the wording of the first question in the Order in Council: while that question addresses the constitutionality of the provisions of one statute – namely, [translation] “the provisions of the first paragraph of article 35 of the Code of Civil Procedure (chapter C-25.01)” – the second question does not address the constitutionality of a legislative or regulatory provision, but rather that of a jurisprudentially developed standard of review. The issue is whether the application of the deferential requirement characteristic of judicial reviews to appeals to the Court of Québec (provided for in the various provincial statutes in question) is compatible with section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867.

[191]     In other words, in contrast to the first question, which pertains directly to the constitutionality of a legislative provision, the second question bears on the application of the duty of deference, which is not explicitly set out in any of the statutes in question. At most, as will be discussed below, some of these statutes mention the appellate power of the Court of Québec over “questions of law or jurisdiction / questions de droit ou de compétence”. Moreover, the wording of the second question appears to be based on an implicit premise that the Court can comment on the constitutionality of a standard – namely, the application of the judicial deference requirement to appeals to the Court of Québec provided for in the various statutes in question – which is jurisprudential, rather than legislative, in origin.[309]

[192]     That being said, section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867 is the foundation for the second question, just as it was for the constitutional challenge initiated by the chief justices of the Superior Court on the first question.

[193]     While the text of section 96 may seem to confer a mere power of judicial appointment, the jurisprudence is clear that this provision prevents the provincial legislatures from granting to a body or tribunal whose members it appoints powers “conformable or analogous to jurisdiction or powers exercised and exercisable by Courts which are within s. 96”,[310] unless those powers are not judicial or are ancillary to an administrative function.[311]

[194]     The issue, therefore, is whether the statutory grant of appellate powers to the Court of Québec, combined with the jurisprudential requirement of a deferential standard of review, violates that precept. The question requires a consideration of the two main mechanisms for challenging decisions in our justice system, appeals and judicial reviews, the latter being a feature of the core and inherent jurisdiction of superior courts that is constitutionally protected by section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867.

[195]     In other words, we must determine whether the standard of review applicable to appeals to the Court of Québec permitted by the provincial statutes identified in the second reference question is such that the Court of Québec is usurping the superintending and reforming power vested in the superior courts and protected under section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867.

4.2. The interveners’ positions

[196]     The chief justices of the Superior Court readily acknowledge that the Court of Québec can validly exercise the appellate function conferred by the provincial statutes identified in the second reference question, insofar as they are genuine appeals.[312] Their main assertion is that it is contrary to section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867 to grant an “inferior” tribunal, such as the Court of Québec, a superintending and reforming power equivalent to that of the Superior Court. They argue that when the Court of Québec sits in appeal of administrative decisions, it applies a standard modelled on that of judicial reviews, which usurps the superintending and reforming power reserved to the Superior Court and, in practice, thereby limits that power through duplication. The chief justices come to this conclusion, among other means, by performing the three-step test initially developed in Re Residential Tenancies Act, 1979.[313] They therefore ask the Court to [translation] “reframe” the role of the Court of Québec when it sits in appeal from administrative decisions by requiring it to apply the standards specific to appeals, rather than the standards applicable to judicial reviews, so as to respect one of the core, if not defining, powers of the Superior Court, while complying with the intent of the legislature having a created a right of appeal.

[197]     For their part, the Attorney General of Quebec, the Attorney General of Canada, the Conférence des juges de la Cour du Québec and the Conseil de la magistrature du Québec are all of the opinion that the deferential requirement applied by the Court of Québec does not infringe section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867, because the appellate jurisdiction of the Court of Québec and the interpretation of its scope by the courts does not affect the Superior Court’s superintending and reforming power.

[198]     The Attorney General of Quebec explains that it has often argued that the correctness standard should apply when the Court of Québec considers a question of law on appeal, particularly because it should act within the administrative system, at the end of a specialized process, by making a specific contribution to the implementation of the legislative scheme in question. The Attorney General of Quebec therefore acknowledges that the judicial deference the Court of Québec applies could be at odds with the traditional concept of an appeal. In her view, however, the situation involves [translation] “an administrative law issue” rather than a constitutional one.

[199]     Relying on Farrah[314] and Crevier[315], counsel for the Attorney General explains that section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867 does not in any way prevent a provincial legislature from granting appellate jurisdiction to an inferior court, provided the power of judicial review of the superior courts is preserved, which is the case for all the legislative schemes identified by the chief justices of the Superior Court. She adds that the choice of the standard of review at the stage of appeals to the Court of Québec is irrelevant for purposes of determining the validity of the appeals — and the corresponding provisions — but, instead, raises a question regarding the legislature’s intent with respect to each of the schemes in question. According to the Attorney General of Quebec, if, in seeking that intent, the resulting interpretation calls into question the constitutionality of the provisions conferring a right of appeal, the courts must reconsider that interpretation to ensure it is consistent with section 96, rather than declaring the provisions in question to be unconstitutional.

[200]     As for the Attorney General of Canada, it is of the opinion that [translation] “the Court of Québec exercises its appellate jurisdiction as if it were carrying out a judicial review of the specialized administrative tribunal”, but insists on the fact that [translation] “the Quebec Superior Court nevertheless retains its superintending and reforming power over decisions of the Court of Québec” (paras. 77-78 of her brief), which confirms the validity of the impugned legislative schemes. In other words, like the Attorney General of Quebec, counsel argues that only the removal by the provincial legislature of the Superior Court’s superintending and reforming power over decisions of the Court of Québec would infringe section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867, because, in such a case, the latter would have final decision-making power, immune from the supervisory power of the Superior Court that is protected under that section.

[201]     Thus, the Attorney General of Canada is of the view that granting appellate jurisdiction to the Court of Québec on questions of law or jurisdiction is not, in and of itself, problematic insofar as the Superior Court retains its superintending and reforming power with regard to those questions. She also bases her reasoning on the aforementioned Supreme Court judgments in Farrah and Crevier, as well as on the decision in Grondin.[316] The Attorney General of Canada states the following in paragraph 94 of its brief:

[translation]

94. Grondin therefore establishes that granting the Court of Québec an appellate jurisdiction is not contrary to section 96 insofar as the decisions of that Court are subject to the superintending and reforming power of the Superior Court.

[202]     In short, the application of judicial review standards to appeals heard by the Court of Québec does not violate section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867. If not, counsel says, the judgments of the Supreme Court would not lean in that direction.

[203]     The Conférence des juges de la Cour du Québec argues that the appellate jurisdiction of the Court of Québec does not in any way bring section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867 into play. It insists on the fact that the application of standards equivalent to those of judicial review stems, not from a legislative choice, but from the jurisprudence of this Court and the Supreme Court, neither of which would have imposed an approach inconsistent with section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867. In its view, the interpretation of the scope of a statutory right of appeal does not transform the appeal into a judicial review and does not in any way exclude the Superior Court’s role over such matters, because the Superior Court fully exercises its superintending and reforming power when judgments of the Court of Québec are brought before it. These two successive steps — an appeal to the Court of Québec, followed by judicial review — do not dilute the Superior Court’s inherent power, and while they may be cumbersome, they do not call into question the constitutionality of the appellate power that the legislature has jurisdiction to grant to the Court of Québec.

[204]     The Conseil de la magistrature du Québec also argues that the standards of review established by the Supreme Court do not infringe section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867. In paragraph 100 of its brief, it states: [translation] “It is not the standard of review of a court sitting in appeal that gives it its jurisdiction; the standard merely states the criteria for analysis that the court must apply”.

[205]     By emphasizing the difference between an appeal (a statutory right that focuses on the appropriateness of the impugned judgment) and judicial review (an inherent power that focuses, instead, on the legality of the judgment), the Conseil de la magistrature du Québec argues that the Court of Québec sitting in appeal does not usurp the superintending and reforming power of the Superior Court, which still retains that power over decisions of the Court of Québec. It adds that the legislature’s intent has always been to grant the Court of Québec a genuine appellate jurisdiction, not a power of judicial review.

[206]     The Attorney General of British Columbia did not take a position on this question.

[207]     The Organisme d’autoréglementation du courtage immobilier du Québec intervened solely to raise the possible consequences, within its sphere of activity, of a judgment declaring the Court of Québec’s appellate jurisdiction invalid. It referred principally to the increase in waiting times and the impact this would have on access to justice and its mission of protecting the public.

[208]     It is also worth noting that, as the Supreme Court has explained, the fact that the Attorney General of Quebec and the Attorney General of Canada both argue that the Court of Québec’s application of administrative law standards of review does not render its appellate jurisdiction unconstitutional is not decisive in and of itself for purposes of opining on the question. The Supreme Court nevertheless indicated that this would be a factor to consider,[317] in light of the fact that sections 96 and 92(14) of the Constitution Act, 1867 represent a compromise involving the administration of justice and the division of powers.[318]

4.3. The statutory appeals in question

[209]     The Court of Québec has been granted a statutory appellate power over various administrative decisions. In 2007, it established an Administrative and Appeal Division within its Civil Division in order to manage, hear and decide all such appellate proceedings entrusted to it.[319]

[210]     Within the framework of this reference, the chief justices of the Superior Court challenge eight legislative schemes that provide for such appeals to the Court of Québec, on the ground that they are contrary to section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867. Given that the issue raised by the second reference question concerns the effect of the standard of review on the constitutionality of these provisions rather than the constitutionality of the provisions themselves, collectively or individually, the parties to the reference did not devote a great deal of time discussing each of these schemes individually, arguing that, as a whole, they either are, or are not, constitutional.

[211]     Nevertheless, we will briefly review the scheme established by each of these statutes, whose relevant provisions can be found in the First Schedule to this opinion.

4.3.1.   Act respecting access to documents held by public bodies and the protection of personal information

[212]     As its title suggests, the Act respecting access to documents held by public bodies and the protection of personal information[320] regulates access to documents held by public bodies while ensuring the protection of personal information. This statute creates the Commission d’accès à l’information, which consists of an oversight division and an adjudicative division (s. 103).

[213]     The statute also excludes the judicial review of the decisions of the Commission, “[e]xcept on a question of jurisdiction / [s]auf sur une question de compétence” (s. 114 para. 1), and provides that two judges of the Court of Appeal may summarily annul any decision of the Superior Court made contrary to that provision in relation to a document (s. 114 para. 2). Moreover, the statute provides that final decisions of the Commission may be appealed to the Court of Québec on questions of law or jurisdiction (s. 147) (as may its interlocutory decisions, with leave). This appeal, which suspends the execution of the Commission’s decision (s. 150), is governed by the articles of the Code of Civil Procedure applicable to appeals (s. 152). No appeal lies from the decision of the Court of Québec (s. 154), but this does not exclude the Superior Court’s exercise of its superintending and reforming power.

4.3.2.   Act respecting the regulation of the financial sector

[214]     The Act respecting the regulation of the financial sector (formerly the Act respecting the Autorité des marchés financiers)[321] creates the Autorité des marchés financiers to ensure the protection of consumers of financial products and services and ensure compliance with the standards and requirements imposed on the various financial sector participants (s. 4). The statute also creates the Financial Markets Administrative Tribunal to make determinations with respect to matters involving the application of laws pertaining to the financial sector (s. 93).

[215]     It also states that judicial review of the Tribunal’s decisions is limited to questions of jurisdiction (s. 115.15.63 para. 1) and that a judge of the Court of Appeal can summarily annul any decision made contrary to that provision (s. 115.15.63 para. 2). Final decisions of the Tribunal, however, can be appealed to the Court of Québec (s. 115.16). Such an appeal, which is governed by the provisions of the Code of Civil Procedure applicable to appeals (s. 115.19), does not suspend the execution of the contested decision, unless otherwise decided (s. 115.21). The statute does not contain a privative clause regarding the judgments of the Court of Québec, and such judgments may also be appealed to the Court of Appeal, with leave (s. 115.22).

4.3.3.   Real Estate Brokerage Act

[216]     The Real Estate Brokerage Act[322] governs real estate brokerage and mortgage brokerage. It establishes the Organisme d’autoréglementation du courtage immobilier du Québec (s. 31), the mission of which is to protect the public in that field (s. 32). A discipline committee is appointed within the Organisme d’autoréglementation du courtage immobilier du Québec; the discipline committee may be seized of any complaint regarding an offence under the statute (s. 93).

[217]     The statute provides for an appeal to the Court of Québec from any decision of the committee (s. 100),[323] which appeal is governed by the provisions of the Professional Code (s. 100). The Court can confirm, alter or quash any decision submitted to it and render the decision which it considers should have been rendered in first instance (s. 175 of the Professional Code[324]). In principle, this appeal suspends the execution of the committee’s decision (s. 101). The statute does not contain a privative clause regarding the decisions of the Court of Québec.[325]

4.3.4.   Act respecting the distribution of financial products and services

[218]     As its title indicates, the Act respecting the distribution of financial products and services[326] regulates the practice of representatives who distribute financial products and services. The statute establishes a discipline committee (ss. 352-353), which hears any complaint made against a representative under the statute, the Securities Act[327] or any regulation under either of those two statutes.

[219]     The statute also provides for an appeal to the Court of Québec from any decision of the committee (except decisions regarding a penalty: in such a case, an appeal is possible only if a penalty has been imposed) (s. 379 paras. 1 and 2), which appeal is governed by the rules under sections 115.16 to 115.22 of the Act respecting the regulation of the financial sector (see supra, para. [215]) (s. 379 para. 3). There is no privative clause limiting the Superior Court’s supervisory power over decisions of the committee or judgments of the Court of Québec (which judgments may be appealed to the Court of Appeal, with leave), and the statute even specifies that subparagraph 4 of the first paragraph of article 529 C.C.P. applies to the forfeiture of office contemplated in its section 373.

4.3.5.   Act respecting administrative justice

[220]     The purpose of the Act respecting administrative justice[328] is to affirm the specific character of administrative justice, to ensure its quality, promptness and accessibility and to safeguard the fundamental rights of citizens (s. 1 para. 1). It establishes the rules of procedure applicable to decisions made in respect of a citizen (s. 1 para. 2) and institutes the Administrative Tribunal of Québec (ss. 1 para. 3 and 14 para. 1), whose function is to make determinations in respect of proceedings brought against an administrative authority or a decentralized authority (s. 14 para. 2).

[221]     The statute also includes a privative clause that insulates decisions of the Tribunal other than decisions on questions of “jurisdiction / compétence” (s. 158 para. 1), and it further provides that a judge of the Court of Appeal can summarily annul any decision made contrary to that provision (s. 158 para. 2). Decisions rendered by the Tribunal’s immovable property division and those rendered in matters concerning the preservation of agricultural land may be appealed to the Court of Québec, with leave of a judge of that court (s. 159), which appeal does not suspend the execution of the Tribunal’s decision, unless a judge of the Court of Québec decides otherwise (s. 162). No appeal lies from the decision of the Court of Québec (s. 164), but this does not exclude the Superior Court’s exercise of its superintending and reforming power.

4.3.6.   Police Act

[222]     The Police Act[329] regulates police officers. It establishes the Comité de déontologie policière which, in particular, has jurisdiction to hear and dispose of any citation in matters of police ethics (s. 194).

[223]     Moreover, it limits the judicial review of decisions of the committee to decisions on questions of jurisdiction only (s. 193), while providing that final decisions of that committee can be appealed to the Court of Québec (s. 241). This appeal suspends the execution of the decision of the committee (s. 246). The Court of Québec has all the necessary powers for the exercise of its jurisdiction (s. 250) and may even summarily dismiss an appeal it deems improper or dilatory (s. 247). It can render any decision which it considers should have been rendered in first instance (s. 252); that decision is without appeal (s. 253), although this does not exclude the Superior Court’s exercise of its superintending and reforming power.

4.3.7.   Act respecting the Régie du logement

[224]     The Act respecting the Régie du logement[330] establishes the Régie du logement (s. 4), whose mission is to inform landlords and tenants about the rights and obligations resulting from a residential lease, promote conciliation between landlords and tenants and decide applications submitted to it regarding residential leases (s. 5).

[225]     The statute provides that decisions of the Régie are not subject to judicial review (s. 18 para. 1), and it further provides that a judge of the Court of Appeal can summarily annul any decision made contrary to that provision (s. 18 para. 2). With some exceptions, decisions of the Régie may, however, be appealed to the Court of Québec, with leave (s. 91). In principle, this appeal suspends the execution of the Régie’s decision (s. 94). The Court of Québec can render the decision on the merits which it considers should have been rendered (s. 101). It can even summarily dismiss an appeal it considers improper or dilatory (ss. 98 and 63.2) and, in such a case, condemn the appellant to pay damages (s. 106). No appeal lies from the judgment of the Court of Québec (s. 102), but this does not exclude the Superior Court’s exercise of its superintending and reforming power.

4.3.8.   Act respecting the protection of personal information in the private sector

[226]     Lastly, the purpose of the Act respecting the protection of personal information in the private sector[331] is to establish particular rules with respect to personal information relating to other persons which a person collects, holds, uses or communicates to third persons in the course of carrying on an enterprise (s. 1). It provides that the Commission d’accès à l’information, created under the Act respecting access to documents held by public bodies and the protection of personal information (see supra, para. [212]), examines disagreements regarding personal information (s. 42).

[227]     The statute also provides that a decision of the Commission on a question of fact coming under its jurisdiction is final and no appeal lies therefrom (s. 59). Moreover, final decisions of the Commission may be appealed to the Court of Québec on a question of law or jurisdiction (as may certain interlocutory decisions) (s. 61). This appeal, which, in principle, suspends the execution of the Commission’s decision (s. 64), is governed by the rules of the Code of Civil Procedure applicable to appeals (but the parties need not file statements) (s. 67). The decision of the Court of Québec is without appeal (s. 69), but this does not exclude the Superior Court’s exercise of its superintending and reforming power.

[228]     At this stage, it should be noted that three of these statutes do not impose any limit on the judicial review of administrative decisions (the Real Estate Brokerage Act, the Act respecting the distribution of financial products and services and the Act respecting the protection of personal information in the private sector), while four of them provide for the judicial review of administrative decisions on questions of jurisdiction only (the Act respecting access to documents held by public bodies and the protection of personal information, the Act respecting the regulation of the financial sector, the Act respecting administrative justice and the Police Act). One statute provides that no judicial review may be exercised against administrative decisions (the Act respecting the Régie du logement).

[229]     It is important to note, however, that none of these statutes excludes judicial review by the Superior Court of the decisions of the Court of Québec sitting in appeal of the administrative decisions.

[230]     We will return to this point later.

4.4. Characterization of the Court of Québec, sitting in appeal, as a court of justice

[231]     The wording and the conditions for the exercise of the right of appeal to the Court of Québec, as set out in the various statutes in question, vary from one to the other.[332] From a reading of the statutes, one cannot distill a single legislative intent. Each scheme seems to have been tailored to a specific sector of activity.

[232]     The section entitled Règles de fonctionnement de la Division administrative et d’appel [Operating rules of the Administrative and Appeal Division] of the official website of the Court of Québec states that this Division relies on some thirty [translation] “specialized” judges.[333] According to the Chief Justice at the time, the establishment of the Administrative and Appeal Division sought to foster [translation] “the quality, consistency, efficiency and speed” of the Court of Québec in dealing with these matters.[334]

[233]     The scope of the notion of “specialization” must be clarified in light of the specialized vocation of administrative decision-makers, which, as will be seen, is the primary rationale for the duty of deference. When the Court of Québec sits in appeal pursuant to the various statutes in question, is it truly a specialized administrative tribunal, or is it more accurate to say that it is a court of justice, which is usually characterized as a “generalist” court?[335]

[234]     This requires a characterization of the Court of Québec.

[235]     The characterization of the Court of Québec, when it exercises its appellate functions (which, for purposes of its internal organization, it does within an “Administrative and Appeal Division”), is paramount for purposes of answering the question before us.

[236]     In Inglis, rendered in 1958 — well before the development of modern-day administrative law and the various standards of review — Rand, J., speaking for a unanimous Supreme Court, explained that section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867 does not in any way prevent a provincial legislature from creating an administrative appellate tribunal and appointing its members:

[…] The Commissioner, by the terms of the statute, is not strictly an appeal Court; his function in appeal is essentially the same as that of the recorder, but on a review level; and its purpose is obviously to furnish the confirmation of a superior and here a possibly more independent functionary. That confirmation lies behind the appeal to the Court of Appeal, the precise nature or scope of which may call for some consideration. Since the Province can create and appoint justices of inferior Courts, there is no reason in the nature of things why it cannot establish an inferior Court of review or appeal; it is the subject-matter rather than the apparatus of adjudication that is determinative. […][336]

[Emphasis added]

[237]     Twenty years later, in Farrah, Laskin, C.J., in concurring reasons, referred to the principles established in Inglis regarding the possibility for a province to create an administrative appellate tribunal.[337]

[238]     Subsequently, in 2011, in Parizeau (leave to appeal to the Supreme Court refused), this Court confirmed that the Professions Tribunal is indeed an appellate body.[338] Although its members are appointed by the province, its constitutionality was not questioned, given its status as a [translation] “specialized administrative tribunal”.[339] Regarding judicial deference, the Court stated the following:

[translation]

[75]      That being said, while these pronouncements [regarding judicial deference] apply when the legislature provides for a right to appeal the decisions of a specialized administrative tribunal to a generalist court of justice – and that is, in fact, what all these Supreme Court judgments are concerned with – do they also apply when the appeal is assigned to another, also specialized, administrative body? Indeed, in the first case, the administrative law dynamic finds full expression and the distinction between specialized administrative tribunals, to which the legislature has given the expert task of developing standards in a particular area, and the generalist courts of justice, the guardians of the rule of law, as pointed out earlier (see supra, para. [69]), dictates that the matter be dealt with according to the rules of judicial review. Can this dynamic and the resulting requirement, however, come into play in the second case, when the appellate body is also an administrative one? The reasons that justify the deference shown by the courts of justice to specialized administrative tribunals, reasons that flow from the organization and the respective role of the executive and judicial branches of government and respect for legislative intent, are hardly persuasive when the appellate body is also an administrative tribunal and it, too, has a specialized mandate. The case at bar is a good illustration of this.

[…]

[81]      The Supreme Court and this Court have repeatedly explained the following: the appellate tribunal may in principle rectify any error of law in the decision under appeal or any palpable and overriding error in the determination of the facts or in the application of the law (if it was correctly identified) to the facts. This standard is just as valid for appeals brought before administrative tribunals, and the standard of intervention developed for judicial appeals can certainly be transposed to quasi-judicial appeals, with the limitations and adjustments imposed by the particular legislation applicable to each case and according to the general rules of administrative law.[340]

[Emphasis added]

[239]     Thus, as with the Professions Tribunal, the inclusion of the Court of Québec within the administrative apparatus, when it sits in appeal of administrative decisions, would not only confirm the constitutionality of that appellate level, but also impose on it the standards of appellate intervention, according to the model proposed in Housen,[341] rather than the standards of judicial review established in Dunsmuir[342]. The inclusion of the Court of Québec within administrative schemes has, in fact, been proposed by certain scholars.[343]

[240]     That being said, when the Court of Québec sits in appeal pursuant to the statutes contemplated in the second reference question, is it acting as a specialized administrative tribunal or rather as a court of justice with specific appellate jurisdiction?

[241]     The structure of the Court of Québec and the manner in which it operates when exercising its appellate functions under the aforementioned statutes support what seems to be a consensus among courts having examined the question: it forms part of the judicial apparatus (just as when it performs its other judicial functions). In fact, in Frères Maristes, this Court was clearly against an approach that would characterize the Court of Québec an administrative body when it sits in appeal of administrative decisions:

[translation]

[5]        […] Moreover, the suggestion of an expanded type of administrative appeal is suspect from an institutional point of view, because it does not fit the purpose of administrative law, which is to use administrative tribunals to foster specialized decision-makers, reduce the costs of accessing justice and hasten the decision-making process.[344]

[242]     The traits of an administrative tribunal, besides its specialization, are the fact that it is “less cumbersome”, “less expensive”, “less formal” and “less delayed”,[345] terms that are not generally used to describe the Administrative and Appeal Division of the Civil Division of the Court of Québec, given the extent of its functions and the fact that it operates judicially. But, above all, as discussed earlier,[346] this Division, which was established by the Chief Justice of that court and whose existence is not contemplated in the Courts of Justice Act or in any other statute, is an integral part of the Court of Québec’s Civil Division, which is, itself, contemplated in the Courts of Justice Act. There can be no question of turning it into an independent section of the Civil Division, let alone a separate tribunal comparable to the Professions Tribunal, for example.[347] In fact, in Parizeau, in order to characterize the Professions Tribunal as an administrative tribunal, this Court clearly distinguished it from the Court of Québec, implying that the latter is indeed a court of justice (which, it must be said, seems obvious in light of the Courts of Justice Act):

[translation]

Although judges of the Court of Québec sit on the Professions Tribunal, the latter is not a court of justice or a division of the Court of Québec, nor is it contemplated in the Courts of Justice Act, R.S.Q c. T-16 (see s. 1) or in the Code of Civil Procedure (except through an express reference in the Professional Code), notwithstanding that its chair sits on the Conseil de la magistrature (see s. 248, para. d.1, of the Courts of Justice Act), just as does the president of the Human Rights Tribunal. It is an administrative tribunal.[348]

[243]     There are several elements that support the conclusion that the Court of Québec is part of the judicial system: it is contemplated in the Courts of Justice Act (s. 1); its members who sit on the Administrative and Appeal Division are judges (s. 79 para. 2);[349] and its operating rules are not more flexible or quicker than those of other judicial tribunals.[350] We also know that the Court of Québec is governed by the same rules of procedure as the Superior Court and that its judges have similar privileges related to judicial independence.

[244]     Furthermore, six of the eight legislative schemes in question here explicitly refer to the Courts of Justice Act, which provides that the Court of Québec may, in the manner provided for therein, adopt such regulations as it considers necessary for the exercise of its appellate jurisdiction.[351]

[245]     It should also be noted that, in Association des pompiers de Montréal, our colleague, Morissette, J.A., clearly stated that the Civil Division of the Court of Québec is a court of justice within the strict meaning of the term.[352]

[246]     In addition, the Administrative and Appeal Division of the Court of Québec is not specialized in the sense in which that word is usually understood. Indeed, as it appears from the statutes mentioned in the second reference question, the Court of Québec sits in appeal in a variety of very different fields, ranging from residential leases, to the preservation of agricultural land, to police ethics and to financial markets.[353] This is precisely what our colleague, Chamberland, J.A., stated in Daigneault:

[translation]

[35]      […] The Court of Québec is a statutory court with a highly varied jurisdiction; it has no particular expertise in matters of professional discipline, or in matters of real estate brokerage. […][354]

[Reference omitted]

[247]     Thus, while the Règles de fonctionnement de la Division administrative et d’appel [Operating rules of the Administrative and Appeal Division] found on the official website of the Court of Québec refer to [translation] “specialized” judges, they are specialized in adjudicating disputes rather than in the usually understood sense of the specialization of an administrative decision-maker who, for example, hears only matters of professional law (such as the Professions Tribunal) or human rights (such as the Human Rights Tribunal)[355]. On this point, Professor Denis Lemieux wrote the following:

[translation]

Insofar as certain judges will sit primarily or solely within the [Administrative and Appeal] Division, they will gain certain expertise with respect to the statutes conferring appellate jurisdiction on the Court. However, the heterogeneity of the various areas of jurisdiction granted will not necessarily require greater deference from the higher courts. […][356]

[248]     Lastly, the argument stating that appeals to the Court of Québec are redundant and add a cumbersome element to administrative justice does not, in and of itself, render them invalid, insofar as the grant of power is constitutional and reflects the intent of the legislature.

[249]     The multiplicity of remedies, however, does present drawbacks,[357] and some would say that this is a strong argument against including the Court of Québec in the administrative apparatus when it sits in appeal of administrative decisions. It might be said that the “cumbersome” element is at odds with the very essence of administrative justice which, by its nature, should be efficient, in addition to being accessible quickly and at a lesser cost. This is the crux of this Court’s explanation in Frères Maristes, cited above.

[250]     All in all, it is clear that the Administrative and Appeal Division is not an entity separate from the Court of Québec, and that the Court of Québec, when it exercises its appellate functions, is a court of justice and not an administrative tribunal.

4.5. The standard of review applicable to appeals to the Court of Québec

[251]     The chief justices of the Superior Court argue that the standards that should apply to appeals under the statutes mentioned in the second reference question are the appellate standards proposed in Housen,[358] rather than the standards of judicial review defined in Dunsmuir.[359] In their opinion, to conclude otherwise would distort the legislature’s intent and usurp the Superior Court’s superintending and reforming power.

[252]     It is not possible to accept this argument, given the prior jurisprudence of this Court and the jurisprudence of the Supreme Court, particularly Proprio Direct, which is binding on this Court in keeping with the principle of stare decisis and which imposes on appeals before the Court of Québec — a court of justice — a standard akin to that of judicial review of administrative decisions.

[253]     As one author has noted, [translation] “the expertise of administrative decision-makers is a key concept that justifies a deferential approach by the courts of justice towards those within the administrative apparatus.[360] This sentence summarizes the reasoning of this Court and the Supreme Court in shaping the standards of review applicable to judicial tribunals when they sit in appeal of administrative decisions.

[254]     From an era in which courts of justice routinely intervened in the decisions of administrative tribunals to one in which they sought out a standard of review by applying the “pragmatic and functional test” in Bibeault,[361] and eventually to one in which they adopted a deferential approach to administrative decisions,[362] “understanding of the role of expertise in the modern administrative state” is central to the ever evolving relationship between judicial and administrative tribunals.[363]

[255]     As of 1989, in Bell Canada, the Supreme Court unanimously explained that even if the jurisdiction of a court on appeal is much broader than the jurisdiction of a court on judicial review, the specialization of administrative decision-makers commands a degree of restraint by the courts when dealing with decisions that fall within the expertise of those decision-makers. Gonthier, J. wrote:

[…] within the context of a statutory appeal from an administrative tribunal, additional consideration must be given to the principle of specialization of duties.[364]

[256]     Although both proceedings require deference, Gonthier, J. was careful to differentiate appeals, stricto sensu, from judicial review.

[257]     A few years later, in Pezim, Iacobucci, J., speaking for a unanimous Supreme Court, cited the remarks of Gonthier, J. in Bell Canada, adding:

Consequently, even where there is no privative clause and where there is a statutory right of appeal, the concept of the specialization of duties requires that deference be shown to decisions of specialized tribunals on matters which fall squarely within the tribunal’s expertise. […][365] 

[258]     Faced with this requirement of deference, in Southam, the Supreme Court, in a unanimous judgment written, once again, by Iacobucci. J., created a third standard of review — that of reasonableness simpliciter — because, as he explained, the appeal of a decision of an administrative tribunal is not like the appeal of a decision of a trial court:

[…] But on the other hand, appeal from a decision of an expert tribunal is not exactly like appeal from a decision of a trial court. Presumably if Parliament entrusts a certain matter to a tribunal and not (initially at least) to the courts, it is because the tribunal enjoys some advantage that judges do not. For that reason alone, review of the decision of a tribunal should often be on a standard more deferential than correctness. Accordingly, a third standard is needed.[366]

[259]     The creation of the reasonableness simpliciter standard therefore introduced an alternative to the patent unreasonableness standard — the Supreme Court viewing the latter as relating to questions of jurisdiction, and therefore judicial review — in order to reflect the reality of appeals of administrative decisions. By creating another standard based on “reasonableness”, the Supreme Court sought to ensure deference to the expertise of administrative decision-makers in such proceedings.

[260]     As we know, it is therefore the standard of reasonableness simpliciter that was subsequently identified as the “proper standard” for appeals of administrative decisions to the courts of justice.

[261]     In 2003, in Ryan, the Supreme Court began its unanimous judgment by reiterating that a court of justice reviewing the decision of an administrative tribunal must first identify the degree of deference required with respect to that decision, whether the review is carried out by way of an application for judicial review or an appeal.[367] This explanation followed in the spirit of the aforementioned judgments in Bell Canada, Pezim and Southam, where, as previously mentioned, the expertise of the administrative decision-makers was a key consideration. In Ryan, Iacobucci, J., speaking for the Court, pointed out that “[t]he specialization of duties intended by the legislature may warrant deference notwithstanding the absence of a privative clause”.[368] Concluding that the applicable standard was that of reasonableness simpliciter, the Court explained that it is essentially the content of the question the court must ask when reviewing an administrative decision that determines the applicable standard of review.[369]

[262]     In Dr. Q, a case in which the reasonableness simpliciter standard was also applied,[370] McLachlin, C.J., speaking for the Court, explicitly set aside the assumption that the existence of a right to appeal — as opposed to judicial review — implies that a matter can be settled without relying on the administrative law principles pertaining to the standard of review.[371] The Chief Justice wrote:

[34]      […] The term “judicial review” embraces review of administrative decisions by way of both application for judicial review and statutory rights of appeal. In every case where a statute delegates power to an administrative decision-maker, the reviewing judge must begin by determining the standard of review on the pragmatic and functional approach. […][372]

[263]     It is true that until the decision in Dr. Q, when the Supreme Court developed its jurisprudence on the basis of such appeals, it did so in the context of courts contemplated in section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867 sitting in judicial review.[373] This is due to the fact that the Court of Québec, an “inferior” court that hears appeals from decisions of certain administrative tribunals, has no counterpart elsewhere in Canada. Nonetheless, the reasoning is based on the fundamental difference between “specialized administrative tribunals” and “generalist courts of justice” and, by the same token, on the importance of the notion of expertise in Canadian administrative law.

[264]     Therefore, the suggestion of the chief justices of the Superior Court to concentrate instead on the difference between “superior courts of justice” and “inferior courts of justice” is inconsistent with developments in the Supreme Court’s jurisprudence and its underlying logic.

[265]     The foregoing decisions were followed by those of this Court in Pigeon c. Daigneault[374] and Pigeon c. Proprio Direct inc.[375]

[266]     In Daigneault, Chamberland, J.A. cited Ryan and Dr. Q and concluded as follows regarding the right to appeal to the Court of Québec from a decision of the discipline committee instituted under the Real Estate Brokerage Act in force at the time:

[translation]

[34]      […] A court called upon to review such an administrative decision, be it by way of application for judicial review, as in the case at bar, or by way of a statutory right to appeal, must first determine the applicable standard of review by applying the pragmatic and functional test […][376]

[267]     Slightly further on, he added that [translation] “[t]he Court of Québec is a statutory court with a highly varied jurisdiction; it has no particular expertise in matters of professional discipline, or in matters of real estate brokerage”.[377] Thus, it was the expertise of the administrative decision-makers versus that of the Court of Québec, an “inferior”, but generalist, court of justice, that once again justified the choice of the reasonableness simpliciter standard.[378] In concurring reasons, Fish, J.A., as he then was, also cited Dr. Q and Ryan and, acknowledging that the latter judgment was rendered within a different context, he stated: “Though Ryan was concerned with a direct appeal to the Court of Appeal, I shall assume that the same standard applies on an appeal from the Disciplinary Committee to the Court of Quebec”.[379]

[268]     In Proprio Direct (CA), rendered a short time after Daigneault, Dalphond, J.A. also identified reasonableness simpliciter as the standard applicable to appeals to the Court of Québec from decisions of a discipline committee.[380] Dalphond, J.A. cited Pezim, Southam and Ryan and stated that [translation] “one must not confuse an ordinary civil appeal, such as under the Code of Civil Procedure, with the judicial review mechanism for decisions of a specialized decision-maker through appeals [to the Court of Québec]”.[381]

[269]     In that case, Dalphond, J.A. also insisted on the importance to be given to the expertise of a specialized administrative body during a review of that body’s decisions by a superior court or a statutory court with an expanded jurisdiction, such as the Court of Québec.[382] This distinction clearly indicates that this Court was well aware of the fact that the Court of Québec is not a superior court, but that this did not prevent it from applying the judgments in Ryan and Dr. Q.

[270]     In 2008, the Supreme Court rendered its decision in Dunsmuir and re-examined the basis for judicial review and for the administrative law standards of review. The Court eliminated the reasonableness simpliciter standard (or merged it with the patent unreasonableness standard), bringing the number of standards back to two: reasonableness and correctness. The intermediate standard which applied more often than not to appeals of administrative decisions to courts of justice was replaced with the reasonableness standard, with the correctness standard being reserved solely for certain questions of law and for excess of jurisdiction. In that decision, Bastarache and LeBel, JJ., speaking for the majority, reiterated the importance of deference to administrative decision-makers and reaffirmed the principles already identified in the jurisprudence of their Court:

[49]      […] In short, deference requires respect for the legislative choices to leave some matters in the hands of administrative decision-makers, for the processes and determinations that draw on particular expertise and experiences, and for the different roles of the courts and administrative bodies within the Canadian constitutional system.[383]

[Emphasis added]

[271]     The Supreme Court of Canada subsequently rendered its decision in another case involving Proprio Direct and originating in Quebec. At the outset, Abella, J., speaking for the majority, traced the procedural route of the case and, referring to the appeal exercised as of right to the Court of Québec under the provisions of the Real Estate Brokerage Act (as it existed at the time), she stated that Proprio Direct had sought a “judicial review” of the decisions of a discipline committee.[384] By merging appeals with judicial review in this manner, Abella, J. followed the jurisprudence of the Supreme Court according to which the appeal of an administrative decision to a court of justice must be treated as a variant of judicial review, in accordance with Ryan and Dr. Q.[385]

[272]     In that new Proprio Direct case, the Court of Appeal had reiterated the jurisprudential principles regarding appeals of administrative decisions to a court of justice, ruling that such appeals were [translation] “governed by the rules ordinarily applicable to judicial review”.[386] In contrast to the Court of Québec, however, this Court had concluded, for purposes of deciding one of the issues in dispute, that the applicable standard was correctness rather reasonableness.

[273]     Abella, J. found that the Court of Appeal’s approach did not show sufficient deference to the expertise of the discipline committee and should have followed the methodology of Chamberland and Dalphond, JJ.A. in Daigneault and Proprio Direct (CA), which was more in keeping with Dunsmuir.[387] In doing so, the majority of the Supreme Court confirmed the application to Quebec law, for purposes of an appeal to the Court of Québec, of the principles set out in Ryan and Dr. Q, which Chamberland and Dalphond, JJ.A. had also cited in their respective reasons.

[274]     Deschamps, J. who, with Rothstein, J., dissented, would not have shown any deference towards the discipline committee, because, first, she was of the opinion that the committee did not have particular expertise over the issue in dispute and, second, because the legislature had established a right of appeal to the Court of Québec so that the Court of Québec could render any decision it thought should have been rendered by the committee. She was of the view that the Court of Appeal’s judgments in Daigneault and Proprio Direct (CA) were inapplicable.[388] Deschamps, J. went further in her concurring reasons in Dunsmuir, superimposing the two types of standards of review by focusing her analysis on the nature of the issue in dispute (law, fact, or mixed law and fact).[389]

[275]     Deschamps, J.’s reasons highlight the difficult interaction between the standards of review for appeals and those for judicial review. They also reveal a degree of inconsistency in the intent of the legislature which, on the one hand, has decided to rely on administrative decision-makers specialized in certain matters, while, on the other hand, extending a right to appeal their decisions to a generalist court without particular expertise.

[276]     Shortly after Proprio Direct (SCC), the Court of Appeal rendered its decision in St-Pie, pointing out the [translation] “dangers of the multiple steps and proceedings” that are characteristic of administrative justice.[390] Relying on Proprio Direct (SCC), the Court nevertheless reiterated that the standards applicable to the Court of Québec hearing appeals of administrative decisions are the standards of judicial review.[391]

[277]     In KPMG, Duval Hesler, J.A. (as she then was), questioned which standard of review applies to proceedings in the Court of Québec against an administrative decision, pointing out that the proceeding is indeed an appeal and that it may therefore [translation] “appear precipitous to fully apply […] the principles of judicial review, which require greater deference”, particularly since [translation] “the provincial legislatures cannot grant provincial courts a power of judicial review, any more so than they can withdraw that constitutionally vested power from the superior courts” (this would appear to be an implicit reference to section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867). Citing Proprio Direct (SCC), the Chief Justice nonetheless stated that the Court of Québec’s criteria for intervening are not those of appellate intervention, but that [translation] “it goes without saying that the Court of Québec can also intervene where there is a palpable and overriding error of fact”, thereby superimposing the two types of standards.[392] The reasons of the Chief Justice in KPMG therefore echo the opinions of Deschamps, J. in Dunsmuir and Proprio Direct (SCC).

[278]     Then, in Parizeau, the line between courts of justice and administrative tribunals was clearly drawn. In that case, this Court reiterated the principle holding that courts of justice must show deference towards specialized administrative tribunals,[393] but it also ruled on the jurisprudential controversy surrounding the Professions Tribunal. Although the Professions Tribunal’s decision-makers are judges, this Court was of the opinion that it is an administrative — not a judicial — tribunal, which is also specialized and therefore need not show deference when considering the decisions of “inferior” administrative bodies.

[279]     Given the Supreme Court’s refusal to hear the appeal in Parizeau, there appear to be two jurisprudential observations on solid ground: an appeal from the decision of an administrative tribunal to a court of justice (be it “inferior” or “superior”) requires the application of administrative law standards (Dunsmuir), while an appeal from the decision of an administrative tribunal to another administrative tribunal calls upon the rules applicable to appeals (Housen).

[280]     Given that no judgment has cast doubt on the Court of Québec’s status as a court of justice, it follows that when it rules on appeals of administrative decisions, the standards in Dunsmuir are the only option.

[281]     All of this leads to the decision of this Court in Frères Maristes. In that case, the respondent had argued that the jurisprudential trend in Ryan, Dr. Q, Daigneault and Proprio Direct (SCC) was based on a mistaken premise in Quebec law, because “appeal” does not mean “judicial review”, and while it may make sense to speak of a duty of deference in an appeal to a superior court within the meaning of section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867, it does not when dealing with an appeal to an inferior court such as the Court of Québec.[394] The Court acknowledged that this argument was not devoid of interest, but considered that it was not the proper forum to debate it. In light of the principle of stare decisis, if followed the nearly unanimous jurisprudential trend on the matter:

[translation]

[5]        The argument is not devoid of interest, but given the current state of the jurisprudence, particularly that of the Supreme Court of Canada, the Court of Appeal is not the appropriate forum for its discussion. […]

[6]        In any event, after the Supreme Court decision in Proprio Direct and several judgments of the Court of Appeal, some prior to Proprio Direct, it is undisputed that the concepts governing judicial review must apply to appeals such as the respondent’s appeal to the Court of Québec in the case at bar. The decision in Montréal (Ville de) c. KPMG inc. did not deviate from this general principle, because the reasons of the majority in that case, far from diverging from the principle, explicitly did not question it. […].[395]

[282]     Notwithstanding this Court’s concerns in Frères Maristes, the Supreme Court declined to hear the appeal. Instead, in Mouvement laïque, rendered less than one year after Frères Maristes, Gascon, J., speaking for the majority, confirmed the jurisprudential trend in Ryan, Dr. Q and Proprio Direct (SCC).

[283]     Although Mouvement laïque dealt with the standards of review applicable to this Court when hearing appeals from administrative decisions, it should be noted that Gascon, J. reiterated the principle in a general fashion:

[38]      In my view, Dalphond J.A. was right: this approach must prevail. Where a court reviews a decision of a specialized administrative tribunal, the standard of review must be determined on the basis of administrative law principles. This is true regardless of whether the review is conducted in the context of an application for judicial review or of a statutory appeal (Association des courtiers et agents immobiliers du Québec v. Proprio Direct inc., 2008 SCC 32, [2008] 2 S.C.R. 195, at paras. 13 and 18-21; Dr. Q v. College of Physicians and Surgeons of British Columbia, 2003 SCC 19, [2003] 1 S.C.R. 226, at paras. 17, 21, 27 and 36; Law Society of New Brunswick v. Ryan, 2003 SCC 20, [2003] 1 S.C.R. 247, at paras. 2 and 21; Canada (Deputy Minister of National Revenue) v. Mattel Canada Inc., 2001 SCC 36, [2001] 2 S.C.R. 100, at para. 27).[396]

[Emphasis added]

[284]     The principles laid down in Mouvement laïque therefore confirm the approach that must be adopted with respect to appeals brought before the Court of Québec, principles that this Court reiterated in Cummings, where Rochette, J.A., citing Mouvement laïque, stated that the right to appeal to the Court of Québec must be approached from the perspective of judicial review.[397]

[285]     Although this jurisprudence as a whole developed without a formal discussion of the constitutionality of the introduction of such a standard, the Court is nevertheless bound by it. Admittedly, the application to appeals before the Court of Québec of the standards specific to the exercise of the Superior Court’s superintending and reforming power has sometimes been criticized, as discussed earlier,[398] a criticism that some of the parties to this reference, including the Attorney General of Quebec, have reiterated.[399]

[286]     In Procureure générale du Québec c. Ville de Montréal, Émond, J.A., as he then was, citing Frères Maristes, stated, in obiter, that [translation] “the application of the reasonableness standard to two subsequent review levels is disconcerting.”[400] Although he followed the applicable test according to the jurisprudence, Émond, J.A. allowed himself the following comment on the matter (on which Savard, J.A., who wrote brief concurring reasons, did not remark[401]):

[translation]

[44]      By limiting the scope of intervention of courts of justice sitting in appeal of specialized administrative tribunals, i.e. by subjecting such appeals to a standard of review determined on the basis of administrative law principles, the Supreme Court has limited the powers granted by the legislature to the Court of Québec in such matters. In practice, this means that the Court of Québec carries out a type of “judicial review”, even before the Superior Court does the same thing pursuant to its superintending and reforming power, when the losing party chooses, as it is entitled to do, to apply for judicial review. One may very well question the benefit and advisability of a review process that involves two successive and similar steps, especially given its potential cost to litigants.

[45] It would undoubtedly be useful to carry out a forward-looking and in-depth reflection on the matter.[402]

[287]     In Azzahra, a recent judgment of this Court, Mainville, J.A. once again reiterated that [translation] “appeals of decisions of a specialized administrative tribunal to a court of justice are, in principle, subject to the standards of judicial review”.[403] Citing the majority reasons in Procureure générale du Québec c. Ville de Montréal, he nevertheless expressed the following concern:

[translation]

[31]      That being said, the application of the reasonableness standard at two levels raises certain questions which it is not appropriate to deal with within the scope of this appeal.[404]

[288]     At this stage, one can conclude that the jurisprudence has not changed fundamentally since Parizeau: an appeal within the administrative system — or within the judicial system — gives rise to the application of appellate standards, but an appeal from within the administrative system to the judicial system instead calls into play the standards applicable to administrative law and judicial review.

[289]     Based on this reasoning, since the various legislative schemes at issue involve the review of decisions of specialized administrative tribunals by a court of justice — the Court of Québec — it is a given that the standards of review set out in Dunsmuir, rather than those set out in Housen, apply, as required by the jurisprudence of the Supreme Court and this Court.

[290]     Consequently, the Court cannot accept the approach suggested by the chief justices of the Superior Court. That approach invites the Court to [translation] “continue” the process initiated in Parizeau and conclude that the decisions likening “appeals” to “judicial review” are applicable only when the appellate function is granted to a “superior” court, as opposed to an “inferior” court such as the Court of Québec. They argue that, in the latter case, it is the appellate standards that should apply.

[291]     Such an approach, however, is not consistent with the jurisprudence of this Court nor that of the Supreme Court.

[292]     We must now determine whether the grant of such an appellate power to the Court of Québec, combined with the requirement that it apply standards of judicial review, results in an infringement of section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867.

4.6. The Re Residential Tenancies Act, 1979 test

[293]     Although the wording of the second reference question emphasizes the requirement of judicial deference, the following question remains: Is the grant of appellate jurisdiction over administrative decisions to a court of justice that is not a superior court within the meaning of section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867 valid?

[294]     In order to answer this question, we must apply the three-step test laid out in Re Residential Tenancies Act, 1979,[405] a test that Lamer, C.J., speaking for the majority of the Supreme Court, aptly summarized in MacMillan Bloedel:

The first branch of the test is an historical inquiry into “whether the power or jurisdiction conforms to the power or jurisdiction exercised by superior, district or county courts at the time of Confederation” (p. 734). […] The second step asks whether the function in question is “judicial” in its institutional setting, and he [Dickson, J.] contrasts “judicial” functions with policy making functions. The final branch of the test involves an assessment of the “tribunal’s function as a whole in order to appraise the impugned function in its entire institutional context” (p. 735). Under this branch of the test it is permissible for administrative tribunals and inferior courts to exercise powers historically belonging to courts with s. 96 judges provided those judicial powers are “merely subsidiary or ancillary” to the general administrative functions assigned to the tribunal (at p. 736).[406]

4.6.1.   Characterization of the impugned powers (step 1)

[295]     At this stage, the question is whether the powers conferred here upon the Court of Québec are “appellate” or “judicial review” powers, particularly in light of the standard of review applicable to appeals to the Court of Québec. This step is crucial for purposes of answering the question before us.

[296]     If the grant of jurisdiction to the Court of Québec were to be characterized as purely appellate, the analysis could stop here. Indeed, as Dickson, J. (as he then was) stated in Re Residential Tenancies Act, 1979: “If the historical inquiry leads to the conclusion that the power or jurisdiction is not broadly conformable to jurisdiction formerly exercised by s. 96 courts, that is the end of the matter”.[407]

[297]     An appeal is a statutory, de jure, and exceptional remedy, while the superintending and reforming power of the superior courts stems from the common law, exists as of right and is constitutionally protected.[408] Since the basis for the judicial review power of section 96 courts is not the same as the basis for their appellate power, one cannot conclude that the superior courts have always had exclusive appellate powers without undermining the very foundation of modern-day administrative law and the characteristic feature of a superior court, namely, its superintending and reforming power, a power that is inherent rather than statutory. Indeed, that is the opinion Professor Hogg expressed:

[…] Superior-court jurisdiction over administrative tribunals has never (apart from statute) included appellate authority extending to all questions of law, but merely review authority over questions of jurisdiction and (on certiorari) errors of law on the face of the record. […][409]

[298]     The argument put forth by the chief justices of the Superior Court, however, is based on the idea that the combined effect of the “appellate” power granted by law to the Court of Québec and the requirement of deference imposed by the jurisprudence is such that this grant of power is “analogous to a power exercised by s. 96 courts at Confederation”.[410]

[299]     When it sits in appeal, does the Court of Québec in fact exercise a power analogous to one of the powers the Superior Court exercised at Confederation?

[300]     As a general rule, an appeal, as a remedial recourse, focuses attention on the correctness or appropriateness of the decision, while judicial review, which is an instrument solely for nullifying or quashing a decision, focuses on the decision’s legality. In the first instance, the decision-maker with the statutorily granted appellate power must determine whether or not the decision is well-founded (“right or wrong), while in the second case, a judge of the Superior Court must determine whether the decision is legal (“lawful or unlawful”).[411]

[301]     Judicial review, therefore, focuses on questions of jurisdiction and certain questions of law akin to questions of jurisdiction of the administrative tribunal or the lower court rendering the decision. In essence, it reviews the legality of a decision so as to protect the rule of law.[412] Appeals, on the other hand, are the last step in a decision-making process established by a legislature; depending on legislative intent, the correctness and the appropriateness of the decision are often put into question.[413]

[302]     As Professor Daly notes, however, review of the legality [translation] “is essentially incidental to the central mission of appellate bodies, which is to correct errors committed by the decision-maker in first instance”.[414] He therefore recognizes that appeals generally involve a review of the legality of a decision.[415] Intrinsically, the “right decision” cannot be unlawful.[416]

[303]     That being said, the chief justices of the Superior Court are asking the Court, concurrently with their central claim regarding the unconstitutionality of the requirement of judicial deference, to confirm what they believe to be the genuine nature of the Court of Québec’s appellate power, namely, a review process governed by the rules in Housen. As a result, they are implicitly acknowledging that the grant of power to the Court of Québec to review the appropriateness, correctness and, consequently, the legality, of a decision is constitutional, but the grant of a power to review only the legality of a decision is not. With all due respect, this position is surprising.

[304]     With respect to the relationship between appeals and judicial review, Denis Lemieux wrote the following:

[translation]

When the appeal is limited to questions of law and jurisdiction, which includes mixed questions of fact and law, the Court will be able to review only the legality of the decision, not its merits. As such, it will play a greater superintending and reforming role. Appeals, however, are preferable to superintending remedies, because the conditions giving rise to the former are somewhat broader and involve fewer procedural limits.[417]

[305]     Based on this excerpt, it appears that if an appeal is limited to questions of law and jurisdiction, it would be akin to judicial review. However, where the appeal is not “limited” in this manner, the power conferred is therefore broader, but, intrinsically and not surprisingly, also includes a superintending and reforming role. Is this to say that an appellate power is always analogous to a power of judicial review, because it always involves a certain review of the legality of the decision? This conclusion does not fit well with the aforementioned judgments in Inglis, Farrah and Parizeau, where it was stated that a province has the constitutional power to create an administrative appeal tribunal.

[306]     One thing is certain, the legislative intent in the schemes in question here is not homogenous, such that the appellate power conferred is sometimes limited and sometimes quite broad. Even where a broad appellate power has been granted, similar to the jurisdiction of the Court of Appeal of Quebec, the Court of Québec must act with judicial deference, as recently reiterated in Mouvement laïque.[418]

[307]     By systematically limiting the intervention of the Court of Québec in this manner and imposing on it the standards of judicial review when it sits in appeal of the decision of an administrative tribunal, does the appellate power granted to it become analogous to the superintending and reforming power of the superior courts?[419] In accordance with the principles laid down by the Supreme Court and by this Court, the Court of Québec can no longer review all errors of law affecting decisions before it; it can only inquire into “the qualities that make a decision reasonable, referring both to the process of articulating the reasons and to outcomes”[420] (save for those rare questions of law and jurisdiction that require the application of the correctness standard[421]).

[308]     That being said, despite the fact that the Court of Québec, sitting in appeal of an administrative decision, and the Superior Court, sitting in judicial review of such a decision, exercise powers originating from different sources (purely statutory in the first case, inherent and constitutionally protected, in the second), in practice their respective interventions are governed by similar standards (since the judgments in Daigneault and Proprio Direct (CA), confirmed by Proprio Direct (SCC)).

[309]     This leads to the conclusion that, strictly speaking, within the meaning of Re Residential Tenancies Act, 1979, the appellate power granted to the Court of Québec is functionally akin to the Superior Court’s superintending and reforming power.

4.6.2.   Are the impugned powers judicial powers (step 2)?

[310]     Following the reasoning developed above and based on the jurisprudence of this Court and the Supreme Court, it appears that the function vested in the Court of Québec in the exercise of its appellate power is undeniably a judicial function.

4.6.3.   Are the impugned powers merely subsidiary or ancillary to an administrative function (step 3)?

[311]     The third test under Re Residential Tenancies Act, 1979 leads to the following question: Are the impugned judicial powers “merely subsidiary or ancillary to general administrative functions assigned to the tribunal” or are they “incidental to the achievement of a broader policy goal of the legislature”[422]? In other words “it is not the detached jurisdiction or power alone that is to be considered but rather its setting in the institutional arrangements in which it appears and is exercisable under the provincial legislation”.[423]

[312]     That being said, at present the Court of Québec has not been assigned any general administrative function. In the words of Dickson. J., citing Farrah, it would appear that “the adjudicative function is [the] sole or central function of the [Court of Québec]”, such that it is a tribunal operating like a section 96 court.[424] In this regard, Pigeon, J., writing concurring reasons in Farrah, stated:

[…] It is clear from reading them that all the tribunals that were found to be validly constituted were administrative agencies, not courts entrusted exclusively with the duty of deciding questions of law. […][425]

[313]     Consequently, this significant judicial function, which finds expression in a variety of fields and is not tied to any administrative function, cannot be characterized as ancillary or subsidiary.

[314]     Asking whether the purpose of the judicial function is the “achievement of a broader policy goal of the legislature” is a more promising test, but does not stand up to analysis either. In the Reference re Residential Tenancies Act, McLachlin, J. (as she then was), speaking for the majority, stated that “[w]hat is required [...] is a unifying concept or goal, and a sufficiently novel philosophy to belie any analogy with the powers previously exercised by superior courts”.[426] In the same vein, in Sobeys, Wilson, J. explained that this test exists “to allow the courts to consider new approaches to old problems, approaches which are more responsive to changing social conditions”.[427]

[315]     There seems to be no doubt that, over the years, the Supreme Court, when faced with questions involving section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867, has applied an “increasingly broad test of constitutional validity”[428] in order to allow for the development of effective provincial administrative justice.[429] However, insofar as the Court of Québec, in exercising its appellate functions, is not part of the administrative apparatus and exercises a function isolated from the rest of the administrative structure of the relevant statutes,[430] it is difficult to argue that it serves the commendable goal the provincial legislature has set in those statutes.

[316]     In short, applying the test in Re Residential Tenancies Act, 1979, it would appear, a priori, that the powers over administrative matters conferred on the Court of Québec are analogous to those exercised by the courts contemplated in section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867.

[317]     Nonetheless, in light of the fact that none of the statutes mentioned in the second reference question excludes the superintending and reforming power of the Superior Court over the decisions of the Court of Québec, the Supreme Court judgments in Farrah, Crevier and, particularly, Grondin offer, however, another perspective on the question.[431]

4.7. Appeals to the Court of Québec

[318]     First, it is worthwhile noting that, in Noël, LeBel, J., speaking for a unanimous Supreme Court, pointed out that provincial legislatures can circumscribe and limit the superintending and reforming power of the Superior Court, provided they do not eliminate it:

[65]      The question of unduly limiting the Superior Court’s power of review does not arise in this case. While review of the legality of actions by public authorities is a central element of the Superior Court’s jurisdiction, the legitimacy of defining certain limits to which that function must be subject is recognized in administrative and constitutional law. That function is not absolute. Provided they do not abolish it, legislatures may circumscribe and limit it, and may do so specifically by express legislative policy, for example by enacting privative clauses (see Crevier, supra; see also Domtar Inc. v. Quebec (Commission d’appel en matière de lésions professionnelles), [1993] 2 S.C.R. 756, at p. 800).

[66]      The principle of the rule of law does not require that all decisions of a lower court or administrative tribunal necessarily be subject to microscopic judicial review. Indeed, the fact that review is often limited to jurisdictional errors provides clear confirmation that it is legitimate to limit this superintending power. Those limits may affect the interest that is required in order for a party to have standing.[432]

[319]     The codification of the Superior Court’s superintending and reforming power, in article 34 C.C.P., also indicates that this power can be excluded by law, except on jurisdictional matters. Paragraph 2 of article 34 C.C.P. reads as follows:

This power cannot be exercised in cases excluded by law or declared by law to be under the exclusive purview of those courts, persons, bodies or groups, except where there is lack or excess of jurisdiction.

 

Ce pouvoir ne peut s’exercer dans les cas que la loi exclut ou qu’elle déclare être du ressort exclusif de ces tribunaux, personnes, organismes ou groupements, sauf s’il y a défaut ou excès de compétence.

[Emphasis added]

 

[Soulignements ajoutés]

[320]     This is why, for the sake of consistency, many provincial statutes limit the Superior Court’s supervisory power, “except on a question of jurisdiction”.

[321]     What is the situation here?

[322]     As mentioned above, all the statutes in question, save for the Act respecting the Régie du logement, which we will revisit below, preserve the Superior Court’s supervisory power over administrative decisions on questions of jurisdiction.

[323]     More importantly, however, none of the legislative schemes in question includes a privative clause limiting the powers of the Superior Court over decisions made by the Court of Québec sitting in appeal of administrative decisions. From this perspective, it can be stated that all of these legislative schemes maintain the Superior Court’s superintending and reforming power.

[324]     As explained above, there is nevertheless a grant of a power to the provincial court which, which when exercised, is analogous to that of the Superior Court. A reading of the Supreme Court judgments, however, suggests that this is insufficient to declare a right to appeal unconstitutional when the power of the superior courts over questions of jurisdiction subsists.

4.7.1.   The judgment in Farrah

[325]     In Farrah, Laskin, C.J., in concurring reasons, explained that when a tribunal has nothing but appellate powers, the effect may be to confer upon it the appearance and power of a section 96 court, which is not constitutionally valid. This would be the case where these appellate powers do not fit within an administrative scheme that would differentiate them from the powers of a court contemplated in section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867:

It is undeniable that s. 96 of the British North America Act may apply to an administrative tribunal of appeal as well as to one concerned with direct enforcement of a regulatory statute, a so-called primary tribunal. Where an administrative appeal agency is constituted, divorced, as is the Transport Tribunal here, from involvement in the exercise of original jurisdiction under the Transport Act and given a purely appellate authority which includes the power conferred by s. 58(a), there is a meshing both of jurisdiction and power, giving it the form and authority of a s. 96 Court. The fact that by s. 59 of the Transport Act, the tribunal “may confirm, vary or quash any decision submitted to it and render the decision which, in its opinion, should have been rendered in the first instance” simply underlines appellate authority of a kind which is normally given to appellate courts that meet the tests of s. 96.[433]

[Emphasis added]

[326]     Laskin, C.J. explained the implementation of that principle as follows in the specific context of the case before him:

It is pertinent to refer again to what Rand J. said in the Dupont case, supra. Since the power in s. 30 of the Transport Act, in respect of which an appeal is given by s. 58(b) is not of a type belonging to a s. 96 Court, appeal jurisdiction in respect thereof may be vested in an administrative tribunal, whatever its character and institutional arrangement. With respect to s. 58(a), however, which concerns an authority or function analogous to that exercisable by a s. 96 Court, the validity of the bestowal of such authority must depend on whether the institutional arrangements under which it is exercised mark it off from those under which s. 96 Courts exercise the same kind of appellate power.[434]

[327]     Dickson, J. referred to the same principle in Re Residential Tenancies Act, 1979,[435] as did Laskin, C.J. in Crevier.[436]

[328]     In Farrah, however, Pratte. J. speaking for the majority, insisted on the idea that it is the transfer of jurisdiction to a provincially established tribunal, to the exclusion of the superior courts, that is unconstitutional:

[…]      The net combined effect of s. 58(a) and of the privative clauses (ss. 24 and 72 of the Transport Act) is therefore to transfer to the Transport Tribunal part of the inherent supervisory authority that was vested in the Superior Court at the time of Confederation[437].

[Emphasis added]

[329]     As mentioned above, in that case, section 58(a) of the Transport Act[438] granted the Transport Tribunal exclusive appellate jurisdiction, on all matters of law, over the decisions of the Transport Commission. By virtue of sections 24 and 72 of the same statute, the decisions of the Transport Commission and those of the Transport Tribunal were completely insulated from the superintending and reforming power of the Superior Court. In that case, the Supreme Court found that the combined effect of insulating the decisions of the Transport Commission from the reforming power of the Superior Court and entrusting that power to a provincially established tribunal, to the exclusion of the Superior Court, resulted in the scheme being contrary to section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867.

[330]     Thus, the mere grant of appellate powers to an administrative tribunal over questions of law is not sufficient for a finding that those powers are unconstitutional.

4.7.2.   The judgment in Crevier

[331]     As set out above, Crevier dealt with section 194 of the Professional Code,[439] as it existed then, which insulated the Professions Tribunal from any judicial review, the essential function of the Professions Tribunal being that of hearing appeals of the decisions made by the discipline committees of the professional orders, a power conferred on it by section 162 of the aforementioned statute.

[332]     Re-examining the “adjudicative” function of a provincial tribunal, Laskin, C.J. questioned whether preserving the supervisory power of the Superior Court over questions of jurisdiction would be sufficient to give validity, within the ambit of section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867, to the grant of that function to such a tribunal. He concluded as follows:

[…] In my opinion, where a provincial Legislature purports to insulate one of its statutory tribunals from any curial review of its adjudicative functions, the insulation encompassing jurisdiction, such provincial legislation must be struck down as unconstitutional by reason of having the effect of constituting the tribunal a s. 96 court.[440]

[333]     Laskin, C.J. continued the analysis begun in Farrah and offered what is a perfect summary of the issue in the present matter, describing the two contrary positions expressed in answer to the second reference question. He wrote:

The Court of Appeal majority viewed the preclusive words of s. 194 as not touching the power and right of the Superior Court to issue a writ of evocation where there has been a want or excess of jurisdiction. Section 194 itself, however, does not recognize this supervisory authority of the Superior Court. If it did, it would be arguable that so long as the Professions Tribunal was subject to the superintendence of the Superior Court on questions of jurisdiction, it would not be tainted as exercising a power belonging to a s. 96 court by an initial but reviewable conclusion that a Discipline Committee had exceeded its jurisdiction. That is not this case, having regard to the embracive terms of s. 194 of the Professional Code. […][441]

[Emphasis added]

[334]     In Crevier, therefore, the Supreme Court confirmed that a provincial legislative scheme is unconstitutional where it insulates a provincial body from the review of its decisions on matters of jurisdiction, because there is “nothing that is more the hallmark of a superior court than the vesting of power in a provincial statutory tribunal to determine the limits of its jurisdiction without appeal or other review”.[442]

[335]     Nonetheless, it should be noted that, in the last sentence of the passage, Laskin, C.J., in obiter, referred to the idea that even in situations in which the Superior Court’s superintending and reforming power were preserved, there could be circumstances where that fact alone would be insufficient to lend constitutional validity to a provincial appeal tribunal:

[…] Even if it were otherwise and the supervisory authority of the Superior Court on questions of jurisdiction was expressly preserved, it would still not be a complete answer to a contention that the Professions Tribunal is exercising powers more conformable to those belonging to a s. 96 court than those properly exercisable by a provincial administrative or quasi-judicial tribunal or even a provincial judicial tribunal.[443]

[336]     Summarizing the principles enunciated by the Supreme Court in Farrah, he wrote, however:

In short, what the Farrah case decided was that to give a provincially-constituted statutory tribunal a jurisdiction in appeal on questions of law without limitation, and to reinforce this appellate authority by excluding any supervisory recourse to the Quebec Superior Court, was to create a s. 96 court. […][444]

[Emphasis added]

[337]     He ultimately concluded as follows regarding the defect tainting the statutes considered in Farrah and Crevier: “In both cases there was a purported exclusion of the reviewing authority of any other court, whether by appeal or by evocation”.[445]

[338]     In those two cases, it was actually the combined effect of an appellate power and privative clauses stripping the Superior Court’s reviewing power over the decisions of the appellate tribunal that led to the declarations of unconstitutionality.

[339]     In the matter at hand, while the Court of Québec is granted a statutory appellate power, the relevant statutes do not contain full and absolute privative clauses similar to those in Farrah and Crevier.

4.7.3.   The judgment in Grondin

[340]     In Grondin, the Supreme Court considered the Act respecting the Régie du logement.[446] The judgment dealt primarily with the jurisdictional powers granted to the Régie du logement, all of which were declared valid under section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867. The more relevant issue for purposes of our analysis are the appeals to the Provincial Court of the decisions of the Régie du logement. The Supreme Court found the scheme established by the Act respecting the Régie du logement to be valid, even though section 18 of the statute insulated the Régie’s decisions from the Superior Court’s superintending power, because there was no provision insulating the Provincial Court from that power.

[341]     In a judgment written by Chouinard, J., the Supreme Court dealt with the matter succinctly:

In cases where there is an appeal to the Provincial Court from decisions of the Board, it is true that judgments of the Provincial Court are not appealable. However, this Court is not immune to extraordinary recourses. No privative clause exists in its case.

In my opinion Farrah and Crevier do not apply to the case at bar.[447]

[342]     Consequently, the Supreme Court found the grant of appellate powers to the Provincial Court constitutional, given the absence of a privative clause in favour of the latter stripping the Superior Court of its superintending power.

4.7.4.   Conclusions arising from the analysis of these judgments

[343]     The conclusion to be drawn from these three judgments is that the essence of the protection offered by section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867 in respect of the superintending and reforming power lies in the ability of the Superior Court to review the legality of final decisions. The type of appeal granted to the Court of Québec is not conclusive, provided the Superior Court retains its right to review the Court of Québec’s decisions. In fact, the chief justices of the Superior Court themselves argue that it is the grant to a provincial court of an exclusive power to hear applications for judicial review that is inconsistent with section 96, pursuant to the first step of the test in Re Residential Tenancies Act, 1979 (paras. 147 and 148 of their brief).

[344]     Adding a level of appeal to the Court of Québec may well make the judicial system more cumbersome and may be an ill-advised legislative choice, but it does not usurp the ultimate power of the superior courts to ensure the legality of administrative action and action by inferior tribunals, unless a privative clause excludes the Superior Court’s supervisory power over questions of jurisdiction (Farrah and Crevier), which is not the case here.

[345]     As mentioned above, none of the statutes in question — save for the Act respecting the Régie du logement (and only with respect to that particular board) — excludes the Superior Court’s supervisory power over “questions of jurisdiction”. Based on recent Supreme Court jurisprudence,[448] this expression, which is associated, as we have seen, with the judgments in Farrah, Crevier and Grondin, must not be read as being limited solely to “true questions of jurisdiction” (in the strict sense of vires), a category that is very narrow.[449] The expression “except on a question of jurisdiction”, within the meaning of Farrah, Crevier and Grondin, also implies safeguarding the Superior Court’s supervisory power over unreasonable decisions of administrative bodies.[450]

[346]     It is true that the provincial legislature uses the term “jurisdiction / compétence” both when conferring a power upon the Court of Québec and when reserving the Superior Court’s supervisory power.[451] While the dual use of the word “jurisdiction / compétence” may be confusing, it is important to note that, notwithstanding the scope of the power granted to the Court of Québec (broad appellate power or appeal limited to questions of law and jurisdiction), the Superior Court retains its superintending power over questions of jurisdiction. Unlike the situation that existed in Farrah and Crevier, it cannot be said that the Court of Québec has been granted a power belonging to the Superior Court and that this power has been removed from within the latter’s purview.

[347]     It is also important to note that the exclusion, in the Act respecting the Régie du logement, of certain types of judicial review of the decisions of the Régie could have resulted in the statute’s unconstitutionality[452], but since there is no privative clause precluding a review of the legality of the Court of Québec’s decisions on appeal — unlike the situation in Farrah and Crevier, where the Superior Court’s reviewing power had, for all intents and purposes, been removed — the Supreme Court, in Grondin, found this legislative scheme to be valid, because it retains the Superior Court’s final decision-making power.[453]

[348]     The jurisprudential development of administrative law standards of review does not cast doubt on this conclusion, notwithstanding that, as a result, the Court of Québec’s intervention is more limited. Just like the legislative scheme under the Act respecting the Régie du logement — which is also being challenged, notwithstanding the decision in Grondin — all the statutes in question in this reference retain the Superior Court’s superintending power. Given the decision in Grondin, where section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867 was at the heart of the dispute, the Court cannot conclude that these statutes infringe that provision of the Constitution.

[349]     It should be noted that even though certain statutes listed in the second reference question contain “reinforcement clauses” providing for a remedy before the Court of Appeal, this does not in any way change the analysis.[454] The judge or judges of the Court of Appeal are empowered to summarily annul any decision of the Superior Court that is contrary to the privative clauses associated with the reinforcement clauses, but in no way do these reinforcement clauses exclude the Superior Court’s superintending power itself.[455]

4.8. The duplication argument

[350]     The Court must now consider the last facet of the argument, namely, that the duplication of the powers of the Court of Québec and the Superior Court dilutes the latter’s superintending and reforming power.

[351]     Does the duplicate application of the reasonableness standard effectively wipe out the Superior Court’s judicial review power?

[352]     When the Superior Court hears an application for judicial review of a judgment of the Court of Québec, it must begin by focusing on the administrative decision in order to first determine whether the Court of Québec identified the appropriate standard (which, in Superior Court, is a question of law subject to the correctness standard), and then determine whether it applied the standard properly. Thus, strictly speaking, the judgment of the Court of Québec is set to one side and the impugned administrative decision is the one under review.[456] This analysis will then serve as the starting point for affirming or reversing the Court of Québec’s judgment.[457]

[353]     The foregoing is what emerges from Rochette, J.A.’s reasons in Cummings:

[translation]

[37]      That being said, the test for determining whether the Superior Court was justified in intervening under the circumstances is relatively simple. We must first go back to the Commission’s decision. If it was reasonable, the Court of Québec should not have intervened and the Superior Court was right to grant the motion for judicial review of the Court of Québec’s judgment. If, however, the Commission’s decision was unreasonable, the Court of Québec’s intervention was justified, but its judgment must nevertheless be considered using the yardstick of “reasonableness”. If it was unreasonable, the Superior Court should have intervened.

[38]      For the reasons that follow, I am of the view that the Commission’s decision was not unreasonable and that the Court of Québec was wrong to intervene.[458]

[354]     Therefore, as long as the Superior Court can review the legality of the administrative decision reviewed by the Court of Québec, we conclude that, in light of the current state of the jurisprudence, there is no dilution of its superintending power, as protected under section 96, notwithstanding the practical concerns such duplication may engender, as stated above (supra, paras. [285] et seq.).

[355]     The chief justices of the Superior Court themselves argue that the Superior Court sitting in judicial review sets to one side the decision of the Court of Québec and reviews the decision of the administrative body directly (para. 163 of their brief). Although, in their view, this way of proceeding is contrary to the requirement of judicial deference,[459] they nevertheless confirm that the Superior Court ultimately retains its superintending and reforming power.

[356]     As mentioned above, the chief justices of the Superior Court also acknowledge that the Quebec legislature has the constitutional jurisdiction to grant a right of appeal to the Court of Québec.

[357]     They are therefore not concerned with the grant of powers, but with the application of judicial deference in the exercise of those powers.

[358]     It should be noted, in passing, that in 2017, the Quebec legislature tabled a bill in the National Assembly which reiterated the relevance of appeals to the Court of Québec and specified the standard of review applicable to these appeals, in a manner approximating that put forth by the chief justices of the Superior Court.[460] There was no follow-up to this initial bill, because provincial elections were held and the bill died on the order paper. Another bill, however, was tabled in the National Assembly on June 13, 2019, containing the same provision as the previous bill regarding the standard of review that the Court of Québec should apply when sitting in appeal of a decision made in the exercise of an adjudicative function.[461] As we saw earlier, the Attorney General of Quebec also acknowledges that the standards applicable to appeals to the Court of Québec could be revisited, but specifies, however, that this is an [translation] “administrative law issue”, rather than a constitutional one.

[359]     The parties to this reference, therefore, do not dismiss each other’s arguments as a whole.

[360]     It is true that, given the standard of judicial deference, the appellate power granted to the Court of Québec operates in a manner analogous to the superintending and reforming power of the Superior Court. The question raised by the chief justices of the Superior Court regarding the appropriateness of this standard therefore seems legitimate. This may even be seen to echo the obiter of Laskin, C.J. in Crevier, where he raised the idea that even where the Superior Court’s superintending and reforming power is preserved, it does not provide a complete answer to the argument that a provincial appellate tribunal is exercising powers more conformable to those belonging to a section 96 court.[462]

[361]     The requirement of judicial deference, however, was established by the jurisprudence of the Supreme Court, notably in Proprio Direct (SCC), which jurisprudence relied, in part, on the judgments of this Court in Daigneault and Proprio Direct (CA). The Court is bound by these precedents.

[362]     It nevertheless notes the argument of the chief justices of the Superior Court to the effect that the apparent duplication of remedies — an appeal to the Court of Québec and an application for judicial review to the Superior Court — where an analogous standard of deference applies, may, as some would contend, generate delays and costs that impede efficient access to justice. Some would argue that the Superior Court remedy could become theoretical and unused if, combined with the remedy before the Court of Québec, it becomes too costly for litigants to have access to a Superior Court.[463] On the other hand, it should be noted that the validity of the right to appeal that the chief justices of the Superior Court have acknowledged also implies delays and costs prior to litigants having access to a Superior Court. In any event, no evidence of the theoretical nature of the recourse before the Superior Court was presented within the scope of the reference.

4.9. Conclusions regarding the second reference question

[363]     In short, the Court of Québec is a court of justice, not an administrative tribunal. Thus, based on well-established precedent, which is binding on our Court, the judges who sit in appeal must apply standards of judicial review.

[364]     The effect is to limit the Court of Québec’s appellate powers over administrative matters, such that it exercises a jurisdiction functionally analogous to that of the superior courts hearing applications for judicial review.

[365]     Notwithstanding this, in all the legislative schemes in question, the Superior Court retains its full superintending and reforming power over administrative decisions and decisions of inferior tribunals as well as its fundamental role as the guardian of an independent and unified system of justice in Canada, as prescribed by section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867. This is a constitutional guarantee that benefits all litigants in Canada.

[366]     It may be said that such duplication makes the system more cumbersome and perhaps stands contrary to the objectives of administrative justice, but this is insufficient to hold that a legislative grant of power is unconstitutional.

[367]     Thus, in light of the Supreme Court judgments in Farrah, Crevier, Grondin and Proprio Direct (SCC), the Court must give an affirmative answer to the second question submitted to it in this reference: It is compatible with section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867 to apply the judicial deference requirement to the appeals to the Court of Québec provided for under the various statutes in question in this reference, given that none of them strips the Superior Court of its superintending and reforming power over decisions of the Court of Québec sitting in appeal.

5.   CONCLUSIONS

[368]      To the first reference question, worded as follows:

[translation provided by applicant’s counsel]

Are the provisions of the first paragraph of article 35 of the Code of Civil Procedure (chapter C-25.01), setting at less than $85,000 the limit to the exclusive monetary jurisdiction of the Court of Québec, valid with regard to section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867, given the jurisdiction of Quebec over the administration of justice under paragraph 14 of section 92 of the Constitution Act, 1867?

the Court answers: no, the provisions of article 35 of the Code of Civil Procedure (chapter C-25.01), setting at less than $85,000 the limit to the exclusive monetary jurisdiction of the Court of Québec, are not valid having regard to section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867.

[369]     To the second reference question, worded as follows:

[translation provided by applicant’s counsel]

Is it compatible with section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867 to apply the obligation of judicial deference, which characterizes the application for judicial review, to the appeals to the Court of Québec provided for in sections 147 of the Act respecting access to documents held by public bodies and the protection of personal information (chapter A-2.1), 115.16 of the Act respecting the Autorité des marchés financiers (chapter A-33.2), 100 of the Real Estate Brokerage Act (chapter C-73.2), 379 of the Act respecting the distribution of financial products and services (chapter D-9.2), 159 of the Act respecting administrative justice (chapter J-3), 240 and 241 of the Police Act (chapter P-13.1), 91 of the Act respecting the Régie du logement (chapter R-8.1) and 61 of the Act respecting the protection of personal information in the private sector (chapter P-39.1)?

the Court answers as follows: yes, it is compatible with section 96 of the Constitution Act, 1867 to apply the obligation of judicial deference to the appeals to the Court of Québec provided for in the various statutes identified in the question, given that none of them strips the Superior Court of its superintending and reforming power over decisions of the Court of Québec sitting in appeal.

[370]     Given the answer to the first reference question, it will be up to the Québec legislature to amend article 35 C.C.P. and set the limis on the jurisdiction of the Court of Québec in light of the factors and monetary indicia identified in this opinion. However, so as to not unduly disrupt the administration of justice,[464] our opinion shall not be implemented for a period of one year as of the date hereof, such that article 35 para. 1 C.C.P. shall be considered valid during that time.

[371]     Moreover, in order to avoid any uncertainty that may result from the answer to the first reference question, the Court declares that judgments rendered by the Court of Québec prior to the date of this opinion and for the period of one year during which the effect of our answer to the first reference question will not be implemented shall be fully effective and enforceable, the whole pursuant to the de facto doctrine and the doctrine of res judicata, as explained by the Supreme Court of Canada, notably, in Reference re Manitoba Language Rights.[465]

[372]     The Court remains seized of the application for reimbursement of the extrajudicial costs and disbursements incurred by the chief justices of the Superior Court to participate in the reference.
