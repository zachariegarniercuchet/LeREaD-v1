Green, J.A.:

 

[1]              The issue in this appeal involves a determination as to whether “the right to engage in mining operations” in s. 9(1)(a) of the Mining and Mineral Rights Tax Act, R.S.N. 1990, c. M-16 encompasses the rights of a licensee under a licence to explore for minerals issued under the Mineral Act, R.S.N. 1990, c. M-12.  On the answer to that question turns the ability of the province to impose an annual tax of 20% on royalties to be received by the appellant, Archean Resources Ltd,  from the operator of any mine developed with respect to the Voisey’s Bay mineral deposits in Labrador.

 

[2]              Archean sought a declaration from the Trial Division that, on a proper interpretation of the applicable legislation, the net smelter royalty it had negotiated with Diamond Fields Resources Inc (DFRI) in consideration for the assignment by Archean to DFRI of Archean’s mineral exploration licence, at a time when the Voisey’s Bay deposits had not been discovered, was not subject to the province’s royalty tax.  The trial judge concluded, applying a “modern” purposive approach to interpretation, that the royalties were payable as a result of a grant to DFRI of “the right to engage in mining operations” and thus attracted tax under s. 9(1)(a).  His decision is reported at (1999), 174 Nfld & P.E.I.R. 183.  On appeal, Archean argues that the trial judge erred in his analysis and application of the proper principles of statutory interpretation to the applicable legislation.

 

BACKGROUND

 

[3]              Archean is a Newfoundland prospecting company engaged in the business of mineral exploration.  Its principals are Albert Chislett and Chris Verbiski, the co-discoverers of the nickel-copper-cobalt ore bodies at Voisey’s Bay, Labrador.

 

[4]              In the early 1990's Archean acquired a number of exploration licences and began exploration in the Voisey’s Bay area.  Pursuant to s. 23 of the Mineral Act, those licences conferred on Archean “the exclusive right to explore for minerals” in the areas described in the licence for a period of 5 years.  They did not, however, confer any right to remove minerals except for the limited purposes of sampling, assaying and testing.  Section 24 of the Act recognized the assignability of the licences to other persons.

 

[5]              In 1993, before the discovery of the Voisey’s Bay ore bodies, Archean granted an option to DFRI to purchase “an undivided 100 percent right, title and interest” in the lands covered by the exploration licences in return for consideration which included a 3 percent net smelter royalty on any subsequent mineral production from those lands.  The option was exercised in 1995 and thereafter, pursuant to the option agreement, DFRI acquired all the rights of the licensee under the exploration licences, with Archean retaining no interest in the lands covered by them.  Instead, Archean became entitled to the royalties provided for in the agreement.

 

[6]              Because the Voisey’s Bay ore deposit is not yet developed, no royalty payments have  been made.  It is a matter of public knowledge that the current holder of the rights relating to the deposit is now in the process of securing a mining lease from the provincial government pursuant to s.31 of the Mining Act.  It was because Archean contemplated the sale to a third party of its contractual right to the royalty payments that, in order to remove any doubt as to its tax position, a declaration was sought from the Court that the royalty payments were not subject to s. 9(1)(a) of the Mining and Mineral Rights Tax Act.

 

[7]              Under the scheme of the Mining Act, only the holder of an exploration licence may apply for a mining lease.  It is the lease which confers upon the lessee “the exclusive right to develop, extract, remove, deal with, sell, mortgage, or otherwise dispose of” all unalienated minerals in, on or under the land covered by the lease (s. 31(5)(a)).  Before that point, no actual mining extraction activity can be undertaken.  To protect a licensee, however, subsections 31(2),(3) and (4) provide that a licensee who properly applies for a lease during the currency of the licence, and has complied with the conditions of the licence, properly surveys the area and completes all assessment work required for the first three years of the licence,  has “a right to the issuing of a mining lease” for a reasonable period not exceeding 25 years.

 

 

THE TRIAL JUDGMENT

 

[8]              As noted, Archean applied to the Trial Division for a declaration that the royalty payments to which it was entitled once the Voisey’s Bay mine became producing were not subject to taxation under s. 9(1)(a) of the Mining and Mineral Rights Tax Act.  The applicable portion of the charging provision reads as follows:

 

9. (1)    A person who receives from

 

(a)        an operator, contractor or other person during a financial year money by way of rental, royalty or similar payment for the grant ... of the right to engage in mining operations;

 

is liable for and shall pay to the minister [of Finance] ... an annual tax of 20% of the net revenue received in consideration of the grant of the right during that financial year.

 

[9]              The key question, it will be seen, is whether the royalty to be received by Archean was in consideration of “the grant ... of the right to engage in mining operations”.  Archean argued that because it acquired the royalty for the transfer only of exploration licences, which in themselves did not allow for the extraction or removal of the minerals, it could not be said that the royalty was given in return for “the right to engage in mining operations”. It followed, it submitted, that the royalty was not subject to tax.

 

[10]         The trial judge rejected this interpretation.  He concluded that Archean’s situation was within the “mischief” to which the Mining and Mineral Rights Tax Act was directed.  In defining what the mischief that was being remedied by the legislation was, he placed considerable reliance on the Report of the Royal Commission on Mineral Revenue (1974), which is generally regarded as the impetus for the legislative reforms reflected in the Act enacted in the following year, as well as on statements by the minister who introduced the Bill in the House.  Applying a “remedial and liberal construction”, he reasoned:

 

[44] ... [M]ining cannot occur at Voisey’s Bay or even be contemplated, unless an operator holds a mining lease.  Mining leases can only be granted on the basis of the exploration licences transferred from Archean and cannot otherwise be obtained.  I am satisfied therefore that in reality the right to engage in mining operations was in fact the benefit received by the optionee when the option was exercised and the transfers completed.  In no other way of which I am aware, could that most basic element of the right to mine, be obtained.

 

[45]     The position is equally clear when viewed from the perspective of the buyer.  What did Diamond Fields ... really purchase with their payment of significant sums of money and their agreement to pay the royalty.  In my opinion at the heart of the transaction was the right to obtain a mining lease and thus ‘the right to engage in mining operations’.  Conditions will necessarily apply, but the right to engage in mining operations is now theirs and under the legislative scheme there was no other manner by which that right could have been obtained.

 

[46]     For these reasons I must deny ... [Archean’s] claim for a declaration ...

 

ISSUES ON APPEAL

 

[11]         Archean argues on appeal that the trial judge erred in applying an improper interpretative approach to the determination of the meaning and scope of               s. 9(1)(a).  In particular Archean argues  that the proper principle of interpretation is the “plain meaning” rule, i.e. that statutory language should be given its plain and ordinary grammatical meaning and that in the absence of ambiguity resort to other interpretive devices or principles, or to extrinsic aids, is unnecessary and inappropriate.  Applying that approach will yield an interpretation, it is argued,  that will recognize that an exploration licence, which does not carry with it the right to extract minerals, cannot on a plain reading of the language in the section encompass a right to engage in mining operations.

 

[12]         Alternatively, Archean argues that  resort to other interpretive devices and extrinsic aids, even if  appropriate, would, if properly employed, lead to the same result.

 

[13]         Finally, Archean argues that, at most, the scope of the provision is subject to reasonable uncertainty and factual ambiguity resulting from a lack of explicitness and that the principle that ambiguities are to be resolved in favour of the taxpayer dictates that the issue should be resolved in Archean’s favour.

 

 

 

 

ANALYSIS

 

[14]         The primary submission of the appellant is that, applying a plain meaning principle of construction, the words of s. 9(1), which impose a tax on consideration given for “the right to engage in mining operations”, do not encompass the rights held by an exploration licensee, which merely involve the right to apply for a lease if certain pre-conditions are satisfied.  The argument is that the words of s. 9(1) plainly do not encompass the broader, more amorphous, rights under a mining licence ( namely, a contingent, conditional and future right to apply for the right to engage in mining operations) and that, accordingly, that should be the end of the matter.  Counsel for the appellant relied on, among other cases, the decision of the Supreme Court of Canada in R. Multiform Manufacturing Co. (1990), 79 C.R. (3d) 390 in support of this position.  There, Lamer C.J. said at p. 394:

 

When the words used in a statute are clear and unambiguous, no further step is needed to identify the intention of Parliament.  There is no need for further construction when Parliament has clearly expressed its intention in the words it has used in the statute.  As Maxwell stated ...:

 

 If there is nothing to modify, alter or qualify the language which the statute contains, it must be construed in the ordinary and natural meaning of the words and sentences.

 

[15]         Other decisions of the Supreme Court, decided both before and after the Multiform decision, stress what appears to be a more contextual and purposive approach to interpretation.  For example, in Stubart Investments Limited v. The Queen (1984), 10 D.L.R. (4d) 1, Estey J. adopted, at p. 32,  Dreidger’s formulation of the “modern rule”, taken from the second edition of his text, The Construction of Statutes, as follows:

 

Today there is only one principle or approach, namely, the words of an Act are to be read in their entire context and in their grammatical and ordinary sense harmoniously with the scheme of the Act, and the intention of Parliament.

 

[16]         This formulation has also been adopted by the Court in cases decided subsequent to Multiform.  See, Re Rizzo & Rizzo Shoes Ltd. [1998] 1 S.C.R. 27 and R. v. Gladue [1999] 1 S.C.R. 688.  

 

[17]         In reality, however, there is no real inconsistency between these two approaches.  Even Multiform only suggests falling back on plain language where “there is nothing to modify, alter or qualify the language which the statutes contains”.  To determine, in any given case, whether there is anything that potentially could modify or qualify the plain words, it will be necessary to examine more than the actual language of the provision being construed.  Multiform itself, therefore, implicitly recognizes that interpretation involves more than a blinkered focus solely on the words used. Rarely will context have no role to play in giving meaning to the sterile words that are employed, given the general plasticity of language.  Multiform does not speak to the situation where, as is often the case, there are other indicators of meaning that, considered reasonably, might have the effect of modifying or qualifying one’s first impression from the initial reading of  the words alone.

 

[18]         The argument of the appellant that the “proper principle” of interpretation is that “in cases of clear and unambiguous statutory language, the scheme, object and intention of Parliament as adverted to in Stubart are apparent and yield the proper meaning based on the plain and ordinary meaning of the statutory language” no longer (if it ever did) represents the proper approach to statutory interpretation.  The “scheme, object and intention of Parliament” must be gleaned, not only from the words used, whether ambiguous or not, but from a broad range of other additional sources of meaning.  All sources of meaning must be analyzed in an integrated fashion to determine what the scope of a particular legislative act is and how it should be fairly applied in a given case.

 

[19]         The starting point for interpretation of any statute enacted by the legislature of this province is the legislature’s own directive to the courts as found in s. 16 of the Interpretation Act:

 

Every Act and every regulation and every provision of an Act or regulation shall be considered remedial and shall receive the liberal construction and interpretation that best ensures the attainment of the objects of the Act, regulation or provision according to its true meaning.

 

[20]         What is noteworthy about this provision is that it applies to all legislative acts regardless of subject matter.  It draws no distinction between social reform legislation, taxation statutes,  legislation that deals with or affects property rights, or penal laws or statutes that affect personal or common law rights, to name a few types of statutes that from time to time have been said to attract special interpretative treatment. It eliminates special approaches, by way of presumptions of strict or other specialized construction, to the interpretation of certain types of statutes and not to others.

 

[21]         It also contains no reference to that fictional notion of “intention of the legislature”, the ascertainment of which is often expressed to be the ultimate goal of statutory interpretation.  Of course, it is idle to speak in any realistic way of the intention of the legislature as a collectivity. It does not exist.  The reality is that a majority of legislators, perhaps for differing reasons and with differing motivations, and influenced by our parliamentary tradition of executive control of the legislature, coalesce to create a formal legislative act which is then consigned, once the formalities of its adoption are complied with, to the courts for application, in cases of doubt, to individual fact situations.  Legislation, by virtue of its nature, the manner in which it comes into being, and by virtue of the fact that it generally operates prospectively, must of necessity be expressed in generalized language.  Not every fact scenario that could be potentially engaged by the subject of the legislation can be anticipated.  Even the most detailed legislative provision cannot purport to address specifically all situations that might potentially be affected or caught by its reach.  One need only refer to litigation that arises out of the application of income tax legislation for verification of this observation

 

[22]         Instead of mandating some fictionalized search for a collective “legislative intention”, s. 16 directs the court to consider every provision “remedial” and to interpret it so that it “best” ensures the attainment of its “objects” according to its “true” meaning.  This requires a consideration, as an integral part of the interpretive exercise, of the problem or “mischief” to which the legislature directed its legislative act as a remedy and then the drawing of an inference, based on the language of the whole enactment and the court’s general knowledge of the state of the pre-existing law and any information as to the broad social context in which the legislative act occurred, as to what, broadly speaking, the object or objects of the legislative act must have been.   The end result is to arrive at a “true” meaning.  That inevitably requires an examination of more than the bare words of the legislative enactment that is in issue, no matter how clear or unambiguous they may at first blush appear.  The surrounding text, the interrelation of other related statutes, the social and legislative context in which the provision was enacted, and other extrinsic aids are all sources to be consulted in this exercise. Obviously, if the bare words of the relevant provision appear to be straightforward and seem on their face to admit of only one meaning, they may end up controlling the result, but even in such a case, it is not sufficient to stop the interpretive exercise at this “plain” meaning; s. 16 requires that at the very least this plain meaning be given a “reality check” by being tested against other relevant sources of meaning to ensure that there is not some nuance or variation in the normal or apparent meaning that might indicate a different meaning in the particular context under consideration. “True” meaning is not plain meaning; it is a conclusion arrived at by reconciling all the appropriate indicators of meaning that the court is directed to consider.

 

[23]         In truth therefore, s. 16 enunciates a principle of harmonization in which the courts are directed, in cases of dispute, to adopt and apply an interpretation that fairly reconciles the language used in the enactment with the broader objects of the legislation so as to achieve the general goal, or to rectify the mischief, to which  the legislative act appears to have been directed. That exercise determines the general ambit of impact of the legislative act and provides the basis for the court to conclude whether the particular fact situation before it should fall inside our outside that ambit.

 

[24]         I do not propose to analyze all of the Supreme Court and other appellate decisions that have been cited.  It is sufficient to say that I agree with counsel for the respondent in this case that those decisions that are often cited as supporting an exclusively plain meaning approach to interpretation can generally be explained as involving situations where reliance on “plain meaning” is expressed as a conclusion as to meaning only after considering other interpretive sources such as legislative purpose, the mischief to be addressed, context and legislative and social history.  “Plain meaning” becomes a euphemism for “the only reasonable interpretation” after the first impressions flowing from the words used have been tested against other indicators of meaning. As McLachlin J. observed in Opetchesaht Indian Band v. Canada (1997), 147 D.L.R. (4th) 1 (SCC) at pp. 22-23, “a court should only be satisfied with the plain meaning of a statute where that meaning is clear and consistent with a purposive reading of the statute as a whole.” [Emphasis added].

 

[25]         In reality, the rhetoric of plain meaning and other formalistic approaches to interpretation often mask a broader hidden analysis.  Ruth Sullivan, in “Statutory Interpretation in the Supreme Court of Canada” (1998-99), 30 Ottawa L.R. 175 argues that the plain meaning (textualist) approach as well as an intentionalist approach (nominally involving a search, including sources beyond the bare words, for legislative intent) do not provide an adequate explanation for the role of the courts in statutory interpretation.  She argues that there should be a more explicit recognition of the role that judges play in adapting and applying legislative provisions to specific fact situations.  She suggests a “pragmatic” theory of interpretation that:

 

...emphasizes the importance of the legislative text and the importance of legislative intent, but places final responsibility for the outcome on the shoulders of the interpreting judges.  On this theory, the judicial task in interpretation is properly characterized not as giving effect to the meaning of the text or the intention of the legislature, but as solving the interpretive problem in an appropriate and acceptable way. (p. 178)

 

[26]         The process of interpretation then becomes an exercise in finding appropriate justifications for a fair and reasonable result in the circumstances of the particular case in a way that respects the important values of society within the context of the legislative directive that has been given.   It recognizes that, within those parameters, “the outcomes of interpretation disputes are appropriately determined by judges, not legislatures” (p. 185) and provides a more realistic explanation of the current practice of Canadian courts without resort to linguistic or legal fictions. It recognizes that resolving particular interpretative disputes involves assigning appropriate weight to various factors to arrive at a result that conforms to the legislative text and broad legislative purpose while at the same time producing an outcome that is just and reasonable. According to Professor Sullivan, “assigning appropriate weight to these factors and balancing them against one another is the essence of the judicial function in interpretation” (p. 184).

 

[27]         This description of the interpretative function is reflected in the third edition of Driedger, authored by Professor Sullivan, at p. 132:

 

...the courts must consider and take into account all relevant and admissible indicators of legislative meaning.  After taking these into account, the court must then adopt an interpretation that is appropriate.  An appropriate interpretation is one that can be justified in terms of (a) its plausibility, that is, its compliance with the legislative text; (b) its efficacy, that is its promotion of the legislative purpose; and (c) its acceptability, that is, the outcome is reasonable and just.

 

[28]         Such an approach, with which I agree, recognizes a limited role for the courts in law-making as they flesh out the skeleton of the legislation in the context of specific fact situations that may not have been anticipated or adverted to by the legislators when adopting generalized text for the purpose of furthering broad policy objectives.  The courts, whose raison d’etre is the resolution of specific disputes in a just and fair manner according to law, have necessarily been assigned the role, both constitutionally and by the legislature itself through its directive in   s. 16 of the Interpretation Act, of deciding interpretative disputes in a manner that is generally perceived as fair and reasonable.  In performing that function, they must of course have regard to the actual legislative text and the reasonable inferences which the court draws as to the overall objective, or parameters of operation, of the legislation.  In so doing, the court pays respect to the principle of deference to the legislative will.

 

[29]         The greater the precision and circumstance-specific the text and the clearer the indicators of legislative objective, the more controlling will those factors be with respect to an outcome in a particular case.  In the end, however, the court must seek to reconcile the text and the legislative objectives with a result that is perceived to be fair and just in the particular circumstances of the individual case.  It is in this latter context that notions of general fairness, and common law, social and constitutional values are engaged and are sought to be harmonized with the other factors of text and legislative objective.

 

[30]         What have been often regarded as sub-rules of statutory construction or presumptions of legislative intent are in reality ways in which the courts attempt to achieve fairness in application of a legislative provision in a particular case.  For example, whether one talks of strict construction of taxing statutes against the taxing authority or a rule that resolves ambiguity in favour of a taxpayer, the reason for such “rules” is that in many cases it would be perceived as unfair to a taxpayer, who orders his or her financial affairs on what appears to be a clear or reasonable construction of a legislative provision, to impose, after the fact, an interpretation that could not clearly have been anticipated in advance.  Rather than talk in terms of presumptions or sub-rules, which in themselves have fictional qualities about them, it is better to consider directly whether a particular text-based construction offends these general notions of fairness in the factual circumstances of the particular case.  As Sullivan points out, conducting an analysis in terms of presumptions of legislative intent (such as, the presumptions that the legislature intends to comply with the Charter, or does not intend an absurdity, or that it does not intend to unduly interfere with property or other common law rights, to name a few), and thereby achieving a narrowing of generalized text, effectively “mutes judicial responsibility and makes it less likely that these presumptions will be critically examined” (p. 225) and “tends to empty the notion of ‘legislative intent’ of real significance or force”.

 

[31]         To those who would argue that this approach is inconsistent with legislative supremacy and the notion that judges are mere appliers, not makers, of law, Sullivan has the following, in my view persuasive, answer:

 

          [The pragmatic approach to interpretation] ... requires judges to acknowledge and justify the other factors that influence their judgments ... [and] ... it requires judges to address the crucial problem of weight. ... Pragmatism is more persuasive because it asks judges to attribute only as much weight to the text as it can bear having regard to considerations like relative clarity, and only as much weight to legislative intention as it can bear having regard to the cogency of the inferences that can be drawn from reading the text in context and from extrinsic evidence. Pragmatism expects judges to also rely on other, judicially-noticed factors but only to the extent such reliance is warranted having regard to the relevance and importance of these other factors.  The clearer the text, the more compelling the evidence of specific legislative intent, the harder it will be to justify departure from those constraints and the more compelling the other factors will have to be.

 

          Another virtue of pragmatism is that it offers a better way of characterizing the separation of powers between legislatures and courts. Under traditional versions of the separation doctrine, the legislature makes the law and the court’s job is to apply that law to particular facts.  Under the pragmatic approach, the legislature makes statutes and the court’s job is to resolve disputes in accordance with the law.  With this approach, statutes are the primary but not the sole source of law. ... In a common law jurisdiction, supplemental law consists of the rest of the statute book, the common law and the evolving legal tradition which draws on current social and political values as well as those of the past.

 

          The final virtue of pragmatism is that it reconciles the reality of judicial choice with the imperatives of democracy. ... [E]ven if it were possible, it would be wrong for courts to apply the directives of the legislature mechanically and blindly to particular facts.  Courts must act as mediators to ensure that disputes are resolved in ways that respect all the values of constitutional democracy. ...

 

          The pragmatic account of statutory interpretation is often resisted because it is thought to give too much discretion to unelected judges, contrary to both democratic principle and the rule of law.  But in truth, pragmatism does not “give” judges additional discretion; it merely acknowledges the discretion they have, and must have, to resolve interpretation disputes.  The only real question is whether we are content with formalism or would rather know the “real” reasons for a decision. [Underlining added]

 

[32]         In other words, while it is the legislature that initiates the law-making process, it must be recognized that law-making is not accomplished in a vacuum; it is designed to have practical application.  In cases of dispute as to application in a given case, the legislature consigns to the courts, as the constitutionally-accepted forum for the arbitration of disputes, the role of resolving particular disputes in a fair and reasonable way, taking into account what the legislature has attempted to accomplish by its legislative act.

 

[33]         It is necessary now to consider how this approach to interpretation affects the result in this case.

 

[34]         The trial judge’s approach to interpretation in this case is summarized in the following paragraph from his judgment:

 

[21]      I therefore propose to interpret section 9 and the Mining and Mineral Rights Tax Act as a whole, according to the contemporary or modern approach which is also in my opinion the approach mandated by the Interpretation Act.  To do so it is proper for me to consider materials touching on the objectives of the Act and the objectives of the Legislature in enacting its provisions.  To put it another way, was there a "mischief" which the Legislature was attempting to rectify.  For these purposes I am entitled to have recourse to the report of a Royal Commission on mineral revenues and to a lesser and very limited extent, to a statement as to the objects of the legislation as expressed by the Minister of Mines and Energy in 1975 during debate in the House of Assembly.

 

[35]         He rejected a "plain meaning" approach to interpretation in the following words:

 

[39]      Can it then be said that in 1995 Archean transferred to Diamond Fields and its successors, "the right to engage in mining operations" in the context of the Act.  At first blush and on a narrow construction of the wording, one might conclude that because an exploration license is one entity and a mining lease is another, that the transfer was not the transfer of the right to engage in mining operations.  However, after review and reflection, I have concluded otherwise.

 

[36]         The trial judge quoted extensively from the Report of the Royal Commission on Mineral Revenue (1974) although he acknowledged that all of the recommendations of that Commission were not necessarily embodied in the ensuing legislation.

 

[37]         He concluded:

 

[40]      The historical background of mining concessions in this province as provided by the .... Royal Commission and a reading of the legislation which followed, makes it clear that the legislative provisions for exploratory licences, mining leases and the tax on royalties, were designed to avoid the undesirable practices of the past whereby concession holders received substantial royalty payments from mine operators, without tax liability.  These payments were described as unearned royalties, and it was such royalties at which the legislation was aimed.

 

[41]      Archean was essentially a prospector.  It sold its exploration licences to Diamond Fields for cash, shares and the right to receive a royalty of 3.0% on net smelter returns.  It now proposes to sell its contractual right to receive the 3.0% royalty, which is the only right which it now has, to a prospective purchaser.  Without the tax liability under section 9, it appears to me that Archean or its purchaser/successor would be in the same position as were the receivers of unearned royalties prior to 1975.

 

. . . . . .

 

[43]      Clearly the objects of the legislation are that unearned royalties derived from mining operations within the province, shall be subject to a specific tax regime in addition to taxes of general application."

 

[38]         The trial judge did not accede to the argument made on behalf of Archean that the focus of the comments in the Royal Commission Report on "unearned royalties" and on the scope of s. 9 was limited to taxation of existing concession holders who had bartered their rights in the past to other operators in return for royalties which had escaped taxation.  He apparently took the view, a view with which I agree, that focus on the ills of the past with respect to the non-taxation of specific concession holders was merely the motivation or justification for recommending a new taxation regime that would not allow for the repeat of situations where people could trade on the non-renewable mineral resources of the province without paying a fair return to the province for that privilege.

 

[39]         The trial judge concluded that the language employed in s. 9 broadly construed in accordance with s. 16 of the Interpretation Act, could, and should, encompass a situation where the rights of a licence holder, as opposed to holder of an actual mining lease, are granted or transferred in return for a royalty payment.  This conclusion was primarily influenced by the fact that under the legislative scheme the only way, short of an express grant by the legislature, in which a right to engage in mining operations could be obtained, was through the prior acquisition of an exploration licence and the fulfillment of the conditions associated with that licence.  He recognized the reality that an exploration licence which has resulted in the discovery of commercial ore has significant value precisely because the holder of it controls the mechanism by which a mining lease can be obtained.  While it is true that the "right" of the holder of a licence to the issuing of a mining lease pursuant to s. 31 of the Mineral Act, can only be exercised if he or she fulfills certain ancillary conditions, the fulfillment of those conditions  (such as completion of previous assessment work and the provision of surveys and markings) is within the control of the holder of the licence. 

 

[40]         The licence holder therefore has it within his or her power to exercise the right to obtain a lease, which cannot be arbitrarily refused in the discretion of the Minister, and, in  the words of s. 31(5)(a) of the Mineral Act, acquire "the exclusive right to develop, extract, remove, deal with, sell, mortgage, or otherwise dispose of all the unalienated minerals, or those specified in the lease, in, on or under the land described in the lease".  While the licence itself confers no right to remove minerals except for very limited purposes (s. 23(2) of the Mineral Act) the licence in effect is the key that enables the benefits of a mining lease to be unlocked. 

 

[41]         Counsel for Archean nevertheless argues that the words "grant of the right to engage in mining operations" in s. 9(j)(a) of the Mining and Mineral Rights Tax Act admit of only one reasonable interpretation, namely, that they are clearly referable to a present grant of an existing right that would have the benefit of conferring upon the recipient a present entitlement to carry on mineral extraction activities".  I do not agree.  The question in reality is whether a licencee’s "right to the issuing of a mining lease" within s. 31(2) of the Mineral Act is the equivalent of "the right to engage in mining operations" in s. 9(1)(a) of the Mining and Mineral Rights Tax Act.  As noted, the ability of a licencee to obtain a mining lease (and the right to "develop, extract, remove, deal with, mortgage, or otherwise dispose of" the minerals in s. 31(5)(a) of the Mineral Act) is dependent solely on matters within the control of the licencee; it does not depend on the discretionary decision of the government.  Provided the licencee has complied with the terms of the licence and submits a proper application with appropriate survey, there is a "right" to a lease and, by extension, a right to engage in mining operations. 

 

[42]         Because the licencee has the legal means to compel the issuance of a lease, which carries with it the right to extract minerals, it can be said, without torturing or stretching the language, that he has a right to engage in mining operations, albeit at some time in the future.  While it is true that the right cannot be exercised at that moment, that is, at any time during the currency of the licence and before a lease is issued, it is nevertheless a present right in a similar way to rights in property and trust law that may be vested in interest but only enjoyable at a later time.

 

[43]         In a sense, the "right to engage in mining operations" in s. 9(1)(a) of the Mining and Mineral Rights Tax Act can be said to be broader in its formulation than the "right to develop, extract, remove ... minerals" in s. 31(5)(a) of the Mineral Act.  I agree with counsel for the respondents that the notion of "engaging" in mining operations is a larger concept than that of extracting minerals.  The "right to engage" connotes possible commencement or arrangement of affairs in the future rather than the actual development or extraction.  The phrase "right to extract minerals" is specifically used in other parts of the legislation.  See, for example, the definition of "operator" in s. 2(j) of the Mining and Mineral Rights Tax Act, which defines an operator as a person who "(i) has the right to extract minerals, and (ii) carries out mining operations ..."  The use of the different phrase "right to engage in mining operations" in s. 9(1)(a) is some indication that a different and broader concept was contemplated, instead of a reference strictly to an existing mining lease and the present right to extract minerals, as was submitted by counsel for Archean.

 

[44]         Finally, as far as the textual analysis in the context of the legislation itself goes, it is not without significance that s. 8 of the Mining and Mineral Rights Tax Act appears to allow an operator to deduct from its net income derived from mining operations, for the purpose of paying a tax on the right to mine, the amount of royalties paid to a person "for the grant of the right to engage in mining operations".  Thus it appears that if a mining company employs a separate prospecting company to stake claims and then takes a transfer of the licence under which the claims were staked in return for a royalty payment to the prospector, it can deduct the royalty so paid as an expense, for the purposes of calculating the tax it would otherwise pay on the right to mine.  The amount of tax that would otherwise be payable by the mining company would therefore be reduced.  It would be odd if the province were to be thereby deprived of such tax revenue in the hands of the mining company and still not be able to recoup it in the hands of the person to whom the royalties were paid.  Otherwise the overall return to the province would not be maximized. 

 

[45]         In light of the recommendation of the Royal Commission which led to the new legislation that "all persons who derive income from mining [should] become subject to tax" and in light of my conclusion that one of the objects of the legislation is to take account of the societal imperative of a fair return to the owner of the resource (the province) in s. 9's  provision for incremental shares of royalties payable by ultimate concession holders on the exploitation of the resource, it would be inconsistent with such broad objectives to interpret the words in s. 9(1)(a) in the narrow manner advocated by Archean.  I agree with counsel for the respondents that the effect of Archean’s argument would lead to an unreasonable and impractical result, namely that a person transferring a licence the day before it matured into a lease would not pay tax, while the same person transferring the licence a day after it had matured, by fulfillment of the conditions for the issuance of a lease, would pay tax.  This would easily lead to a formalistic manipulation of the documentation and its timing to avoid tax that might otherwise be payable.  It would frustrate the overall intent of the legislation. 

 

[46]         I conclude, therefore, that the reasoning of the trial judge that the right to engage in mining operations encompasses the right pursuant to a licence to obtain a mining lease is not inconsistent with the text of the legislation when read in its proper context.  It is also consistent with the broad objective sought to be achieved by the legislation as a remedy for the ills identified by the Royal Commission report that precipitated it.  The interpretation given by the trial judge can therefore be justified in terms of its plausibility (its compliance with the legislative text) and its efficacity (its promotion of the broad legislative purpose).  In my view, it is an interpretation that is also acceptable in the sense that the outcome is reasonable and just.  It ensures that people who trade on the non-renewable mineral resources of the province pay a fair return to the province.  Whilst in some cases it would be unfair to give to a taxation statute an interpretation at variance from that which might reasonably be relied upon by the taxpayer in ordering his or her affairs, this is not one of those cases.  It is highly unlikely that Archean would not have engaged in its search for minerals in this case if it knew in advance that the tax imposed under s. 9 might potentially apply to it.  Nor has it been suggested that Archean would only have contemplated the sale of its right to the royalties to a third person if they were not subject to any tax. 

 

[47]         This is not a case where the social and legal values of protection of vested property rights affect the fairness of the result arrived at by the trial judge.  The nature of this legislation is that it attempts to obtain for the province a fair return for non-renewable resources that are owned by the province. 

 

SUMMARY AND CONCLUSION

 

[48]         The interpretation applied by the trial judge, and his decision resulting therefrom, can be justified in terms of its consistency and promotion of the broad  purpose of the legislation and is not inconsistent with the language of the legislative text, when read in proper context.  Furthermore, in the circumstances of this case the outcome is not unreasonable or unjust. 

 

[49]         Therefore the appeal should be dismissed with costs to the respondents.

O’Neill, J.A.:

 

[52]         This is an appeal from a decision of the Trial Division ordering that a declaration be issued as requested by the respondents, the defendants at trial, that the “Net Smelter Royalty” payable to the appellant, the plaintiff at trial, is taxable under s. 9(1)(a) of the Mining and Mineral Rights Tax Act, R.S.N. 1990, c. M.-16 (the Act).

 

[53]         The facts are not in dispute.

 

[54]         The Appellant, Archean Resources Ltd. (“Archean”) is and was a Newfoundland prospecting company, engaged in the business of mineral exploration. Archean’s principals, Albert Chislett and Chris Verbiski, are the co-discoverers of the Voisey’s Bay nickel-copper-cobalt ore bodies (the “Voisey’s Bay Property”).

 

[55]         Archean holds a contractual royalty entitling it to receive 3% of net smelter returns respecting mineral production from the Voisey’s Bay Property (“the Net Smelter Royalty”).

 

[56]         Archean acquired the Net Smelter Royalty pursuant to a mineral option agreement, (the Labrador Option Agreement), in consideration of the transfer of certain mineral exploration licences to Diamond Fields Resources Inc. (“DFRI”).  The Net Smelter Royalty entitlement under the Labrador Option Agreement was itself negotiated and concluded in 1993.

 

[57]         The mineral licences covering the Voisey’s Bay Property were acquired by DFRI upon exercise of the option conferred by the Labrador Option Agreement.

 

[58]         The position of the Respondents is that the Net Smelter Royalty is taxable under Section 9(1)(a) of the Act. 

 

[59]         It is Archean’s contention that because it acquired the Net Smelter Royalty for the transfer or “grant” of mineral exploration licences, and the transferred mineral licences did not carry with them “the right to engage in mining operations”, Archean is not subject to the Section 9(1)(a) royalty tax.

 

[60]         Archean has been and continues to be approached by parties interested in purchasing the Net Smelter Royalty.  It contends that the respondents’ position on its taxability has a negative impact on the Net Smelter Royalty’s valuation.

 

[61]         Archean sought a declaration in the Court below that the Net Smelter Royalty was not taxable under Section 9(1)(a) of the Act.

 

[62]         The learned trial judge found that on a “remedial and liberal construction”, Archean’s sale or transfer of its mineral exploration licences to DFRI for cash, shares and the right to receive the 3% royalty constituted the grant of a right to engage in mining operations, within the meaning of Section 9(1)(a) of the Act.  The trial judge based his finding on:

 

(i)         mining of the Voisey’s Bay Property can only occur under mining leases issued on the basis of exploration licences transferred from Archean;

 

(ii)        in reality it was the right to obtain a mining licence and thus the right to engage in mining operations which was received when the option was exercised and the exploration licence transfers completed; and

 

(iii)       DFRI’s payment of significant sums of money, the transfer of shares, and their agreement to pay the Net Smelter Royalty was, in substance, payment for the right to engage in mining operations which could only be obtained through mineral exploration licences.

 

[63]         The parties have agreed that the issue before the Court is whether the trial judge erred in his interpretation and findings which allowed the conclusion that Archean’s Net Smelter Royalty was taxable under s. 9(1)(a) of the Act.  This issue can be broken down as follows:

 

(a)        Applying appropriate statutory interpretation principles, can it be said that Archean’s transfer of mineral exploration licences to DFRI, in consideration of which it acquired the Net Smelter Royalty, constitutes the grant of a right to engage in mining operations within the meaning of s. 9(1)(a) of the Act so as to make the Net Smelter Royalty taxable?

 

(b)        Alternatively, in the event that the trial judge’s interpretative approach properly referenced extrinsic materials such as the 1974 Report of the Royal Commission on Mineral Revenue, was Archean within the mischief to which the Act was directed, and, in particular, is Archean properly classified as an earner of unearned income?

 

(c)        Alternatively, in the event that the trial judge’s interpretation is a sustainable one, is Archean entitled to avail of a presumption that, where a taxing statute is not explicit, reasonable uncertainty resulting from a lack of explicitness in the statute should be resolved in its favour because the interpretation contended for by Archean is a reasonable one.

 

Was there a grant of “the right to engage in mining operations” within the meaning of s. 9(1)(a) of the Act?

 

[64]         Sections 2(g), 2(j), and 9(1)(a) of the Act are:

 

(2)    In this Act

 

(g)        “mining operations” means the extraction or production within the province of minerals up to and including primary crushing and includes the transportation, handling, storing, distribution and sale of those minerals, but does not include processing;

 

(j)         “operator” means a person who

 

(i)         has the right to extract minerals, and

 

(ii)        carries out mining operations on or under lands within the province;

 

9(1) A person who receives from

 

(a)        an operator, contractor or other person during a financial year money by way of rental, royalty or similar payment for the grant to the operator, contractor or other person of the right to engage in mining operations; or

 

.....

 

is liable for and shall pay to the minister in the manner and at the time set out in this Act an annual tax of 20% of the net revenue received in consideration of the grant of the right during that financial year.

 

[65]         In his analysis of the principles to be applied in the interpretation of statutes, and, in particular to the interpretation of the Act, a taxing statute, the trial judge referred to s. 16 of the Interpretation Act, c. 1-19 R.S.N., to Pierre-André Côté’s The Interpretation of Legislation in Canada, Second  Edition, and to Driedger on The Construction of Statutes, Third Edition, by Ruth Sullivan, two authoritative texts.

 

[66]         The Interpretation Act, and particularly s. 16 thereof, is the authority which must always be employed in the interpretation of the statutes of this Province.  Section 16 is as follows:

 

Every Act and every regulation and every provision of an Act or regulation shall be considered remedial and shall receive the liberal construction and interpretation that best ensures the attainment of the objects of the Act, regulation, or provision according to its true meaning.

 

[67]         In Côté at pp. 409-10, is the following:

 

Should taxation statutes be interpreted today in the same way as other legislation, or are they still subject to the special rules of strict or literal interpretation?  Several reasons suggest that the traditional method should be abandoned.  The exclusively literal approach to interpretation is on the wane; the real meaning of words can only be discovered by reading them in their context, and therefore in the light of the legislation’s objectives.  The traditional idea that tax statutes only serve for the collection of revenue is obsolete.  Taxation legislation is frequently a means of social and economic intervention.  In the context of the modern welfare state, the nature of taxation has changed ....

 

In order to reflect accurately recent taxation case law, it is necessary to distinguish between literal and strict interpretation.  As a rule, taxation statutes are no longer subject to a strictly literal construction.  They are interpreted in the same way as other types of legislation (according to the contextual, purposive or historical approach, etc.)  But if after a thorough effort there are still reasonable doubts as to the enactment’s meaning, the statute will generally be applied to limit its effect and favour the tax payer.

 

[68]         In Driedger, at p. 131, the modern rule of statutory interpretation is set out:

 

There is only one rule in modern interpretation, namely, courts are obliged to determine the meaning of legislation in its total context, having regard to the purpose of the legislation, the consequences of proposed interpretations, the presumptions and special rules of interpretation, as well as admissible external aids.  In other words, the courts must consider and take into account all relevant and admissible indicators of legislative meaning.  After taking these into account, the court must then adopt an interpretation that is appropriate.  An appropriate interpretation is one that can be justified in terms of (a) its plausibility, that is, its compliance with the legislative text; (b) its efficacy, that is, its promotion of the legislative purpose; and (c) its acceptability, that is, the outcome is reasonable and just.

 

[69]         The trial judge concluded from his perusal of the authorities that the traditional strict and literal interpretation of taxing statutes has “yielded to the doctrine that taxing statutes are to be treated in the same manner as other legislation, with the proviso that in cases of reasonable doubt, the enactment is construed in favour of the subject”. 

 

[70]         The appellant acknowledges in its argument that the literal approach to the interpretation of statutes no longer applies and that the “proper interpretative approach to taxing statutes” is that stated in Stubart Investments Ltd. v. The Queen (1984), 10 D.L.R. 1 (S.C.C.), but that the approach mandated there is “nevertheless subject to the ‘plain meaning’ rule that when statutory language is clear, it should be given its plain and ordinary meaning, and resort to interpretative devices or extrinsic aids is inappropriate”.

 

[71]         In Stubart, Estey J. for the majority of the Supreme Court of Canada said at p. 32:

 

While not directing his observations exclusively to tax statutes, the learned author of Construction of Statutes, 2nd Ed. (1983), at p. 87, E.A. Dreidger, put the modern rule succinctly:

 

Today there is only one principle or approach, namely, the words of an act are to be read in their entire context and in their grammatical and ordinary sense harmoniously with the scheme of the Act, the object of the Act, and the intention of Parliament.

 

[72]         In support of its position, however, the appellant refers to R. v. Multiform Manufacturing Co. Ltd.  (1990), 79 C.R. (3d) 390, a decision of the Supreme Court of Canada, decided some five years after Stubart.  There, Lamer C.J.C., for the Court said at p. 394:

 

When the courts are called upon to interpret a statute, their task is to discover the intention of Parliament.  When the words used in a statute are clear and unambiguous, no further step is needed to identify the intention of Parliament.  There is no need for further construction when Parliament has clearly expressed its intention in the words it has used in the statute.  As Maxwell stated in The Interpretation of Statutes, 12th ed. (1969), at pp. 28-29:

 

If there is nothing to modify, alter or qualify the language which the statute contains, it must be construed in the ordinary and natural meaning of the words and sentences.  “The safer and more correct course of dealing with a question of construction is to take the words themselves and arrive if possible at their meaning without, in the first instance, reference to cases.

 

[73]         The appellant also referred, in support of its position, inter alia, to R. v. McCraw, [1991] 3 S.C.R. 72, Sunnyside Nursing Home v. Builders Contract Management Ltd., [1990] 5 W.W.R., 289 (Sask. C.A.) and R. v. Wonderland Gifts Ltd. (1996), 140 Nfld. & P.E.I.R. 219, a decision of this Court.   In Wonderland Gifts, Marshall J.A. of this Court said:

 

... where a legislative provision is clear and plain, the meaning governs and the courts may not, under the guise of interpretation, second guess the wisdom of the legislature by adopting implausible constructions not in harmony with its intent and object or attempt to fill gaps in the legislative scheme.

 

Later, Marshall J.A., on p. 231, adopted the words of Denning L.J. in Asher v. Seaford Court Estates, [1949] 2 K.B. 481 (C.A.) on the obligation placed upon a trial judge in interpreting a statute where there is an ambiguity:

 

He must set to work on the constructive task of finding the intention of Parliament, and he must do so not only from the language of the statute, but also from a consideration of the social conditions which gave rise to it, and of the mischief which it was passed to remedy, and then he must supplement the written word so as to give “force and life” to the intention of the legislature.

 

[74]         The appellant’s position was that the language of s. 9(1)(a) is “clear and unambiguous” and does “not admit of the remedial and liberal construction imposed by” the trial judge.  Further, it argued that the statutory provisions applicable to the required analysis were themselves, “plain and unambiguous”.

 

[75]         By way of summary, Archean’s submission was that “in cases of clear and unambiguous statutory language, the scheme, object and intention of Parliament as adverted to in Stubart are apparent and yield the proper meaning based on the plain and ordinary meaning of the statutory language”.

 

[76]         Although Multiform might appear to support the Archean argument, the passage quoted still directs the court “to discover the intention of Parliament”.  Further, the words must be “clear and unambiguous”, which is not here the case.  Even if they were, then that principle has not been followed by the Supreme Court in subsequent cases and the contemporary principle as set out in Stubart and followed in Rizzo and Gladue to which reference will be made later has certainly replaced it.

 

[77]         The appellant has also argued that the “remedial and liberal” interpretation approach ignores the presumption that the legislature does not intent to encroach upon the private property rights of subjects.  I cannot see any merit to this argument in the particular circumstances here.  The main purpose of the Act is stated clearly in its full title: An Act To Impose Taxes On Income From Mining Operations Within The Province And On Income Obtained Or Derived From Persons Holding Rights To Mine”.  That purpose is manifested in s. 9 of the Act where clearly, persons or institutions are to pay a tax.  Whether the person liable to pay the tax is the appellant is another question.  Certainly, there is a clear intention that taxes be paid and hence a clear intention to affect rights.

 

[78]         For the respondent, it was argued that the “plain meaning” approach, isolated from the whole context of the legislation, no longer commands respect.  Counsel for the respondent relied on the principle set out in at p. 430 of Driedger:

 

It is sometimes said that the courts should not look to extrinsic materials, even though the materials otherwise would be admissible, unless the legislation to be interpreted is ambiguous or unclear.  It is arguable that this constraint is pointless and misleading and should be abandoned.

 

The constraint is pointless because, given the plasticity of language and the complexity of law, virtually all legislation is ambiguous in that it is susceptible of more than one reading.  There is little point in a test that virtually all legislation meets.  The constraint also is misleading in that it perpetuates the empty rhetoric of the plain meaning rule.  Under current judicial practice, to say that a provision is not ambiguous, that its meaning is clear or “plain”, is a conclusion reached at the end of interpretation, not a threshold test.  It is a judgment that can appropriately be made only in light of all the available evidence of legislative meaning.

 

[79]         The whole thrust of this argument is that the plain meaning of a word, words or expressions, however “plain” they may appear to be, can only be ascertained when looked at and examined in the light of the context in which they are used which must include the whole purpose and object of the statute in which they appear.  This argument finds further support in R. v. Adams, [1995] 4 S.C.R. 707.  There,  Sopinka J., for the Supreme Court of Canada, said at p. 719:

 

In approaching the interpretation of any statutory provision, it is prudent to keep in mind the simple but fundamental instruction offered by the court in Reigate Rural District Council v. Sutton District Water Co. (1908), 99 L.T.R. 168 (K.B.), at p. 170, and affirmed by this Court in Hirsch v. Protestant Board of School Commissioners, [1926] S.C.R. 246:

 

... it is always necessary in construing a statute, and in dealing with the words you find in it, to consider the object with which the statute was passed, because it enables one to understand the meaning of the words introduced into the enactment.

 

This well settled rule of statutory interpretation has continued to be followed by this Court to the present time.

 

[80]         The respondents’ position is that the words at issue in s. 9 of the Act are general ordinary words which have not been given nor have they acquired any particular meaning in the context of the mineral rights regime in this jurisdiction and the court then must define and interpret that meaning in accordance with the modern or pragmatic methodology.  Counsel for the respondent also referred the Court to Rizzo & Rizzo Shoes Ltd. (Re), [1998] 1 S.C.R. 27 where Iacobucci J. for the court said, at p. 41, with respect to the interpretation of statutes:

 

... Elmer Driedger in Construction of Statutes, (2nd ed., 1983) best encapsulates the approach upon which I prefer to rely.  He recognizes that statutory interpretation cannot be founded on the wording of the legislation alone.  At p. 87 he states:

 

... Today there is only one principle or approach, namely, the words of an Act are to be read in their entire context and in their grammatical and ordinary sense harmoniously with the scheme of the Act, the object of the Act, and the intention of Parliament.

 

[81]         In R. v. Gladue, [1999] 1 S.C.R. 688, the Court was considering an amendment to the Criminal Code codifying, for the first time, the fundamental purpose and principles of sentencing and specifically dealing with s. 718.2(e), the effect of which the Court found was to alter the method of analysis to be used in determining a fit sentence for an aboriginal offender.  The decision of the Court was delivered by Cory and Iacobucci JJ.   The following is to be found at p. 704:

 

As this Court has frequently stated, the proper construction of a statutory provision flows from reading the words of the provision in their grammatical and ordinary sense and in their entire context, harmoniously with the scheme of the statute as a whole, the purpose of the statute, and the intention of Parliament.  The purpose of the statute and the intention of Parliament, in particular, are to be determined on the basis of intrinsic and admissible extrinsic sources regarding the Act’s legislative history and the context of its enactment: Rizzo & Rizzo Shoes Ltd. (Re), [1998] 1 S.C.R. 27, at paras. 20-23; R. v. Chartrand, [1994] 2 S.C.R. 864 at p. 875; E.A. Driedger, Construction of Statutes (2nd Ed. 1983) at p. 87; Driedger on the Construction of Statutes (3rd Ed. 1994) by R. Sullivan, at p. 131.

 

In its decision the Court considered the wording of s. 718(2) and the general scheme of the part of the Criminal Code in which it appears, its legislative history, the social context surrounding its enactment, including the “overrepresentation” of aboriginal Canadians in penal institutions.

 

[82]         In my view, the law is clear that, faced with the task of interpreting words or expressions in a statute, the court or judge must, before coming to a conclusion as to what these specific words or expressions means, consider them in “their entire context, harmoniously with the scheme of the statute ... its purpose and the intention of the legislature”.  Further, the statute must be considered in the light of s. 16 of the Interpretation Act (assuming the court is dealing with a Newfoundland statute), which requires that the Act and every provision thereof be considered remedial and shall receive the liberal construction and interpretation that best assures the attainment of the objects of the Act and provisions, according to its true meaning.

 

[83]         The trial judge, having set out the facts and referred to the various rules of interpretation already canvassed, continued:

 

I therefore propose to interpret section 9 and the Mining and Mineral Rights Tax Act as a whole, according to the contemporary or modern approach which is also in my opinion the approach mandated by the Interpretation Act.  To do so it is proper for me to consider materials touching on the objectives of the Act and the objectives of the Legislature in enacting its provisions.  To put it another way, was there a “mischief” which the Legislature was attempting to rectify.  For these purposes I am entitled to have recourse to the report of a Royal Commission on mineral revenues and to a lesser and very limited extent, to a statement as to the objects of the legislation as expressed by the Minister of Mines and Energy in 1975 during debate in the House of Assembly.

 

[84]         Clearly, the trial judge rejected the argument of the appellant that the plain meaning rule should be applied in the interpretation of the Act.

 

[85]         The trial judge reviewed the pre-1975 mineral concession regime in the Province and set out some of the observations of the report of the “Royal Commission on Mineral Revenue” which was presented in December 1974.  He concluded:

 

It is clear from the Commission’s observations and recommendations, that one of the major ills of the pre-1975 concession system was that the concession holders who had the right to explore and mine, but did not usually do so, were in receipt of significant sums of “unearned” income which was not subject to mining taxes.  Though all of the recommendations of the Royal Commission were not embodied in the 1975 legislation, it is clear that the thrust of the recommendations gave rise to the taxing procedures provided for in the Mining and Mineral Rights Taxing Act and particularly in section 9.

 

[86]         The trial judge also referred to Hansard, the record of debates in the House of Assembly, and quoted from a statement of the Minister of Mines and Energy in the House of Assembly, made during the debates on the Bill which would later become the Act:

 

... whereas the first part imposes a mining tax, the second part imposes what we would call a mineral rights tax.

 

This is done, Mr. Speaker, in recognition of the fact as set out by the Royal Commission on Mineral Revenue that there was significant revenue being derived by certain companies who had originally held mining rights and who had transferred these to other companies, significant revenue being received by such companies that was not subject to the mining tax and where a government was not receiving a share of such revenue.  So, we have put forth, incorporated the principle in this bill that in addition to the normal mining tax that both operators, and we will call them landlords, both operators and people who receive rentals, royalties and like payment from operating companies, that both of these types of companies or individuals should pay a mineral rights tax, a tax on revenue that they derived from the right to mine, the right to mine that they have obtained from the people of this Province.

 

The trial judge concluded that Hansard makes clear the intent to tax companies, such as the appellant, which receive royalties from “the right to mine”.

 

[87]         Counsel for the appellant had also argued that since the Act is a taxing statute, the burden of proof that tax was exigible lay with the government and cited CUQ v. Corp. Notre-Dame De Bon-Secours, [1994] 3 S.C.R. 3 in support of this position. The principle is set out in the decision of Gonthier J. at p. 15 where he said:

 

According to the general rule which provides that the burden of proof lies with the plaintiff, in any proceeding it is for the party claiming the benefit of a legislative provision to show that he is entitled to rely on it.  The burden of proof thus rests with the tax department in the case of a provision imposing a tax obligation and with the taxpayer in the case of a provision creating a tax exemption.

 

While I agree with the principle, I cannot see its application here in light of the very clear findings made by the trial judge.  Obviously, if at the end of his very thorough examination of the circumstances and the law, the trial judge was not able to decide whether Archean was liable for the tax, then since the burden of proof would be on the government, the tax on the royalty would not be applicable.  Clearly, that is not the case here.

 

[88]         The trial judge, having referred to the “historical background of mining concessions” in the province as provided by the 1973 Royal Commission, and a reading of the legislation which followed, concluded that the legislative provisions for exploratory licences, mining licences and the tax on royalties were designed to avoid the undesirable practices of the past whereby concession holders received substantial royalty payments (described as “unearned royalties”) from operators, without tax liability, and that it was these royalties at which the legislation was aimed.

 

[89]         In his analysis, the trial judge stated, in my view correctly, that the interpretation of s. 9 of the Act was the real issue.  He said:

 

Can it then be said that in 1995 Archean transferred to Diamond Fields and its successors, “the right to engage in mining operations”, in the context of the Act.  At first blush and on a narrow construction of the wording, one might conclude that because an exploration licence is one entity and a mining lease is another, that the transfer was not the transfer of the right to engage in mining operations.  However, after review and reflection, I have concluded otherwise.

 

[90]         Earlier in his judgment, the trial judge had referred to the several provisions of the Mineral Act, R.S.N. 1990, c-M 12, and set out several of the relevant sections of that Act.  He noted that the Mineral Act provided for a system whereby persons, on complying with stipulated procedures, could be granted exploration licences authorizing the recipient to engage in mineral exploration on specified areas.  He noted that Archean obtained a significant number of exploration licences in areas of Labrador and had begun exploration.  He added:

 

It has been explained to me that exploration companies such as Archean rarely have the expertise and capital to develop promising fields and therefore they seek out or may be approached by, junior mining companies who may wish to purchase their interests.  Such companies in turn seek to involve what are referred to as senior mining companies, which have the technical and financial resources to develop mines.

 

The foregoing is what happened in the present case when it had been determined that “Voisey’s Bay” is one of the most significant nickel/copper/cobalt deposits in the world.

 

[91]         Exploration licences are issued under s. 22 of the Mineral Act; that section is not relevant here.  What is relevant however, is s. 23 of that act:

 

23.(1)  Subject to the terms under which a licence is issued, the licensee has the exclusive right to explore for minerals, in, on or under the area of land described in the licence from the date of issuing for the period of

 

(a)        in the case of a licence covering lands open to staking, 5 years; and

 

(b)        in the case of a licence covering reserved lands, 4 years.

 

     (2)  A licence confers no right to remove minerals except for the purpose of sampling, assaying and testing, and a licensee who removes or sells minerals in contravention of this section

 

(a)        commits a material breach of the terms of the licence; and

 

(b)        is guilty of an offence under this Act.

 

[92]         It is not necessary to set out the various steps in the Mineral Act whereby mineral exploration licence may be obtained.  Suffice it to say that, subject to the terms under which a licence is issued, the licencee has the exclusive right to explore for minerals in the area described in the licence but, as set out in s. 23(2), has no right to remove minerals except for “sampling, assaying and testing”.


 

[93]         Section 24 of the Mineral Act permits the transfer of an exploration licence and is as follows:

 

24.  Nothing in this Act shall prevent the holder of a licence from transferring, assigning, mortgaging, pledging or conveying that licence to another person if section 6 is complied with, but a transfer, assignment, mortgage, pledge or conveyance of the minerals in, on or under part of the area covered by the licence is not valid unless the written approval of the minister is obtained and is filed with the document presented for registration under section 6.

 

Section 6 deals with the registration of, inter alia, transfers, assignments, mortgages and agreements relating to minerals and rights with respect thereto.  It is not relevant for our purposes.

 

[94]         On what basis can it be said that Archean’s transfer of its mineral exploration licences to DFRI, as permitted by s. 24 of the Mineral Act, was a “grant ... of the right to engage in mining operations”, as contemplated by s. 9 of the Act, so as to attract tax on the royalty payable thereunder, which here would be when mining operations commence?  Obviously, at the time the transfer was made to DFRI, nobody, including Archean, had the right to engage in mining operations on the lands covered by the exploration licences and it follows that, there being no right to engage in mining operations, there could be no grant of mining operations.

 

[95]         Earlier in his decision, in considering the arguments of counsel, the trial judge had stated that although an exploration licence does not, in itself, entitle the holder of it to a mining lease, on compliance with s. 31 of the Mineral Act, which would include the licence holder having complied with all the terms of the licence during its currency, a mining lease would be granted.

 

[96]         There is no suggestion that any of the requirements in s. 31 are not reasonably capable of completion by the holder of a licence.  Further, upon compliance with the Mineral Act, the holder of the licence has under s. 31, “a right to the issuing of a mining lease ...”.

 

[97]         Clearly, the option agreement anticipated that on completion of the transfer of the exploration licences, mining operations would be carried on since part of the payment for the transfer, the royalty, could only come into existence on the carrying out of mining operations producing “net smelter returns”.  In effect then, what was being transferred from Archean for good and valuable consideration was the right, albeit after compliance with certain statutory provisions under the Mineral Act, to obtain a mining lease and “the right to engage in mining operations”.

 

[98]         With respect to the question posed by the appellant as to whether Archean was within the “mischief” to which the Act was directed and in particular, whether Archean can be properly classified as an earner of unearned income, it can fairly be said that that question has already been answered in the remedial and liberal interpretation given to s. 9.  True, as argued by the appellant, s. 9(1) was enacted “in response to the recommendations of the Royal Commission” to which reference has already been made.  Archean argues, with some justification, that it is in a completely different position from that of the several companies and groups referred to earlier which had acquired mining rights from the Province and had granted or subleased them to mine operators in exchange for royalties and other consideration, which in effect, were “unearned”.  In that regard, Archean did not acquire a right to mine, it acquired prospecting or exploration rights and actually discovered a valuable mineral property.  It could have, had it wished, applied for and obtained a mining lease.  It chose to transfer its exploration licences to another group which would then enable that other group to apply for and obtain a mining lease.  It was this choice which set up the right in Archean to receive, when mining operations would begin, a royalty. 

 

[99]         Whether Archean would be a recipient of “unearned royalties” is not important for our purposes.  The Act is cast broadly and imposes a tax on any person who receives “money by way of rental, royalty or similar payment” in consideration of a grant to another of “the right to engage in mining operations ...”.  The Act does not in any way limit the tax to those who had, in the past obtained mining concessions even though they had been identified by the commission as the principal reason why the Crown was not receiving appropriate revenues from mining operations in the Province.

 

[100]     Although there were several references as set out earlier to concession holders as landlords, that word, in my view, was used in an illustrative sense and should not be taken as an indication that some property rights must be maintained before the tax becomes applicable under s. 9(1)(a) of the Act.  The tax is payable on the royalty received even where that royalty is payable strictly on a contractual basis, as here.

 

[101]     The third issue raised by the appellant in its factum can also be disposed of readily.  I have already concluded, as did the trial judge, that the transfer of the exploration licence was a transfer of the right to engage in mining operations so as to bring Archean within the purview of s. 9 of the Act.  Reference has already been made to the “first blush”, “narrow construction”, and “might conclude”,  statement by the trial judge.  Certainly, on a proper interpretation of the Act, it cannot be said that there was any reasonable uncertainty resulting from lack of explicitness in the statute so that this Court could conclude that Archean was not liable for the tax.

 

[102]     It follows, in my view, that Archean is liable to pay the tax on the net smelter royalty to be received by it when mining operations begin.
