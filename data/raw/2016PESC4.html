I. Motions to strike out the plaintiffs= statement of claim

 

[12]           Both the government and the defendant Paul Jenkins filed motions seeking to strike numerous paragraphs of the plaintiffs= statement of claim, with Paul Jenkins seeking to strike the claim in its entirety.  No motion to strike was filed by the third defendant, Garth Jenkins. The government and Paul Jenkins make many similar arguments with respect to the alleged violations of pleading, but their arguments frequently relate to different paragraphs within the statement of claim.  However, the general principles to be considered apply to each such motion.

 

[13]           Rule 25 of the Rules of Civil Procedure, entitled APleadings in an Action@, sets out the fundamental requirements of any pleading. The first Apleading@ in any action is the statement of claim issued by the plaintiff.  Rule 25.06 (1) states:

 

25.06 (1)   Every pleading shall contain a concise statement of the material facts on which the party relies for his or her claim or defence, but not the evidence by which those facts are to be proved.

 

[14]           That Rule provides the basis for determining what a proper pleading should and should not contain.  A statement of claim (or a statement of defence) should not become a rambling narrative of the relationship between the parties but should clearly and concisely recite only essential or material facts necessary to base a claim. Superfluous, irrelevant, extraneous, repetitive, or immaterial statements are not to be tolerated. Rule 25.11 states:

 

25.11   The court may strike out or expunge all or part of a pleading or other document, with or without leave to amend, on the ground that the pleading or other document,

 

(a)   may prejudice or delay the fair trial of the action;

 

(b)   is scandalous, frivolous or vexatious; or

 

(c)   is an abuse of the process of the court.

 

[15]           Rule 21.01(1)(b) provides that a party may move before a judge to strike out a pleading on the ground that it discloses no reasonable cause of action or defence.  Rule 21.01(3)(d) provides that a defendant may move before judge to have an action stayed or dismissed on the ground that the action is frivolous or vexatious or is otherwise an abuse of the process of the court.

 

[16]           Taylor J. identifies other factors limiting the parameters of pleadings in his decision in Ayangma v. Prince Edward Island, 2005 PESCTD 25, which was cited by both the plaintiffs and the defendants. At paras. 20-24, he states:

 

20     In addition to the provisions under the Rules of Civil Procedure, the court has an inherent jurisdiction to strike pleadings which are oppressive, frivolous, vexatious, embarrassing or an abuse of the process of the court (see Reichel v. Magrath (1889), 14 App. Cas. 665 referred to in Casson, Odgers on High Court Pleading and Practice, (1991); R. v. Mills (1983), 3 C.R.R. 63 (Ont. H.C.) at p. 78; R. v. Osborn [1969] 1 O.R. 152 at 155, quoting Haggard v. Pelicier Freres, [1892] A.C. 61 at pp. 67‑68).

 

21     "Frivolous", "vexatious", "abuse of the process of the court" (not to be confused with the tort of abuse of process), and "embarrassing" are to some extent terms of art in the law. They bear their common usage meanings together with specific meanings attributed to them in the operation of the law.

 

22     The terms are sometimes used interchangeably, and their meanings are not closed; they often appear to overlap and are fluid and fact based (see Margem Chartering Co. v. Cosena S.R.L., [1997] 2 F.C. 1001 (Fed. T.D.), (1997) 127 F.T.R. 161 at 169; in ATL Industries Inc. v. Han Eol Ind. Co., [1995], 36 C.P.C. (3d) 288 (Ont. Gen. Div.); Farley J. at p. 314 referring to House of Spring Gardens Ltd. v. Wait, [1990] 3 W.L.R. 347 (C.A.)] at pp. 358‑359; McIlKenny v. Chief Constable of West Midlands Police (sub nom. Hunter v. Chief Constable of West Midlands Police), [1982] A.C. 529 at 536, and Foy v. Foy (No. 2) (1979), 26 O.R. (2d) 220 (C.A.); George v. Harris, [2000] O.J. No. 1762 (S.C.J.)).

 

23     Among the meanings attached to all these terms are variations on the theme that the pleading is without merit, or asks for relief that cannot or should not be given, or discloses no reasonable cause of action or defence. If this were all the terms meant, it would not be necessary to include them in the Rules, as Rule 21.01(1)(b) already empowers the court to strike out a pleading that discloses no reasonable cause of action or defence.

 

24     In addition, however, these terms are used to describe violations of the rules of pleadings and procedural violations. When used in these circumstances, the terms are employed to describe pleadings which:

 

               a) violate the rules of pleadings;

 

             b) are confused, rambling, ambiguous or difficult to understand;

 

             c) contain irrelevant or immaterial facts, or evidence as opposed to                 facts;

 

             d) are redundant or unnecessarily prolix;

 

             e) are vindictive or used for an ulterior purpose;   

 

             f) would involve the parties in a dispute that is wholly apart from                  the issues;

 

             g) contain opinions or argument;          

 

             h) while technically permissible, are still contrary to the intent of                   the rules of pleading.  

 

 

[17]           Further, at para. 49 of Ayangma, after reviewing numerous prior decisions of the Supreme Court and Court of Appeal of Prince Edward Island, Taylor J. states:

 

49     The problems identified in the Prince Edward Island cases which may lead to having a statement of claim being struck as frivolous, vexatious, an abuse of process, or something which may prejudice or delay the fair trial of the action are:

 

        1) unnecessary length;

 

        2) improper pleading of evidence rather than material facts to support a party's claim;

 

        3) difficulty in ascertaining what the plaintiff seeks;

 

        4) repeated breach of the rules of pleading ("plain and obvious");

 

        5) impossibility of fixing the claim by a series of amendments;

 

        6) whether a series of amendments would make of it a new statement of claim;

 

        7) whether the defendant may be embarrassed or prejudiced in meeting the claim;

 

        8) whether the claim pleads "facts" which are speculative.

 

 

[18]           There is a substantial difference between a dispute which plays itself out in public and one that is presented to the courts for resolution. A public dispute may be limited by little more than the laws of defamation. However, for a legal dispute presented to the courts, there are some significant and purposeful terms of engagement.  Those terms of engagement will assist in determining which, if any, statements in the statement of claim are to be struck out.

 

 

 

A. The government=s motion to strike, in part, the statement of claim

 

[19]           The government moves to strike numerous paragraphs of the statement of claim on the grounds:

 

a)         the statement of claim violates the rules of pleading;

b)         the allegations contained in the statement of claim may delay the fair trial of an action;

c)         the allegations contained in the statement of claim are scandalous, frivolous, or vexatious;

d)         the allegations contained in the statement of claim are an abuse of process;

e)         the allegations contained in the statement of claim disclosed no reasonable cause of action; and

f)         Rules 25 and 21 of the Rules of Civil Procedure.

 

[20]           In their submissions, the government grouped the impugned paragraphs by the nature of the purported pleadings violation and presented their arguments accordingly.

 

[21]           At the outset I will make the comment that the provisions of the prayer for relief contained in paragraphs 1(a) through 1(d) of the statement of claim do not clearly spell out the total amount claimed against each of the 11 separate defendants.

 

[22]           Paragraph 1(a) is a claim of $25,000,000.00 against one defendant only, the Government of Prince Edward Island, for breaches of contractual obligations. That is clear. 

 

[23]           However, paragraph 1(b) claims  $25,000,000,00  Aalternatively, against all of the Defendants@ for conspiracy.  I am uncertain if that claim is for a collective $25 million or, given there are 11 defendants, whether the plaintiffs= total claim is for $275 million. 

 

[24]           Paragraph 1(c) also claims $25,000,000,00 against Aall the Defendants@ for breach of confidentiality, raising the same question about the extent of the plaintiffs= claim.

 

[25]           Paragraph 1(d) claims Aagainst the Defendants, Garth Jenkins and Paul Jenkins, for $25 million@. Again it is ambiguous as to whether that claim is for $25 million against each of the two named defendants, or for a collective $25 million.

 

[26]           Obviously, the plaintiffs are claiming a large sum of money. The defendants, individually, have the right to know the full extent of the claims they are facing. For example, Paul Jenkins is potentially facing a claim of $75 million. Reading the claim another way, he might only be required to defend claims on a shared basis.  Clarification is required.

 

i) a wholesale violation of the rules of pleading

 

[27]           The government submits that, overall, the statement of claim is so full of immaterial facts, evidence, argument and opinion that it constitutes Aa wholesale violation of the rules of pleading@ and ought to be struck out in its entirety.

 

[28]           They note firstly that the statement of claim stretches to 55 pages containing 283 paragraphs, which is anything but concise.  They submit that filing a Along rambling narrative document@ as a pleading is a clear violation of the rules of pleading, particularly the principles of brevity and materiality, and constitutes a legal embarrassment.

 

ii) pleading irrelevant and immaterial facts

 

[29]           Counsel for the government identified paragraphs containing information which they submit is clearly irrelevant to the claim. That information, contained in paragraphs 32, 33, and 63 of the statement of claim, speaks of family relations, and engagement, marriage, vacations, wedding plans, wedding gifts, etc.  I find those paragraphs to be irrelevant to any of the claims presented, and they ought to be struck out.

 

iii) pleading evidence

 

[30]           The government then refers to eight paragraphs which they allege violate the rule against pleading evidence. Specifically those paragraphs are 70, 79-81, 145, 181, 183, and 215. Those paragraphs consist mostly of lengthy verbatim quotations from email correspondence or from documents. The paragraphs go well beyond pleading material facts and plead the evidence which might be offered in support of material facts.  As was stated in Fairbanks v. Nova Scotia (Attorney General),  (2000) 190 N.S.R. (2d) 120, 2000 CarswellNS 399, in reference to some letters which were quoted verbatim in a statement of claim,  A... pleading evidence is giving the court an incomplete picture because such evidence may have been chosen by a selection process which may not give the court a complete picture. There is also to be considered the letters may be ruled not admissible at trial. One does not know this until the trial is in process@.  I find those paragraphs violate the rule against pleading evidence and ought to be struck out.

 

iv) pleading opinion and argument

 

[31]           Opinions and argument are matters for counsel to address in their submissions to the court. They are not to be contained in a statement of claim.  The dozen paragraphs cited which the government submits violate the rule against pleading argument and opinion are paragraphs 248, 252, and 256-265.  By way of example, paragraph 248 alleges an individual Atook the highly unusual step@ of taking a particular action. Similarly, in paragraph 252, it references a certain action taken and states it Awas purely an economic decision@. In numerous other paragraphs the statement of claim alleges one or more individuals Alied@ in making particular statements.  These statements may well be views held by the plaintiffs, but such arguments and opinions are not appropriate pleadings and ought to be struck out.

 

v) pleading privileged statements

 

[32]           Included in the statement of claim, at paragraph 259, is a summary of a statement allegedly made on the floor of the Prince Edward Island Legislative Assembly.

 

[33]           The Ontario Superior Court of Justice addressed the question of privilege applying to statements made during the course of proceedings in a legislative body.  In Ontario v. Rothmans Inc., 2014 ONSC 3382, at para. 15, the court confirmed, Ait is well-established that statements made by a person during the course of parliamentary proceedings cannot be used against the person in a civil action.@ 

 

[34]           The plaintiffs acknowledge that the statement made in the provincial legislature is subject to parliamentary privilege and could not be used in support of a claim against the individual making the statement. However the plaintiffs expressed their intention to use the statement in their claim against the government, not against any individual. At para. 21 of the Rothmans decision, the court stated:

 

21.   The parties acknowledge that parliamentary privilege can be waived. However, the privilege belongs to Parliament and therefore it is up to Parliament - not the person who made the statement - to decide whether privilege is to be waived in a particular case.

 

[35]           And at para. 31, the court stated:

 

31     Third, because the immunity is absolute, any self‑serving motivations of the person participating before the committees would not affect the privilege. Motive is irrelevant to an absolute privilege. In Roman Corp. v. Hudson's Bay Oil & Gas Co., the court struck out a statement of claim noting that it had "no power to inquire into what statements were made in Parliament, why they were made, who made them, what was the motive for making them or anything about them".

(Emphasis added).

 

[36]           No waiver of privilege was given in this case. Paragraph 259 ought to be struck out.

 

vi) pleadings which are scandalous

 

[37]           Paragraph 277 names individuals who are not parties to the action and alleges they conspired with various defendants to file unsubstantiated allegations with the Superintendent of Securities for the purposes of discrediting the plaintiffs and CMT=s principal, Paul Maines.  The government submits this pleading is scandalous, in the legal sense.  A pleading can be scandalous when it is offensive, irrelevant, or, in addition, as in this case, constitutes a collateral attack asserting a cause of action against a non-party, leaving the non-party no ability to answer to the claim.  Taylor J. also addressed this issue in Ayangma, supra, where the statement of claim included accusations against non-parties, alleging they engaged in a conspiracy.  At para. 50(l) he stated:

 

50 (l) The statement of claim is scandalous at many points (Rule 25.11(b)). It accuses the Human Rights Commission (paragraphs 129, 158) and the French and Eastern School Board (paragraph 129) of conspiracy, and the PEI legal community (paragraphs 127, 128, 182, 234, 251, 254, 276, 290, 291) of malice, abuse of court process, and possibly defamation. These bodies are not parties to the action, and the claim is therefore scandalous in the legal sense, meaning that it is both offensive and irrelevant.

 

[38]           I find paragraph 277 to be scandalous, and it ought to be struck out.

 

vii) pleadings which disclose no reasonable cause of action (contract)

 

[39]           Paragraph 1(a) of the statement of claim reads:

 

1.  The Plaintiffs claim:

 

(a)  against the Defendant, Government of Prince Edward Island, damages for breach of its good faith performance of contract and failure to act honestly in the performance of its contractual obligations in the sum of $25,000,000.00.  

 

[40]           The government attacks this paragraph alleging it discloses no reasonable cause of action.  With respect to such an allegation, the court is to assess only the pleadings and, if the court concludes it is Aplain and obvious@ that no cause of action is disclosed, the claim ought to be struck out. The court is not to consider any additional evidence when such an argument is raised.

 

[41]           The Acontract@ referred to in paragraph 1(a) is identified in paragraph 170 of the statement of claim as being a Memorandum of Understanding between Innovation PEI and CMT=s wholly-owned subsidiary, 7645686, operating as TBT.

 

[42]           Firstly, the claim is expressed as being made by the APlaintiffs@, plural.  It is plain and obvious from the balance of the statement of claim that only one plaintiff, 7645686, purports to have been involved in a contract. That being said, CMT would have no recourse pursuant to the contract, and its claim relating to that contract ought to be struck out. 

 

[43]           Secondly, in paragraph 274 of the statement of claim, the plaintiffs state certain officers or agents of the government Adid not act honestly in the performance of  their contractual obligations to the plaintiffs@.  It is plain and obvious from the pleadings that the individuals named in paragraph 274 did not assume any contractual obligations.  If such a contract is proven, it can only be proven against the parties to that contract. None of the individuals named were parties to the contract. The plaintiffs= claim discloses no reasonable cause of action against the individuals, and to the extent such a claim is made, it shall be struck out.

 

[44]           The plaintiffs cite Bhasin v. Hyrnew, 2014 SCC 71, for the proposition there is a general duty of honesty in contractual performance. However, while the Supreme Court of Canada did recognize a new common law duty in that case, that duty applies only to the parties to the contract, and does not apply to non-parties.

 

[45]           Similarly, paragraph 1(c) of the statement of claim reads:

 

1.  The Plaintiffs claim:

 

(c)  against all the Defendants, damages for breach of confidentiality in the sum of $25,000,000.00.

 

[46]           That breach of confidentiality is fleshed out to some extent in paragraph 281 of the statement of claim which alleges various government officials Arepeatedly violated the confidentiality provisions of the MOU@.  As with the allegation in paragraph 274 that various officials failed to act honestly in the performance of Atheir@ contractual obligations, paragraph 281 ignores the material facts set out in the statement of claim at paragraph 170, which confirms that the only parties to the MOU were Innovation PEI and 7645686.  There is no cause of action against the individual officers of the government as is claimed in paragraph 281. The individuals identified were not parties to the contract. Both paragraph 281 and the claim for damages set out in paragraph 1(c) of the claim ought to be struck out with respect to defendants who are government officials or agents.

 

viii) pleadings which disclose no reasonable cause of action (conspiracy)

 

[47]           The government also seeks to strike out the plaintiffs= claims of conspiracy, stating those claims do not disclose a reasonable cause of action given the insufficiency of material facts set out in the statement of claim.  That claim is framed as follows:

 

1.  The Plaintiffs claim:

 

(b) alternatively, against all of the Defendants, damages for conspiring to intentionally interfere in economic relations in the sum of $25,000,000.00;

 

[48]           Once again, this issue was addressed in Ayangma, which case was relied upon by counsel for the plaintiffs and the defendants.  The court noted, at paras. 94-96:

 

94     As to conspiracy, in H.V.K. v. Children's Aid Society of Haldimand‑Norfolk, [2003] O.J. No. 1572 (S.C.J.), Himel, J. relied on Normart Management Ltd. v. West Hill Redevelopment Co. (1998), 155 D.L.R. (4th) 627 (C.A.) in stating that the pleading must set out certain material facts in making a claim for the tort of conspiracy. At paragraph 21 he stated:

 

(1) all of the parties to the conspiracy must be identified and their relationship to each other described;

 

            (2) agreements between the various defendants must be pleaded with all facts material to such agreements including the parties to each agreement, the date of the agreement and the object and purpose of each agreement;

 

(3) the overt acts of each of the alleged conspirators in pursuance or furtherance of the conspiracy must be pleaded with clarity and precision, including the times and dates and such overt acts;

 

 (4) the pleading must allege the injury and the damage occasioned to the plaintiff and special damage in the sense of a monetary loss which the plaintiff has sustained must be pleaded and particularized.

 

[49]           Allegations in the nature of conspiracy, fraud, or bad faith require a higher degree of disclosure and precision than other types of allegations or claims made in the statement of claim.  The parties must be made aware, in the statement of claim, of the details of the alleged agreement they purportedly entered into, their relationship to their alleged co-conspirators, the precise actions they purportedly took in furtherance of the conspiracy, and the specific damages purportedly arising from the alleged conspiracy. (See Pindoff Record Sales Ltd. v. CBS Music Products Inc., [1989] O.J. No. 1382, 1989 CarswellOnt 490, at paras. 10-14).

 

[50]           Paragraphs 275, 277 and 278 in the statement of claim allege conspiracy. However, there are no material facts pleaded with respect to the alleged conspiratorial acts of each of the defendants, nor are there any details of the intended conspiracy. The relationship between the alleged co-conspirators is not described. The damages allegedly occasioned to the plaintiffs were not particularized in any manner, but instead were simply expressed as a monetary amount in the sum of $25 million.

 

[51]           Counsel for the plaintiffs argue that, in accordance with Hunt v. Carey Canada Inc., [1990] 2 S.C.R. 959, 1990 CanLii 90, (1990) 74 D.L.R. (4th) 321 (S.C.C.), and Operation Dismantle Inc. v R., [1985] 1 S.C.R. 441 (S.C.C.), the court ought to generously construe the statement of claim and overlook deficiencies or inadequacies which are merely the result of drafting deficiencies. Further they submit claims should not be struck out merely because they are long, and involve complex issues of fact and law, or novel legal propositions.

 

[52]           There is nothing in the defendants= submissions which suggest they are seeking to strike claims because they are complex or novel.  The Aplain and obvious@ test established in Hunt did not nullify the effect of the Rules of Court or rules of pleading. It is clear from both Hunt and Operation Dismantle, that some degree of accommodation is fair and proper when a common sense reading of the statement of claim discloses a proper cause of action despite drafting deficiencies. However, that does not give a plaintiff license to violate multiple rules of drafting or ignore fundamental principles of fairness and disclosure when the plaintiff=s claims require another party to ascertain, with some precision, the case it must meet.  The defendants are not required to scour through a lengthy statement of claim to ascertain which details sprinkled throughout the document might constitute actions the plaintiffs view as conspiratorial.

 

[53]           The plaintiffs have failed to set out the material facts to disclose any reasonable cause of action in the nature of conspiracy, and paragraphs 1(b), 275, 277 and 278 ought to be struck out.

 

[54]           I will summarize my findings on the government=s motion following consideration of the same type of motion presented by Paul Jenkins.

 

B. The defendant, Paul Jenkins=, motion to strike, in whole or in part, the statement of claim

 

[55]           The defendant, Paul Jenkins, relies on the same rules and principles as the government and seeks to have numerous paragraphs in the statement of claim struck out.

 

i) a wholesale violation of the rules of pleading

 

[56]           The defendant Paul Jenkins submits the plaintiffs= statement of claim is unnecessarily long and replete with violations of the rules of pleadings.  He submits the statement of claim should be struck in its entirety as being scandalous, frivolous, vexatious, an abuse of the process of the court, and embarrassing. III. Security for costs

 

[89]           Each of the defendants has filed a motion asking the court to order the plaintiffs to provide security for costs in this matter.  I will deal firstly with the motion presented on behalf of the government. 

 

[90]           Rule 56 of the Rules of Civil Procedure provides for the court to order security for costs on such terms as are just. Rule 56.01 reads as follows:

 

56.01   In any proceeding where it appears that

 

(a) the plaintiff or applicant is ordinarily resident outside Prince Edward Island;

 

(b) the plaintiff or applicant has another proceeding for the same relief pending in Prince Edward Island or elsewhere;

 

(c) the plaintiff, or any person through or under whom he claims, has a judgment or order against him for costs that have not been paid;

 

(d) the plaintiff or applicant is a corporation or a nominal plaintiff or applicant, and there is good reason to believe that the plaintiff orapplicant has insufficient assets in Prince Edward Island to pay thecosts of the defendant or respondent;

 

(e) there is good reason to believe that the action or application isfrivolous and vexatious and that the plaintiff or applicant hasinsufficient assets in Prince Edward Island to pay the costs of thedefendant or respondent; or

 

(f) a statute entitles the defendant or respondent to security for costs, the court on motion by the defendant or respondent may make such order for security for costs as is just.

 

[91]           In Johnston v. Montreal Trust Co. of Canada, [1993] P.E.I.J. No. 57, 1993 CarswellPEI 158, 346 A.P.R. 276, 110 Nfld & P.E.I.R. 276, DesRoches J. (as he then was) confirmed that the onus rests on the moving party to establish a prima facie case that at least one of the circumstances found in Rule 56.01 exists.  He stated, at para. 19:

 

19.   The onus of satisfying the court that the matter comes with [sic] Rule 56.01(d) is on the defendant. When that has been shown, the onus of establishing that the plaintiff has sufficient assets to avoid an order is on the plaintiff. Where the plaintiff seeks to avoid an order for security for costs on the grounds of impecuniosity, there is an obligation on the plaintiff to substantiate impecuniosity by way of evidence, and then further show why, despite impecuniosity, justice demands that the plaintiff be permitted to continue the litigation without posting security for costs.

Onus shifts to plaintiffs to demonstrate impecuniosity

 

[94]           Since the defendants have discharged their onus, the onus shifts to the plaintiffs to substantiate their impecuniosity if they wish to avoid an order for security for costs.  The plaintiffs may establish impecuniosity, and thereby discharge their onus, by way of evidence presented to the court. (See Johnston v. Montreal Trust Co. of Canada, supra). 

 

[95]           Counsel for each of the defendants submit the case law places a significant burden on the plaintiffs to show compelling evidence of both direct and indirect impecuniosity. They must show not only that they have no assets or money, but they have no access to assets or money. This issue was dealt with by Sutherland J. in the case of Smith Bus Lines Ltd. v. Bank of Montreal, [1987] O.J. No. 1197, 1987 CarswellOnt 566, 61 O.R. (2d) 688, at para 43:

 

43.   The term "impecuniosity" does not appear in the rule; it is a term introduced as part of the judicial gloss upon the rule in response to the words "as is just" in the part of the rule stating that (upon satisfaction of the stated conditions precedent) "the court ... may make such order as is just". The corporate plaintiff wishing to be allowed to proceed with its action, without either showing sufficient assets or putting up security, must first show "impecuniosity" meaning not only that it does not have sufficient assets itself but also that it cannot raise the security for costs from its shareholders and associates, partly because the courts do not want a successful defendant to be effectively deprived of costs where, for example, wealthy shareholders have decided to carry on business and litigation through a shell corporation. To go the impecuniosity route the plaintiff must establish by evidence that it cannot raise security for costs because, if a private company, its shareholders have not sufficient assets. As expressed by Reid J. in John Wink Ltd. v. Sico Inc. (1987), 57 O.R. (2d) 705 at p. 709, 15 C.P.C. (2d) 187...: "If an order for security stops a plaintiff in its tracks it has disposed of the suit." To raise impecuniosity there must be evidence that if security is required the suit will be stopped ‑‑ because the amount of the security is not only not possessed by the plaintiff but is not available to it. Here there is simply no evidence to that effect. (Emphasis added).

[104]       As was stated in the Superior Salmon Farms Ltd. case, (quoted at para. 32 of the Crudo case above), it is obvious someone is prepared to finance the prosecution of this action by CMT. That person or persons should also be prepared to either provide security for the costs of the defendants in the event the claim fails, or they, or the plaintiffs, have to establish that security cannot be raised.

 

[105]       An order for security for costs is not required when a plaintiff has sufficient assets on Prince Edward Island to meet a post trial order of costs against them if they are unsuccessful in their litigation.  However, when a plaintiff has no such assets in the province, the defendant is entitled to know if it proceeds to defend the litigation and is successful, it will have recourse to recover monies it spent defending against the plaintiff=s unfounded claims. That comfort is provided to the defendant by way of a pretrial order for security for costs. Without such an order, the plaintiff is essentially able to cause the defendant to spend what could be hundreds of thousands of dollars without any hope of recovering those funds even if it is successful. For that reason, the onus is on the plaintiff to prove it is impecunious in order to avoid providing security for costs.

 

[106]       The plaintiffs have acknowledged they have insufficient assets in Prince Edward Island to pay the costs of the defendants if called upon to do so. However, I find that apart from bald statements made by Maines on behalf of CMT, little or no evidence has been tendered as to the impecuniosity of the plaintiffs.  The onus of proof on the plaintiffs has not been met.  I am not, therefore, prepared to relieve the plaintiffs from the operation of Rule 56 which provides the court with the authority to make an order for security for costs as is just.

 

Government did not cause the plaintiffs= assets to be insufficient

 

[107]       In addition to claiming impecuniosity, which I have found the plaintiffs failed to demonstrate, they claimed their purported impecuniosity arose as a result of the conduct of the defendant government.  However, had I found the plaintiffs to be impecunious, I would not have found them to be in that position as a result of actions of the government.

 

[108]       The plaintiffs have not established a causal link between their financial position and government=s actions.  The admitted facts show the plaintiff CMT was in financial difficulty long before any involvement with the government. As of 2008, CMT had losses in excess of $15 million. The company has a repeated history of dissolution in Florida and was only reinstated in 2012.  Their shares were deregistered by the Security and Exchange Commission.  They were found to have violated the Securities Act. One of their current directors is permanently barred from any association with a member of the New York Stock Exchange.  All of these factors would impact CMT=s ability to conduct business and raise capital. 7645686 would suffer the effects of the same issues given that, according to Maines, it was incorporated for one purpose only and its business did not materialize, and it was Aentirely dependent upon funding from CMT@.

 

Merits of the plaintiffs= case

 

[109]       If a plaintiff is not found to be impecunious, it may be able to avoid an order for security for costs if it can show its claim has a good chance of success. However, the evidence filed does not allow the court to draw that conclusion. The action appears to be complex, involving numerous individuals and corporations, and raising significant issues of credibility.   It is not possible to weigh the evidence, assess credibility, or draw inferences such that I could determine the plaintiffs have a good chance of success and therefore should be relieved of posting security for costs.

 

Quantum

 

[110]       In assessing a Ajust@ amount of security, some guidance is found in the Court of Appeal decision in Elbaz v. Prince Edward Island (Minister of the Crown), 2010 PECA 9.  The court must take into account all the circumstances of the case including the stage at which the security is sought. The order must strike a balance between the need to provide a defendant with protection against costs, and the right of the plaintiff to pursue the claim, absent a burdensome order to post security for those costs. Such orders are never final and may be increased or decreased at any time. An order is usually structured to protect the defendant against costs for the next reasonable steps in the proceeding. (See Elbaz at paras. 19, 20 and 22).

 

[111]       The plaintiffs filed a draft bill of costs setting out what they anticipate the total costs of the proceedings to be, beginning with the commencement of pleadings and ending with the completion of trial.  Each of the three defendants did the same.

 

[112]       The plaintiffs= draft bill of costs fixed the total costs, including taxes, at $102,500, on a partial indemnity basis. However, at the hearing, counsel for the plaintiffs stated that the same bill of costs would apply to each defence group, namely, the government, Paul Jenkins, and Garth Jenkins. In other words, his total estimate of $102,500 would jump to $307,500. However, that estimate covers the period to the end of trial and the plaintiffs submit that if the court, in its discretion, orders security for costs, it should do so only up to the point of completion of a summary judgment motion, if such a motion is to be made. The plaintiffs submit the costs to that point would be just over 50% of the total cost, or approximately $156,000.

 

[113]       Counsel for each of the defendants raised a number of deficiencies in the plaintiffs= proposed bill of costs.

 

[114]       Firstly, counsel for the government pointed out there is no allocation for preliminary motions such as defendants= motions to strike, plaintiffs= motions to strike, defendants= motion for security for costs, motions for production of documents, etc.

 

[115]       Secondly, the bill of costs does not contemplate discovery of any non-parties. While it may not be the norm to discover non-parties, the statement of claim in this matter makes reference to 39 individuals who can be presumed to have information which may be significant to the claim. 

 

[116]       Thirdly, the bill of costs does not contemplate discovery of a representative from the Government of Prince Edward Island, who is a named defendant in its own right.

 

[117]       Additionally, in the statement of claim there is an allegation of conspiracy against two individuals at a particular investment firm. Counsel for the government asks the question, AIs anyone really going to proceed to trial without discovery of alleged co-conspirators?@ 

 

[118]       The bill of costs does not include anything for disbursements. This case will be long and complex and would likely involve thousands of documents. Apart from gathering, copying and binding those documents, and transcripts of discovery, other disbursements would include travel, expert fees and court fees. 

 

[119]       There is no allocation set out for pretrial conferences. In the simplest of cases it is not possible to proceed to trial without at least one such conference, and it would be reasonable to anticipate more than one pretrial conference leading up to complex trial. After the completion of the pretrial conference, or conferences, as the case may be, a trial date is set, and trial preparation begins in earnest.

 

[120]       It was noted as well that the plaintiffs= draft  bill of costs did not allow for two counsel to be on the file for each defendant group.  Notwithstanding the Court of Appeal=s recent comments in Donovan v. QCRS, 2016 PECA 1, on what it perceives to be the excessive use of lawyers on various matters, I find on a claim of this size, being for a minimum of $25 million against any defendant, it would not be unreasonable for any party to engage two counsel.  In fact, the plaintiffs themselves were represented by two counsel on the motions which are the subject of this decision. Each of the other defendant groups were also represented by two counsel. On matters which are of great significance to those directly involved, it cannot be said that it is either unreasonable or unanticipated that the parties will engage the services they believe are necessary for them to meet with success.

 

[121]       The allocation of time for discovery of various people is also challenged by counsel for the defendants. As an example, they cite the fact the total time allocation for each opposing counsel, including themselves, to both prepare for and complete their examination of  Mr. Sheridan is two days.  Within the statement of claim, Mr. Sheridan is mentioned at least 40 times.  Counsel submits there would be Athousands of documents for Mr. Sheridan to review@. Proper preparation alone would be difficult to accomplish in two days.  The plaintiffs have not provided a realistic estimate of the time required to complete such an examination.

 

[122]       Counsel for Paul Jenkins submitted as well that the plaintiffs= estimate of how long the defendants will discover his clients is neither realistic or accurate.  He submits the defendants= estimates are much more insightful when it comes to determine the time required to discover the opposing party. I agree with that submission.

 

[123]       The bill of costs submitted by the government reflects anticipated costs, on a partial indemnity basis, of $639,000, approximately 60% or $385,000 of which would be spent on pretrial activities. The government=s substantial indemnity estimate is approximately $890,000, with its estimate for full indemnity approximating $1,225,000. Those estimates do include allocations for pretrial motions, case management, documentary and oral discovery and preparation for and attendance at trial, which is estimated to run for 42 days. The disbursements, which are broken down into various categories, total $120,500. Taxes on fees and disbursements range from $78,500 on a partial indemnity basis to $150,000 on a full indemnity basis.

 

[124]       Counsel for Paul Jenkins filed a draft bill of costs in the same format. The totals for partial indemnity costs were in the range of  $581,000, with substantial indemnity running to $825,000 and full indemnity being $904,000.

 

[125]       Counsel for Garth Jenkins followed a similar format, arriving at partial indemnity costs of $500,000, substantial indemnity costs of $681,000 and full indemnity costs of $815,000.

 

[126]       The total of estimated partial indemnity costs for the three defendants is $1,720,000. On a substantial indemnity basis, the total rises to approximately $2,400,000. Full indemnity estimates exceed $2,900,000.  Counsel for all three defendants estimate approximately 60% of fees on whatever scale would be incurred in pretrial matters, with preparation for and attendance at trial accounting for 40% of the cost.

 

[127]       I find the estimate of costs submitted on behalf of the plaintiffs to be quite unrealistic. The allegations in the statement of claim are serious, the issues are complex, and the stakes are high. The claim is for $25 million dollars, or multiples thereof. The estimates that the trial would take 40 days, or even more, are reasonable. The amount of pretrial work required is proportionate to the length of the trial. The nature and breadth of the allegations, which include breach of good faith, failing to act honestly in the performance of contractual obligations, conspiring to intentionally interfere with economic relations, breach of confidentiality and breach of fiduciary duty, will lead the defendants to mount a thorough and vigorous defence designed to foreclose any opportunity for the plaintiffs= success. Allegations attacking the character of the defendant are likely to attract costs on a substantial indemnity basis if the allegations are proven to be unfounded. Defending allegations of negligent behavior is one thing. Defending allegations of inherently dishonest behavior is quite another.

 

[128]       In Aluma Systems Canada Inc. v. Strait Crossing Inc., 2004 PESCTD 12, the plaintiff was claiming the sum of $4.3 million in a civil lawsuit. The defendant projected its legal costs to be approximately $1.35 million. The court ordered security for costs in the sum of $1 million and provided the plaintiff with four options for providing such security.

 

[129]       In Aviaco International Leasing Inc. v. Boeing Canada Inc., [2000] O.J. 3284, 2000 CarswellOnt 3088, the Ontario Superior Court of Justice ordered security for costs in the amount of $1.19 million on a claim alleging conspiracy, breach of contract, intentional interference with economic relations, fraud and deceit.  There were multiple defendants and the amounts claimed totaled $450 million.  In awarding significant costs, Nordheimer J. referred to the nature and breadth of the allegations and stated, AThe action is a complex one and, as one would expect, is going to be hard-fought.@

 

[130]       The Prince Edward Island Court of Appeal recently ordered security for costs in the amount of $500,000 in respect of a claim for the recovery of aircraft engines capable of generating revenue of between $240,000 and $720,000 annually. In National Police of Columbia v.  Dash 224 LLC, 2014 PECA 16, the court ordered the security to be provided either by way of a bond, or an irrevocable letter of credit satisfactory to the Prothonotary of the Supreme Court of Prince Edward Island.

 

[131]       In Treats Inc. v. Richter, Usher & Vineberg, 2003 CarswellOnt 2312, the Ontario Superior Court of Justice ordered security of costs on a substantial indemnity basis in the amount of $680,000 on a claim of $14,500,000.  The claims against the defendants included fraudulent misrepresentation and conspiracy. The decision was affirmed on appeal.

 

[132]       In CASP et al. v. Canada (Attorney General), 2015 PESC 25, following a 38 day trial dealing with a claim for $8,000,000 involving allegations of bad faith against the government defendant, costs were awarded to the successful defendant on a partial indemnity basis in the sum of $1 million.

 

[133]       I find the range of cost estimates provided by the three defendants for each category of indemnification to be reasonable.  As I have already stated, given the nature of the allegations, costs would likely be awarded against the plaintiffs on a substantial indemnity basis if the allegations are shown to be unfounded. Unfortunately, at this stage of the proceeding, it is not possible to draw conclusions about the likelihood of success of what will be a newly drafted statement of claim.  Bearing in mind that, in accordance with Rule 56.07, the amount of security ordered may be increased or decreased at any time, I intend to base the order for security for costs on a partial indemnity scale.

 

[134]       Counsel for Paul Jenkins submitted the rates prescribed in Practice Note 21 should be adjusted for inflation as they were established in 2005 and remain unchanged since then.  He cites Shepard v. Sanderson, 2012 PESC 20, at para. 21, where Mitchell J stated:

 

... Practice Note 21, being the September 1, 2005, Costs Guide, suggests $160 as the maximum hourly rate for a lawyer of his experience.  That maximum rate is intended to apply only to more complicated matters and to the more experienced counsel in each category.  While this matter is not especially complicated, I will allow $160 an hour, taking into account inflation since 2005. ... (Emphasis added)

 

[135]       I agree the rates set in the Costs Guide were not meant to be stagnant. Applying inflation since 2005 would increase the rate by almost 20%, bringing it to approximately $190 per hour. That is a factor which may appropriately be considered when determining an award for costs following trial. However, in exercising my discretion, I decline to apply any inflationary adjustment to the 2005 rates for the purposes of fixing an amount to be ordered as security for costs.

 

[136]       I have considered the submissions from counsel for the plaintiffs to the effect that imposing an order for security for costs against the plaintiffs would impose Asome difficulty@ on the companies. At the same time, it is necessary to provide some measure of protection to the defendants who will be spending substantial sums of money responding to the plaintiffs= claims. I have therefore decided that the order for security for costs should be based on the projected estimates covering the period to the completion of pretrial matters, leaving consideration of costs for the actual preparation for and attendance at court to a later date. I accept the submissions of defence counsel estimating that 60% of their total costs would be incurred in reaching that stage.

Party                Partial indemnity costs            60% of anticipated (partial) costs

 

1) Government                      $638,970.                                $383,382.       

 

2) Paul Jenkins                      $581,194.                                $348,716.

 

3) Garth Jenkins                   $500,254.                                $300,152.

 

TOTAL                                                                              $1,032,250.

 

 

[138]       The combined security for costs award, based on 60% of estimated partial indemnity costs for the three defendants, totals $1,032,250.  I have considered all of the circumstances surrounding this matter, including the effect such an order may have on the plaintiffs.

 

[139]       The plaintiffs claim an order for security for costs would impose Asome difficulty@ on them, and make it Avery difficult for the Plaintiffs [sic] pursue their claim against the Defendants and raise the necessary capital to carry on its intended business@.  I accept that such an order will impose some difficulty. Mounting a significant legal challenge such as this would likely present some difficulty for most plaintiffs. In this case, the plaintiffs have failed to show they are impecunious, but do not have sufficient assets in the jurisdiction to respond to any potential costs order following a trial. There are continuing to raise capital to carry on their intended business. Unfortunately, engaging in legal disputes from time to time is part of doing business. They have launched a complex legal claim which will call upon considerable resources in order for them to pursue their claims. Similarly, each of the defendants will be called upon to expend considerable resources in their own defence.

 

[140]       The Rules of Civil Procedure and the case precedents provide a roadmap for determining what would be Ajust@ in the circumstances. Courts are to balance the interests of each party and arrive at a fair result. That includes a consideration of the time and complexity involved in the matter. It includes a consideration of the nature, breadth, and seriousness of the allegations. While plaintiffs have a right to pursue their claims, defendants have a right to know that, if they are successful, they will have a reasonable chance of recovering a reasonable portion of their expenditures.

 

[141]       I have considered the totality of the order, the magnitude of the claims presented, and the fact that each defendant must maintain and pursue its own defence against claims which are a minimum of $25 million, but could be double or triple that amount.  I am satisfied that orders for security for costs in the amounts of $383,382 in favor of the Government of Prince Edward Island, $348,716 in favor of Paul Jenkins, and $300,152 in favor of Garth Jenkins are just and reasonable.

The letter of credit must be unconditional and irrevocable.
