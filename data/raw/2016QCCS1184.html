INTRODUCTION

[1]           The defendants make a motion to quash a writ of seizure before judgment by garnishment on the basis that the property held by the garnishee on behalf of the defendants was funds in a bank account in Switzerland and was therefore beyond the jurisdiction of this Court.

CONTEXT

[2]           Instrubel is suing the Republic of Iraq and three entities related to the Republic of Iraq.[1] It is seeking to enforce in Québec arbitral awards dated February 6, 1996 and March 12, 2003 rendered by the International Court of Arbitration in Paris in its favour against Iraq. Those awards were worth approximately $32 million as of March 12, 2003. Instrubel filed its motion for recognition and enforcement on March 11, 2013.

[3]           On July 30, 2013, Instrubel requested from this Court the issuance of a writ of seizure before judgment by garnishment with respect to the air navigation charges held by the International Air Transport Association on behalf of Iraq. Instrubel alleged in the supporting affidavit that it had reason to believe that the assets were located in Québec and the request for the writ of seizure by garnishment asked to seize the assets at IATA’s address in Montreal, but the assets to be seized are described as follows:

All aerodrome charges and air navigation charges of the Republic of Iraq billed and/or collected and/or otherwise already held by the International Air Transport Association, either at its head office in Montréal or at any of its worldwide branches, on behalf of the Republic of Iraq.

(Emphasis added)

[4]           Justice Gaudreau authorized the issuance of the writ as requested. It was served on IATA on the same date.

[5]           Iraq made a motion to quash the seizure on August 5, 2013. It alleged four grounds:

A.           Failure to disclose material information;

B.           Insufficient and misleading allegations in support of the Plaintiff’s writ of seizure;

C.           Foreign states’ immunity against seizures before judgment; and

D.           Foreign state’s property’s immunity from execution.

[6]           Meanwhile, on August 12, 2013, IATA filed its negative declaration. It stated that it “does not currently have in its possession any sums of money, securities or movable property that is marked as belonging to the Defendants”, but it added that it had in its possession an amount of approximately US$166 million that it held in trust for the benefit of the Iraqi Civil Aviation Authority (ICAA). IATA did not disclose where the funds were held.

[7]           Further, in its negative declaration, IATA argued that the funds held for ICAA were to be used for air traffic control services and equipment and therefore were immune from seizure.

[8]           Notwithstanding the filing of a negative declaration, IATA treated the full amount of approximately US$166 million as seized.

[9]           On September 16, 2013, the parties proceeded before Justice Casgrain on the motion to quash the seizure.

[10]        In advance of the hearing, the parties agreed that they would proceed only on grounds A and B, and that grounds C and D would be pleaded at a later date with Instrubel’s contestation of IATA’s negative declaration.

[11]        On November 12, 2013, Justice Casgrain rendered his judgment dismissing Iraq’s motion to quash and upholding the seizure. The parties agree that the judgment is limited to grounds A and B, and that grounds C and D remain outstanding.

[12]        Instrubel filed its contestation of IATA’s negative declaration on November 14, 2013. That contestation also remains outstanding.

[13]        On November 25, 2013, IATA served a motion asking that the Court limit the amount purported to be seized to $85 million. It further asked for a declaration that the order be “without prejudice to any and all rights or arguments of the Garnishee”.

[14]        On December 11, 2013, the parties appeared before Justice Décarie, who rendered an order by consent limiting the amount seized to $90 million and allowing IATA to transfer the funds held under seizure to the trust account of its attorneys. The order was made “without prejudice to any and all rights or arguments that the parties may wish to invoke or make”.

[15]        Two years later, grounds C and D of the motion to quash and Instrubel’s contestation of IATA’s negative declaration remain outstanding. On September 28, 2015, Iraq amended its motion to quash by amending paragraph 47 in ground D and by adding a new ground E that challenged the jurisdiction of this Court to issue the writ of seizure on the basis that the property seized was funds in a bank account in Switzerland and was therefore beyond the jurisdiction of this Court. This was the first time that Iraq suggested that the funds were held in a bank account in Switzerland at the time of the seizure and that the Court lacked jurisdiction to issue the writ. Iraq also requested permission to proceed immediately with grounds C, D and E.

[16]        Instrubel opposed the amendments and Iraq made a motion to amend the motion to quash. On November 30, 2015, the Court dismissed the motion to amend paragraph 47 but allowed the addition of the new ground E.[2] The Court further held that Iraq could proceed immediately with the new ground E, but that grounds C and D would be pleaded at a later date with Instrubel’s contestation of IATA’s negative declaration, as the parties had previously agreed. As a result, the issues of whether the funds held for ICAA were held on behalf of one of the Defendants and whether the seizure is valid under the State Immunity Act[3] will be debated at a later time. For purposes of the present motion with respect to ground E, the Court will assume that the funds held by IATA were held for Iraq and were subject to seizure.

[17]        For the purposes of the hearing on ground E, IATA filed into the Court record an affidavit dated December 4, 2015 which confirmed that as of the date of the seizure, the funds held by IATA for ICAA were held in a bank account in Switzerland, and that the amount of $90 million was transferred to the trust account of IATA’s attorneys in Québec on February 9, 2015.

[18]        In order to clarify an issue which arose after the hearing, the parties filed a joint stipulation on March 11, 2016 which provided further detail as to the funds held in the bank account.

POSITION OF THE PARTIES

[19]        Iraq argues that the Court did not have jurisdiction to order a seizure before judgment by garnishment of property located outside Québec and that the writ of seizure issued in the present matter is therefore null.

[20]        Iraq also argues that the transfer of the funds to Québec in February 2015 cannot retroactively validate an illegal seizure, particularly since Iraq did not know that the funds were being transferred from Switzerland when it consented to the order. Further, it argues that Justice Décarie’s order was made without prejudice to the rights of the parties.

[21]        IATA supports Iraq’s position.

[22]        Instrubel argues that the motion to quash should be dismissed for the following reasons:

A.           The Court had jurisdiction to issue the writ;

B.           Iraq has in any event waived any right it may have had to challenge the Court’s jurisdiction to issue the writ by its actions and inactions and because IATA has moved the funds into Québec; and

C.           Even if Iraq could raise jurisdiction, it would first have to succeed in establishing falsity, which is no longer open to it.

ISSUES

[23]        As preliminary matters, the Court will review (1) its jurisdiction to quash a seizure authorized by another judge of this Court, (2) Iraq’s right to raise the jurisdiction issue more than two years after the seizure, and (3) the context in which this seizure was made and the impact of that context on the other issues.

[24]        The first issue that the Court must then decide is the nature of the relationship between IATA and Iraq, and in particular whether IATA was holding property in Switzerland on behalf of Iraq or whether IATA owed a debt to Iraq.

[25]        If the Court concludes that IATA owed a debt to Iraq, and given that IATA is domiciled in Québec, then the jurisdiction issue should be fairly straightforward.

[26]        On the other hand, if IATA held funds in a bank account in Switzerland on behalf of Iraq, then the jurisdiction issue becomes whether the Court has jurisdiction to issue a writ of seizure before judgment by garnishment in recognition and enforcement proceedings when (1) the garnishee is domiciled in Québec but (2) the property held by the garnishee on behalf of the defendant is outside Québec.

[27]        If the answer is yes and the Court had jurisdiction, the only issue is whether the Court should have exercised that jurisdiction.

[28]        If the answer is no, then a series of other issues must be decided:

1.            What is the effect of including property outside Québec in the writ? Is the seizure in whole or in part null, or is it ineffective in respect of the property outside Québec?

2.            If the writ was ineffective in respect of property outside Québec, does it become effective when the property is later brought into Québec?

3.            If the answer to question 2 is yes, the Court will have to consider the circumstances under which the property was brought into Québec, including the alleged error as to where the funds were located and the reservation of rights.

ANALYSIS

1.            Jurisdiction of the Court to quash the seizure for excess of jurisdiction

[29]        As a preliminary matter, the Court raises the issue of its jurisdiction to quash a seizure authorized by another judge of this Court. The parties all assumed that the Court had jurisdiction and did not raise this issue.

[30]        Clearly, the Court has jurisdiction to quash a seizure authorized by another judge of this Court if the allegations in the affidavit in support of the seizure are insufficient or false.[4] The Code gives the defendant the right to have the seizure quashed by another judge of the same court because it was authorized on an ex parte basis, and the defendant therefore never had the opportunity to raise these issues before the judge who authorized the seizure.

[31]        The jurisprudence recognizes that there are grounds for quashing a seizure before judgment other than insufficiency or falsity.[5] Although Article 738 old C.C.P. does not apply in those cases, the courts have concluded that the defendant can nevertheless proceed by motion to quash before another judge of the court that authorized the seizure.[6]

[32]        The Court therefore concludes that it has jurisdiction to quash the seizure in the present matter.

2.            Whether Iraq can raise the jurisdiction argument now

[33]        Instrubel argues that Iraq cannot raise the jurisdiction argument now for several reasons:

·                     It is well outside the five-day delay in Article 738 old C.C.P.;

·                     It has in any event waived any right it may have had to challenge the Court’s jurisdiction by its actions and inactions and because IATA has moved the funds into Québec; and

·                     Even if Iraq could raise jurisdiction, it would first have to succeed in establishing falsity, which is no longer open to it.

[34]        The Court dealt with some of these issues when it granted Iraq’s motion to amend its motion to quash. In that judgment, the Court held that “if the seizure is illegal because it exceeds the Court’s jurisdiction, then it should be quashed, regardless of any delay.”[7]

[35]        With respect to the first argument, the five-day delay specified in Article 738 old C.C.P. does not apply to a motion to quash a seizure for a ground other than sufficiency or falsity of the allegations in the affidavit.[8]

[36]        On the waiver argument,[9] the Court notes that the jurisdiction of the Court which is at issue in the present matter is not the jurisdiction of the Court over a defendant. The Québec courts can exercise jurisdiction over a defendant who is not domiciled in Québec, if they have jurisdiction within the rules set out in Articles 3148 and following of the Civil Code of Québec. In those cases, it is well established that the defendant can waive any lack of jurisdiction over it through its consent or its failure to act. The plaintiff who has successfully sued a defendant not domiciled in Québec may require the assistance of a foreign court in order to execute the judgment.

[37]        In the present matter, what is at issue is the jurisdiction of the Court to seize assets that are outside Québec. This is an issue of the extent of the powers of the Court, and it is not affected by any consent or failure to act of the defendant. It is a matter of public order that can be raised at any time and that cannot be waived.[10]

[38]        The second argument is dismissed.

[39]        Instrubel’s third argument is that the new ground E is simply a falsity argument dressed up in jurisdictional clothing.

[40]        Instrubel’s affiant stated in his affidavit in support of the seizure that:

33.          Instrubel has reason to believe that the Republic of Iraq owns assets that are located in the Province of Québec. More precisely, those assets are the air navigation charges imposed by the Republic of Iraq and comprised of, inter alia, fly-over fees, airport maintenance fees and route maintenance fees. Such air navigation charges are payable by airlines and countries to the Republic of Iraq in order to be granted the permission to fly over their airspace.

(Emphasis added)

[41]        Instrubel argues that Iraq’s jurisdiction argument is based on the factual allegation that the funds were held in Switzerland at the time of the seizure, and therefore it contradicts the assertion in paragraph 33 of the affidavit. Instrubel argues that this is a falsity argument that should have been raised within five days under Article 738 old C.C.P.

[42]        The Court does not agree.

[43]        Instrubel said in its affidavit that it had reason to believe that Iraq owned assets located in Québec. That allegation was sufficient to justify the Court issuing a writ of seizure by garnishment for assets located in Québec. If the seizure had been limited to assets located in Québec, IATA would have responded that it did not hold any assets on behalf of Iraq in Québec, and nothing would have been seized. Instead, Instrubel requested a writ of seizure by garnishment for assets “either at its head office in Montréal or at any of its worldwide branches”, the Court issued the writ, and IATA answered that it held funds. The allegation that Instrubel had reason to believe that Iraq owned assets located in Québec is not relevant in the context of this broader seizure. It is an issue of jurisdiction, not an issue of the falsity of the affidavit.

[44]        As a result, the Court concludes that Iraq can raise the jurisdiction issue at this time.

3.            Considerations relating to the recognition and enforcement of foreign arbitral awards

[45]        The issue of the Court’s jurisdiction to issue a seizure by garnishment must be analyzed in the context of this case as a whole.

[46]        In the present matter, Instrubel has obtained two arbitral awards against Iraq and it seeks the assistance of the Court to enforce those arbitral awards in Québec. Québec has no connection, whether real and substantial or otherwise, with the parties to the arbitration or with their dispute. This does not matter in the context of recognition and enforcement proceedings: as stated by the Supreme Court of Canada in Chevron Corp. v. Yaiguaje, the usual tests for jurisdiction in Articles 3148 and following C.C.Q. do not apply to recognition and enforcement proceedings, because the role of the enforcing court is limited to assisting in the enforcement of what has already been decided in another forum.[11] As a result, the key consideration is the presence of assets upon which the judgment can be enforced:[12]

[49]      In most recognition and enforcement proceedings, the only factor that draws a foreign judgment creditor to the province is the potential for assets upon which to ultimately enforce the judgment.  Enforcement is limited to the seizable assets found within the province.  

(Emphasis added)

[47]        The Supreme Court said the following with respect to the jurisdiction of the enforcing court with respect to enforcement measures:[13]

[46]      Second, enforcement is limited to measures ― like seizure, garnishment, or execution ― that can be taken only within the confines of the jurisdiction, and in accordance with its rules: Pro Swing, at para. 11; J. Walker, Castel & Walker: Canadian Conflict of Laws (6th ed. (loose-leaf)), at p. 11-52.  The recognition and enforcement of a judgment therefore has a limited impact: as Walker states, “[a]n order enforcing a foreign judgment applies only to local assets” (p. 14-11).  The enforcing court’s judgment has no coercive force outside its jurisdiction.  

(Emphasis added)

[48]        In the present case, the only connection to Québec is that IATA’s head office is in Montréal, and that IATA holds funds for or owes a debt to Iraq. If Instrubel cannot execute against those funds or that debt in Québec, then there is no reason for Instrubel to seek the recognition and enforcement of the arbitral awards in Québec.

[49]        These considerations have an impact on the present motion to quash.

[50]        On the one hand, the Court should be careful before quashing the seizure in the present matter because the effect of quashing the seizure may be the discontinuance of the recognition and enforcement proceedings.

[51]        On the other hand, the Court should only exercise its jurisdiction if it can assist in the enforcement of the arbitral awards. If, for example, the Court concludes that its jurisdiction is limited to freezing the assets by seizure before judgment and that the intervention of the courts of another country will be necessary to execute against the assets, it may be that the Court should not exercise its jurisdiction to seize the assets but rather should leave the matter to the courts that have jurisdiction to execute against those assets.

[52]        The Court will return to these issues below.

4.            The nature of the relationship between IATA and Iraq

[53]        The first issue that the Court must decide is the nature of the relationship between IATA and Iraq, and in particular whether IATA held property in Switzerland on behalf of Iraq or whether IATA owed a debt to Iraq.

[54]        Instrubel argues that IATA owed a debt to Iraq and that the debt was located at the domicile of the debtor, which was in Québec.

[55]        Iraq argues that IATA was holding the funds for Iraq and that the funds in the bank account in Switzerland therefore belonged to Iraq. Further, Iraq argues that Instrubel only ever asked to seize the air navigation charges held by IATA on behalf of Iraq, and that it cannot now plead that it seized a debt owed by IATA to Iraq. Finally, Iraq adds that if there was a debt owed by IATA to Iraq, it was payable in New York.

[56]        The contract between IATA and ICAA[14] includes the following provisions:

·                     ICAA has instructed the commercial airlines to pay IATA “acting as its Agent”;[15]

·                     The fees are “collected by IATA on behalf of the [ICAA]”;[16]

·                     “IATA will remit to the [ICAA] the route facility charges collected on the [ICAA’s] behalf, less the agreed administrative fees”;[17] and

·                     “ALL AIR NAVIGATION CHARGES OF IRAQ WILL BE BILLED AND COLLECTED BY THE INTERNATIONAL AIR TRANSPORT ASSOCIATION (IATA) ON BEHALF OF THE CAA of the STATE OF IRAQ”.[18]

[57]        The contract between IATA and ICAA is governed by Swiss law. Since no proof was made of Swiss law, the Court applies Québec law to characterize the relationship between IATA and Iraq.[19] Under Québec law, the relationship is a mandate whereby IATA is acting on behalf of Iraq.[20] This means that the funds collected by IATA on behalf of Iraq belong to Iraq and IATA has an obligation to remit them to Iraq, as opposed to the funds belonging to IATA and IATA having a debt to Iraq:[21]

Under the general principles of the law of mandate, it is clear that the obligation of a mandatary towards the mandator is not a debt. The person who has bought property on behalf of a third party who wishes to remain unknown is no more indebted for the price paid than he is the owner of the property. The true owner is the mandator, and the obligation of the mandatary nominee is to render an account to the mandator and deliver over what he has received on his behalf (C.C., art. 1713). What he receives, even if it is money, does not belong to him: he is obliged to keep it separate from his own property. 

(Emphasis added)

[58]        The ownership of the funds does not change merely because IATA deposits the funds in a bank account:[22]

Le dépôt de sommes d’argent dans un compte en fiducie ne modifie pas les droits de propriété de la personne au bénéfice de qui ces sommes sont conservées ni les droits d’appropriation qu’elle peut avoir et qui persistent soit entre les mains de la banque, soit entre les mains du syndic. Le Tribunal ne voit pas pourquoi le véritable propriétaire, la réclamante, ne pourrait pas faire valoir ses droits sur les fonds qui sont présentement en possession du syndic.

(Référence omise)

[59]        However, the comingling of funds in a bank account can affect ownership rights. In the present matter, the funds were comingled in the bank account with funds belonging to other countries. The parties filed a joint stipulation specifying that the bank account in Switzerland contained only funds collected by IATA on behalf of its clients, that the management fees payable to IATA by its clients were at times debited from the funds held in the account, and that IATA maintained records of the amounts collected on behalf of each of its clients. The Court concludes that the funds belonging to Iraq were readily identifiable, and therefore that they continued to belong to Iraq.[23]

5.            The jurisdiction issue

[60]        If the Court had concluded that IATA owed a debt to Iraq, then the jurisdiction issue would be much easier: IATA would be a party domiciled in Québec which owed a debt to Iraq. The fact that IATA had deposited the funds which gave rise to the debt in a bank account in Switzerland would not be relevant. The Québec courts would have jurisdiction to issue a writ of seizure by garnishment against IATA, because all that is being seized is the debt and not the bank account. The fact that contractually the debt is payable in New York also would not be relevant, because IATA is in Québec. IATA would be ordered not to pay its debt to Iraq pending the final judgment, and might ultimately be ordered to pay the debt to Instrubel if Instrubel is successful on the merits. None of this would have any impact on the bank account, which IATA would be free to do with as it pleased, provided that it did not pay its debt to Iraq.

[61]        However, the Court has instead concluded that IATA held the funds in the bank account on behalf of Iraq. The Court must therefore decide whether it had jurisdiction to issue a writ of seizure before judgment by garnishment when (1) the garnishee is domiciled in Québec but (2) the property held by the garnishee on behalf of the defendant is outside Québec. If the answer is no, then the seizure should be quashed. If the answer is yes, there may be a further issue as to the nature of the seizure: whether the Québec court’s jurisdiction is limited to freezing the assets or whether it can execute against those assets. If the Court’s jurisdiction is limited to freezing the assets pending the intervention of another country’s courts, it might not be appropriate for the Court to exercise that jurisdiction in a matter of recognition and enforcement of a foreign arbitral award.

[62]        The authorities submitted by the parties do not provide a clear answer to the jurisdiction issue.

[63]        The authorities submitted by Iraq establish that the Court cannot order the seizure of property outside Québec[24] and that the Court has no jurisdiction to issue a writ of seizure by garnishment when (1) the garnishee is domiciled outside Québec and (2) the property held by the garnishee on behalf of the defendant is also outside Québec.[25] Only Professor Castel suggests in a short passage that the Court has no jurisdiction to issue a writ of seizure by garnishment when the property held by the garnishee on behalf of the defendant is outside Québec:[26]

Le créancier québécois ne peut pas saisir des biens appartenant à son débiteur, situés hors du Québec en vertu d’un bref de saisie-exécution ou de saisie-arrêt émis au Québec, à moins de n’avoir recours aux tribunaux étrangers.

(Emphasis added; references omitted)

[64]        Instrubel submitted authorities that establish that the Court has jurisdiction to issue a Mareva injunction against a defendant domiciled in Québec and that the Mareva injunction can extend to property owned by the defendant which is located outside Québec.[27]

[65]        The jurisdiction issue in the present case essentially comes down to the question of whether a seizure before judgment by garnishment is in the nature of an ordinary seizure before judgment that affects the property, in which case it would not be valid if the property was outside Québec, or whether it is in the nature of a personal order like a Mareva injunction, in which case it would be valid if the garnishee was in Québec and regardless of where the property was located.[28]

[66]        The seizure by garnishment has features of both a seizure and a personal order.

[67]        The writ provides as follows:

We order you, Garnishee, to appear before the Court at the courthouse of Montreal located at 1, Notre-Dame Street on August 13, 2013, in room 1.110, at 9:00 a.m. to declare under oath the amounts of money, securities or movables belonging to Defendants which are in your possession for whatever purpose or grounds, and to hold them until the Court has ruled upon the matter.

(Emphasis added)

[68]        This essentially reproduces the first paragraph of Article 625 old C.C.P.:[29]

625.     Seizure by garnishment is effected by the service on the garnishee and on the judgment debtor of a writ of seizure by garnishment. The writ orders the garnishee to appear on the day and at the hour fixed to declare under oath what sums of money he owes to the debtor or will have to pay him and what movable property he has in his possession belonging to him, and not to dispossess himself thereof until the court has pronounced upon the matter. The writ also summons the debtor to appear on the day fixed and show cause why the seizure should not be declared valid.

(Emphasis added)

[69]        Instrubel pleads that the writ and Article 625 old C.C.P. merely order the garnishee personally to do and not to do certain things and therefore create obligations that personal to the garnishee.

[70]        If that is the case, then the seizure by garnishment would be somewhat similar to a Mareva injunction, which is a personal order addressed to the defendant. As a comparison, the relevant conclusion in the Mareva injunction issued in one of the Cinar cases is as follows:[30]

[95]      ORDERS Defendants Ronald A. Weinberg, the Estate of Micheline Charest, 153114 Canada Inc., 3238369 Canada Inc., McRaw Holdings Inc. and 2950995 Canada Inc. not to mortgage, pledge or otherwise encumber, dispose of, cede, assign or transfer any of their assets, wherever situated, whether held directly or indirectly through other persons (physical or legal), including particularly but without restricting the generality which precedes the following immovable properties and interests and/or rights in the following:

[list of properties, including immoveable properties located outside Québec and property held by third parties outside Québec]

(Emphasis added)

[71]        In another judgment in the Cinar saga, Justice St-Pierre, then of this Court, issued a Mareva injunction even though a seizure before judgment had been authorized. It is interesting to note that in that case, the plaintiff limited its seizure before judgment to the defendant’s bank accounts in Québec, and that it sought a Mareva injunction with respect to the defendant’s bank accounts outside Québec. Justice St-Pierre made the following distinctions between a seizure before judgment and a Mareva injunction:[31]

[9]        Une saisie a pour objet de mettre sous mains de justice les biens visés : il s'agit d'un recours qui s'attache aux biens eux-mêmes.  La mesure est plus ou moins facile à exécuter, voire efficace, selon les biens touchés (tangibles ou intangibles). 

[10]      Quant à l'injonction de ne pas faire, comme ce qui est demandé en l'espèce, il s'agit d'un recours qui vise les personnes et leurs faits et gestes: un recours qui vise à contrôler des comportements par voie d'interdiction(s) qu'il est possible de publiciser, de façon à les rendre opposables non seulement à la personne visée, mais aussi à toute celles qui en ont connaissance.

(Emphasis added)

[72]        Iraq pleads that the seizure by garnishment is a seizure of assets with the same effects as any other seizure. The only difference, according to Iraq, is that instead of exercising control over the assets by having a bailiff seize them, the same control is exercised through the garnishee’s declaration.

[73]        Articles 626 and 638 old C.C.P.[32] provide:

626.     The effect of seizure by garnishment is to place under judicial control the sums of money and movable property belonging to the debtor and to make the garnishee the guardian thereof.

…

638.     If the garnishee declares that he has in his possession movable property, the judgment orders that it be sold, and the garnishee must deliver it to the officer charged with selling it. In the case of currency, bank notes, current moneys, negotiable securities or titles of debt payable to bearer, the garnishee may be ordered to deposit them in the office of the court, or to deliver them to a designated person, according to circumstances.

The proceeds of sale of the movable property are distributed in the manner prescribed in articles 613 to 616.

(Emphasis added)

[74]        As a result, the seizure by garnishment is not merely a personal order affecting only the garnishee, but it has an impact on the property seized:

·                     A seizure by garnishment is “quant au tiers saisie, une procédure d’exécution” as opposed to a “mesure conservatoire”;[33]

·                     It places the property belonging to the debtor under judicial control;[34]

·                     It makes the garnishee the custodian of the property seized; and

·                     The garnishee can be ordered to deliver the property seized to the bailiff.

[75]        In the present case, the property that was seized was, at least at the time of the seizure, funds held in a bank account in Switzerland. Although the Court is not sending a bailiff to Switzerland to physically seize the bank account, the Court is placing under judicial control funds in a Swiss bank account, it is making IATA the custodian of the funds, and it may ultimately order IATA to deliver the funds to a bailiff in Québec.

[76]        The primary jurisdiction with respect to assets is the Court of the place where the assets are located.[35] A Quebec court should not exercise judicial control over assets outside Québec and should not be deciding who is the custodian of those assets. The assets are within the jurisdiction of the Swiss courts and these are matters for the Swiss courts.

[77]        For these reasons, the Court concludes that it did not have jurisdiction to authorize a writ of seizure by garnishment that extended to assets held by the garnishee outside Québec.

[78]        The Court adds the following. If the Court had been satisfied that a seizure by garnishment was a personal order and did not affect the property, it would follow that the Court had jurisdiction to authorize a seizure by garnishment in the present matter. However, as discussed above, such jurisdiction might not be sufficient to justify the exercise of jurisdiction by our courts in the context of recognition and enforcement proceedings. Our courts have no connection to the present dispute other than assisting in the execution of the arbitral awards. If the intervention of the courts of the country where the assets are located will be necessary later to execute the arbitral awards against those assets, then it is arguable that those courts should be involved from the outset instead of the Québec courts. Given the Court’s conclusion on the jurisdiction issue, it is not necessary to conclude on this issue.

6.            Effect of the excess of jurisdiction

[79]        Iraq argues that the effect of the excess of jurisdiction is that the writ of seizure by garnishment authorized by the Court is a nullity.

[80]        The Court does not agree.

[81]        As discussed above, the Court had jurisdiction to authorize a writ of seizure by garnishment against IATA. Justice Casgrain reviewed the issues of sufficiency and falsity of the affidavit, and he confirmed the seizure. The Court only exceeded its jurisdiction when the writ included the words “or at any of its worldwide branches”, which extended the seizure to property situate outside Québec.

[82]        The appropriate remedy is not to annul the writ. It is sufficient in these circumstances for the Court to strike the words “or at any of its worldwide branches” from the writ, such that the property seized must be located in Québec, and to order IATA to make a new declaration.

7.            Effect of bringing the property into Québec

[83]        Instrubel argues that even if the writ of seizure by garnishment is limited to property held in Québec such that the funds in the bank account were not initially seized, those funds were subsequently seized when they were brought into Québec.

[84]        This issue is made more complicated by the circumstances under which the funds were brought to Québec: in particular, Iraq argues that there was a mistaken belief on its part that the funds were in Québec, and that the transfer was made “without prejudice to any and all rights or arguments that the parties may wish to invoke or make”.

[85]        The Court will first consider whether a seizure which was initially ineffective because the property was outside Québec, can subsequently become effective when the property is brought into Québec. At this first stage, the Court will ignore the other complicating factors, and will consider them only if it concludes that the subsequent transfer of the funds into Québec can render the seizure effective.

[86]        A seizure by garnishment has effect at the time that it is served. As set out above, the garnishee is ordered “to appear on the day and at the hour fixed to declare under oath […] what movable property he has in his possession belonging to [the debtor], and not to dispossess himself thereof until the court has pronounced upon the matter.” If the affirmative declaration is not contested and the clerk orders the garnishee to pay the debt directly to the plaintiff, the effect is that the debt is assigned to the plaintiff “from the date of the seizure” (Article 637 old C.C.P.[36]).

[87]        Further, there is only one appearance and one declaration by the garnishee. The Code is explicit with respect to the obligation to declare indebtedness: the garnishee must declare “his indebtedness to the debtor at the time of the service of the writ upon him and of any indebtedness that has since accrued” (Article 630 old C.C.P.[37]; emphasis added). With respect to movable property, the Code provides only that the garnishee must declare “the movable property in his possession belonging to the debtor” (Article 630 old C.C.P.), but this must mean the property in his possession at the time of the declaration. Similarly, the seizure is discharged if the garnishee declares that he is not indebted to the defendant and the plaintiff cannot prove that he is (Article 636 old C.C.P.[38]). There is no obligation to make a subsequent declaration if the garnishee subsequently obtains a new debt or property.

[88]        The situation is different if the seizure relates to the defendant’s salary. In that case, the seizure “remains binding” so long as the defendant remains employed by the employer and all claims have not been paid. The employer must make monthly declarations and deposits, and must make a declaration when the defendant ceases to be employed (Article 641 old C.C.P.[39]).

[89]        For all of these reasons, the Court concludes that the subsequent transfer of the funds into Québec is not relevant and cannot render the seizure effective. Justice Forget, then of this Court, came to a similar conclusion in Van Den Brink on an issue of jurisdiction:[40]

Le Tribunal écarte ces prétentions. Premièrement la juridiction doit s’établir au moment de l’institution de la procédure; deuxièmement on a convenu de remplacer les autruches par une somme d’argent pour une raison évidente d’ordre pratique; une telle substitution ne devrait porter préjudice ni à l’une ni à l’autre partie.

[90]        It therefore is not necessary to consider the other grounds raised by Iraq to contest the effect of bringing the funds into Québec, namely that there was a mistaken belief on Iraq’s part that the funds were in Québec, and that the transfer was expressly made “without prejudice to any and all rights or arguments that the parties may wish to invoke or make”.
