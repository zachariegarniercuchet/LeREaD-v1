Complaint

[1]               A filed a complaint on behalf of B against the Board of School Trustees of School District No. C (the “Board”) and D, the Superintendent, (collectively the “Respondents”) alleging discrimination based on physical and mental disability and place of origin, in the provision of a service customarily available to the public, contrary to s. 8 of the Human Rights Code. Specifically, A alleges that the Respondents imposed a discriminatory fee, based on place of origin, for the provision of an educational program in the 2006-2007 school year; that the Respondents knew, or ought to have known, that B suffered from a disability and failed to accommodate B during the 2006-2007 school year; and that the Respondents refused to re-enrol B for the 2007-2008 school year based on B’s place of origin and/or physical or mental disability.

[2]               The Respondents deny any discriminatory conduct. They say that A never advised the Respondents that B had a physical or mental disability. Further, the Respondents say that A and B are not members of the public entitled to a free public education under the School Act, R.S.B.C. 1996, c. 210 or the Code. The Respondents say that the complaint fails to disclose a prima facie case of discrimination. Further, and in the alternative, the Respondents say that while B attended school in B.C., all actions and decisions made by the Respondents were non-discriminatory or, in the further alternative, made with bona fide and reasonable justification, and/or B was accommodated to the point of undue hardship.

[3]               The Respondents apply to dismiss the complaint pursuant to ss. 27(1)(b), (c) and (g) of the Code. Further, or in the alternative, the Respondents apply to dismiss the complaint against D based on s. 44(2) of the Code.

[4]               A applies for an order limiting publication of any personal identifying information pursuant to Rule 6 of the Tribunal’s Rules of Practice and Procedure. The Respondents do not oppose that application.

Summary of Decision

[5]               The application to limit disclosure of personal identifying information is granted.

[6]               Parts of the application to dismiss are late-filed, but I exercise my discretion to extend the time limit set out in the Rules and consider the entirety of the application.

[7]               The allegation regarding the discriminatory imposition of a fee in the 2006-2007 school year and the allegations related to place of origin comments by the Principal are late-filed and I find it is not in the public interest to extend the time for filing. Those portions of the complaint are dismissed.

[8]               The allegations relating to differential treatment based on disability during the 2006-2007 school year and the allegations relating to unidentified staff making comments about B’s place of origin do not disclose a contravention of Code and are dismissed.

[9]               The allegations that B was refused registration for the 2007-2008 school year based on place of origin or disability are timely, but are dismissed pursuant to s. 27(1)(c) on the basis that there is no reasonable prospect of success.

[10]           My reasons follow. I will first deal with the application for an order limiting disclosure. I will then set out the background to the complaint, as drawn from the materials provided to the Tribunal by the parties, and then address the application to dismiss, including whether it is timely. In doing so, I am not making any findings of fact.

[11]           For ease of reference, throughout this decision I will refer to the Board of School Trustees as the “Board”, the school that B attended as the “School” and the Superintendent as “D”.

Application to Limit Disclosure of Personal Information

[12]           Rule 6(2) of the Tribunal’s Rules of Practice and Procedure states that the Tribunal’s proceedings are public and the information in a complaint file, including personal information, may be disclosed to the public in certain specified circumstances. These include the publication of a preliminary decision, at a hearing, in a final decision of the Tribunal, on judicial review, on successful application under the Freedom of Information and Personal Privacy Act, and under Rule 6(3).

[13]           Rule 6(5) provides that a participant requesting an order limiting public disclosure must set out all the reasons why their privacy interests outweigh the public’s interest in access to the Tribunal’s proceedings. There is a heavy burden on a party seeking to justify a limit on disclosure. The Tribunal’s processes are to be conducted openly and only restricted in certain limited exceptions where it is necessary to protect competing social values of overriding importance: A and B v. Famous Players Inc. and C, 2005 BCHRT 432.

[14]           A seeks an order limiting publication of any personal identifying information regarding A or B, including their names, address or location. The Respondents consent to this request. Given the Respondents’ consent, and the fact that the complaint involves a minor child, the Tribunal will anonymize the names in this decision and will not refer to the specific school location or School District. In my view, the issues raised in the application to dismiss can be fully addressed, and the public interest in the Tribunal’s processes satisfied, without providing identifying information.

Background to the Complaint

[15]           A and B are not resident in Canada. A’s child, B, was accepted as an out-of-district student by the Board for the 2006-2007 school year. A was required to pay a fee of $600 to the Board which A says was not required to be paid by Canadian families. A says this constitutes discrimination based on place of origin.

[16]           A says that commencing in September 2006, B was discriminated against while at school due to a disability. A says that as soon as the staff recognized that B had a disability, they did not inform her of it, but instead started to punish B. A says that B was physically and verbally discriminated against on the school grounds, both inside and outside of the classroom. In particular, A says that B was excluded from play with other children because of B’s disability and that teachers did not offer B any assistance because of B’s place of origin. Specific incidents that A alleges are discriminatory include:

i)        Treats were brought into class and B was accused of stealing one when students refused to share with B;

ii)       A new teacher did not allow B to greet and play with former classmates and did not let them high five each other;

iii)     Teachers made B’s friend come in early at lunch recess so the friend would not be available to play with B;

iv)     Teachers instructed students not to talk to B;

v)      Other children on the playground would not let B play with them and teachers “shrugged off B’s concern”;

vi)     Other students attacked B on the playground and threw gravel in B’s face and down B’s shirt;

vii)   B was placed in games with students 5-6 years older which alienated B from younger students;

viii)  Despite the school’s awareness of B’s need for structure and physical activity, this was neglected during breaks and recesses;

ix)     A parent stirred up animosity toward B with other parents and physically and verbally abused B; and

x)      Certain staff told the Principal and A that they felt offended when B did not display the proper “respect” they demanded from B, especially because B was not from Canada. The Principal told A on more than one occasion that because B “was not a citizen of Canada, B had to perform better than expected of other students, for if B did not, it would jeopardize B being allowed to continue to be a student at the school.”

[17]           A says that B has a mild degree of Sensory Integration Disorder (“SID”), which requires B to be involved in physical activity at normal intervals, not exceeding any average student’s time on the playground or in class. However, A says that B was denied “even the most basic inclusion in regular activities”, and was intimidated and harassed. Rather than being reasonably accommodated, A says B was excluded from daily recess activities and mediation offered other students in disagreements at the school.

[18]           On August 27, 2007, A applied by voicemail for B to continue at the School for the 2007-2008 school year.  A says the Principal replied by voicemail as follows:

We decided not to have B come back to school, as some of the other parents and students have had conflicts with B’s presence, and it would just be easier for them if B was not there this year. We didn’t know you were planning on having B return this year, but when we became aware of it, we had a discussion and made this decision.

[19]           On August 27, 2007, the Principal also wrote A advising that B would not be accepted as a student for the 2007-2008 school year. In the letter, the Principal states:

B requires extra services within the school that we are not in a position to supply because there is essentially no funding in place for B. B would require daily learning assistance on top of B’s regular class instruction to help B achieve at a level appropriate for B’s age. Also, if B were to attend [School], I would recommend that B see a counsellor once a week to help B deal with some of the conflict issues B has with several of B’s peers. These services are over and above class instruction. Unfortunately, if we were to do all that is necessary to ensure B was successful in all areas, B would tax our very limited resources at [School]. We have a very limited amount of time for counselling and learning assistance here and it is more or less based on need each year. We will not be given extra time or resources from the district for B because there is no funding in place for B.

The second reason is that last year it became increasingly difficult for our staff to keep B from getting into difficult situations with other students during recess and lunch. You will recall that by the end of last school year, B was missing many of B’s lunch hours because B was being sent to the office almost every day. It is unrealistic to expect our supervisors to have to pay such extra attention to B to ensure B does not have disagreements with other students. Also, it is unrealistic for me to have to mediate situations that have arisen between parents over events with B that may or may not have taken place.

[20]           In September 2007, A wrote a lengthy letter to D, which appears to be in response to the Principal’s decision. A says that B has “absolutely minimal behaviour issues in the classroom setting, studies well and completes all assignments on time and cheerfully.” A says “the bulk of the problem is the second hand reports from peers whom report second hand information on the playground at recess, often elaborated or not clearly defined to playground monitors.” A expresses the view that all, or at least the majority, of B’s negative behaviours occurred on the playground and were based on student recollections of incidents, rather than the observations of adult monitors. A therefore suggested the provision of more structured recesses for B with closer adult supervision as a possible solution to the behavioural issues. A also suggested that B’s nationality was the reason that B was “being thrown out” of school and that it was also the reason that B had been blamed as the instigator of the negative playground behaviours.

[21]           A sought to appeal this matter to the Board, but D would not allow an appeal stating that it was his responsibility to make a final decision regarding B’s admittance. D confirmed his decision in writing to A on September 24, 2007. A alleges this decision constitutes discrimination based on place of origin and disability.

[22]           A says that B progressed well in the classroom and did not require any extra personnel or learning aids. In this regard, A says B’s disability did not require the Board to commit any additional funding. A says that no extra staff, learning assistance or external devices were necessary to accommodate B’s needs.

[23]           The Respondents deny any discrimination and provided the following additional contextual information related to the complaint.

[24]           B was not a resident of the School District or a Canadian resident during any school year in question.

[25]           Under Board Policy 5100, then titled “Non-Canadian Students”, the Board determined that B could attend the School for the 2006-2007 school year and B did so.

[26]           On the School’s Registration Form, which was signed by A, there were no previous schools identified that had been attended by B. Instead, there was a statement that B had been home schooled in the United States.

[27]           In answer to a question regarding whether learning assistance was required, A circled “yes” and wrote “social conformity – very spirited + needs patient respectful guidance.” There was no response in the space provided on the form for identifying whether special needs assistance was required by the student. A admits in her response to the application to dismiss that “there was no evidence that B had special needs requiring extra assistance” and “there was no evidence that B had special needs nor did the family suggest such prior to his enrolment at [the School].”

[28]           On August 30, 2006, A sent an email to the School Principal stating:

It would be far better to start young B in Kindergarten now, while B would be proud to be there, than to have to hold B back in the future of failure or other problems arising out of not having the sound foundations of discipline and social skills that are set out in Kindergarten.

[29]           At the time, B was seven years old. However, due to B’s home schooling, A requested B be placed in Kindergarten. A advised the Principal that B was “at the level of a 4-6 year old socially and emotionally” and stated that “B has absolutely none of the social/behavioural skills of an average 1st grader, as B has never been required to.” A described B as an “extremely headstrong” child, who had never had the chance to learn how to “sit still, follow simple instructions, stand in line or pay attention in a group setting.” No physical or mental disability was identified in the email and A does not assert that any information was presented to the Respondents prior to B’s attendance at the School for the 2006-2007 school year that identified B as having a physical or mental disability.

[30]           B started Kindergarten in September 2006 at the School and remained in a Kindergarten class until October 26, 2006 when B transferred to a Grade 1-2 class.

[31]           B’s Kindergarten teacher states that she noticed some negative behaviour, but attributed this to immaturity and not having been previously schooled with other children. The teacher felt B’s behaviour might improve if B had the opportunity to model older children. Therefore, while originally it was thought B might transfer to Grade 1 in January, B transferred to a Grade 1-2 class at the end of October 2006.

[32]           B was academically ready for Grade 1, but continued to have some behavioural issues. The Principal and Grade 1 teacher say they attributed B’s behavioural issues to weak parenting, or a lack of parental control, and the delay in starting school with other children.

[33]           The Principal and both teachers describe lengthy experience as educators. They all state that they did not perceive B to have a physical or mental disability and that they were not informed by A that B had a physical or mental disability. Further, A does not assert that A sought any specific accommodation for B due to an identified physical or mental disability during the 2006-2007 school year.

[34]           The Grade 1 teacher also says that A never complained to her about the teacher’s treatment of B or how others treated B. A wrote the Grade 1 teacher a letter at the end of the school year praising the teacher for “a job splendidly done in bringing B to this point in so short a time.”

[35]            The Respondents deny isolating B from his former Kindergarten classmates. They say that Kindergarten class is a ½ day afternoon program with its own recess time. It does not start until after the regular school lunch break and the class breaks did not overlap. They say no other non-Kindergarten students shared a lunch break or recess with Kindergarten students. A did not dispute this information.

[36]           Further, the Respondents say that no one at school is allowed to “high five” in the hallway, including B. Again, A did not dispute this information.

[37]           As well, the Respondents say that B was often rough and confrontational, which included kicking and spitting at other students. Whenever any student, including B, engaged in such behaviour during a break, the student was sent inside. The Respondents say that B was treated no differently than any other student who behaved disruptively.

[38]           The Respondents also say that they raised their concerns about B’s behaviour with A and set out specific consequences if B’s behaviour did not improve, including that B might be asked to leave the school. Many incidents were written up on “white slips”, which are notes of negative behaviour sent home to be signed by the parent.  

[39]           A acknowledges being aware of the School’s concern about B’s behaviours, but differs with the School about the proper approach to those behaviours. In an undated letter to one of B’s teachers, copied to the Principal, entitled “The Plan”, A described a simple goal setting plan as a means to “decrease the efforts we ourselves must expend in the end.” A also expressed appreciation at the teacher’s suggestion “regarding the prospect of my finding a personal Coach (counsellor) to aide us in making sure B successfully learns to master boundaries, and acquire a reasonable sense of self-guidance-discipline.” A was going to look “into childhood education experts in [the area] to help find easier ways to control [B’s] independent ways.” However, A also stated it was too busy a time to do so now, but that A might do so later.

[40]           The Respondents say that on approximately June 18, 2007, A told the Principal that B was going to be taken out of school early. The Principal suggested B stay until the end of the school year and assumed from the conversation that B would not be returning for the next school year. Subsequently, A requested copies of B’s school records as A was considering home schooling B the next school year.

[41]           The Respondents say that it was only when the Principal received A’s voicemail on August 27, 2007 that he became aware A wished to enrol B for the 2007-2008 school year.

[42]           The Respondents say that B was not a resident of the School District, or the Province, and B’s enrolment at the School was at the discretion of the Board. While in 2006-2007, the Board charged parents or guardians of such students a yearly tuition of $600, this fee was significantly increased for the next school year.

[43]           On June 12, 2007, the Board adopted the Out-of-Province Students Policy (the “Policy”). The Policy states that, on occasion, out-of-province and [U.S.] families that are not ordinarily resident in British Columbia may wish to have their children attend a school in the School District. However, since such students do not meet the requirements of B.C. residency as set out in s. 82(2) of the School Act, no funding is provided for them by the Ministry of Education. Therefore, a fee is charged to out-of-province students who are accepted and registered at a school in the School District. The Policy does not differentiate between out-of-province Canadian students and out-of-province non-Canadian students.

[44]           Regulations made under the Policy give the Superintendent the sole authority to authorize the acceptance and registration of out-of-province students, subject to age qualifications and placement conditions. The Regulations also state that the student must not require additional services and their placement in a class must not violate class size legislation. As well, the Regulations state that commencing September 2007, the fee will be equivalent to the “FTE base amount” the School District would have received from the Ministry of Education if the student had been funded.

[45]           In the 2007-2008 school year, the Ministry of Education provided a base sum of close to $6000 for each student enrolled and resident in the School District. While the Ministry of Education provided additional funding for students designated as special needs students, no additional funding was provided for students who required learning assistance or other services.

[46]           D exercised his discretion under the Policy and did not authorize B’s enrolment in the 2007-2008 school year. D wrote to A on September 24, 2007 and advised that “as an out-of-province resident, your family does not have the same rights to access public education services in British Columbia and [the School District] as a B.C. resident.” D stated that because B would require the commitment of additional school resources and time, the registration request was denied. The Policy did not provide any right of appeal to the Board.

[47]           On January 23, 2008, A filed a human rights complaint on behalf of B.

 

Applications to Dismiss

[48]           Section 27(1) of the Code provides the Tribunal with the discretion to dismiss a complaint without a hearing in certain circumstances. The Respondents have applied to dismiss the complaint pursuant to ss. 27(1)(b), (c) and (g). These sections provide:

27 (1) A member or panel may, at any time after a complaint is filed and with or without a hearing, dismiss all or part of the complaint if that member or panel determines that any of the following apply:

                        …..

(b) the acts or omissions alleged in the complaint or that part of the complaint do not contravene the Code;;

(c) there is no reasonable prospect that the complaint will succeed;       

…..

(g) the contravention alleged in the complaint or that part of the complaint occurred more than 6 months before the complaint was filed unless the complaint or that part of the complaint was accepted under section 22(3).

[49]           Further, or in the alternative, the Respondents apply to dismiss the complaint against D, the individual respondent, based on s. 44(2) of the Code.

Timeliness of Applications to Dismiss

[50]           Rule 26 of the Tribunal’s Rules of Practice and Procedure provides that:

(2) A respondent making an application to dismiss all or part of a complaint under section 27 of the Code must have filed a Response to Complaint Form and must apply under rule 24:

(a) within 70 days of the date on the tribunal’s letter to the participants advising that it accepted a complaint under rule 13(2) or (3);

(b) if the tribunal has extended the time for filing the Response to Complaint Form under rule 13(4), at the same time that the respondent must file the Response to Complaint Form under rule 13(6);

(c) if the tribunal adds a respondent, at the same time that the respondent must file the Response to Complaint Form under rule 13(7);

(d) within 30 days from the date on which the information or circumstances that form the basis of the complaint came to the respondent’s attention; or

(e) if the party has applied to defer the complaint before an application to dismiss is filed, within 30 days from the date of a decision denying the application to defer; or if the application to defer the complaint is granted, within 30 days from the date that the deferral is lifted by the tribunal or expires according to its terms.

[51]           The policy rationale underlying Rule 26 is to ensure that applications to dismiss are dealt with as efficiently and as early in the process as possible: Freeman v. Coast Mountain Bus Company, 2005 BCHRT 77, para. 12.

[52]           In this case, the Tribunal had extended the time to file the Response the Complaint Form (“Response”) to August 27, 2008 and the Respondents filed it that day. The application to dismiss was filed on January 7, 2009.

[53]           The Tribunal had previously authorized the filing of the application pursuant to s. 27(1)(g) as it went to the issue of timeliness: Verslype v. Onyx Industrial Services and Crowe, 2005 BCHRT 85, para. 13. However, since the balance of the application, on its face, appeared to be untimely, I requested submissions on whether I should exercise my discretion pursuant to Rule 3(3) to waive or vary the time for filing.

[54]           I will now turn to a consideration of those submissions.

Previous Application to Dismiss

[55]           A says that the Respondents filed a previous application to dismiss on August 27, 2008 which was denied by the Tribunal, and says the Respondents should not be entitled to file a second application.

[56]           A is mistaken about a previous application to dismiss. On August 27, 2008, the Respondents filed their Response. In it, they indicated they were seeking dismissal of the complaint in its entirety based on s. 27(1)(a),(b),(c),(d) and (g) of the Code. However, the Respondents did not actually file an application to dismiss at that time. The simple assertion in the Response of an intention to seek dismissal does not constitute an application to dismiss. No Form 8-Application was filed at that time by the Respondents, as is required by Rules 26 and 24(4). No schedule for submissions was set by the Tribunal and no submissions were actually made by the parties. Further, the Tribunal issued no decision in regard to any such application. The application to dismiss that is currently before the Tribunal is the Respondents’ only application to dismiss.

Document Disclosure

[57]           The Respondents say that the application to dismiss is timely based on Rule 26(2)(d). They say that, in the circumstances of this case, document disclosure was necessary to allow them to assess whether the complaint had a reasonable prospect of success. In particular, they note that the allegation of physical and mental disability was unsupported by any documentary or other evidence in their possession. Therefore, they say it was reasonable in these circumstances to wait until the complainant’s documents had been disclosed in order for a proper assessment of whether, and on what grounds, to bring an application to dismiss.

[58]           A was scheduled to complete document disclosure by December 8, 2008. The Respondents say that in a letter faxed to them on December 6, 2008, A advised the Respondents that medical records relating to B’s diagnosis and treatment were being obtained and would be provided at a later date. A did, however, provide the Respondents with a few general documents about SID. The Respondents note that it was only in A’s response to their application to dismiss that A provided further documentation from a “PT”, which referred to B’s participation in therapy for “sensory integration deficits” in 2008. A did not produce any medical diagnosis or medical records prior to the filing of the application to dismiss and none were attached to A’s response to the application to dismiss.

[59]           The Respondents’ submissions do not specifically identify what new information or circumstances were disclosed by A on or about December 6, 2008 that forms the basis of their application to dismiss. Rather, it appears that the Respondents were waiting for medical documentation so that they could respond to that documentation in any application to dismiss, and when none was forthcoming, filed the application within 30 days from the date scheduled for the complainant’s disclosure. They did so based, in part, on their understanding of a former Tribunal Practice Direction which stated that the Tribunal “will treat disclosure of the complainant’s documents and witness lists as information that may form the basis of an application to dismiss the complaint under s. 27(1)(c).”

[60]           The Respondents argue that it would be a waste of the parties’ and Tribunal’s resources if an application had been made prematurely and lacked sufficient supporting materials. The Respondents note that the complaint form and amendments are “lengthy, complex and confusing in parts.” Given this, together with the lack of any documentation regarding B’s physical or mental disability in the Respondent’s possession or control, they say it was reasonable to wait until after A’s document disclosure to file the application to dismiss: Daley v. B.C. (Ministry of Health) and others (No. 2), 2007 BCHRT 170.

[61]           The Tribunal has held that Rule 26(2)(d) ought to be interpreted relatively strictly. The mere fact of disclosure will not necessarily amount to new “information or circumstances” for the purposes of Rule 26(2)(d): Ehrler and Ehrler v. B.C. (Min. of Employment and Income Assistance)(No. 3), 2006 BCHRT 184, para. 34. Rather, the information or circumstances must, in fact, be new to the Respondents and must also form the basis of the application: Freeman, paras. 12-13. It is incumbent on a respondent to show in their application to dismiss how the disclosure lays the foundation for the application: Alessa v. British Columbia (Ministry of Health Services), 2005 BCHRT 442, para. 18.

[62]            After reviewing the documentation disclosed by A, and the Respondents’ application, I find there was no new information or circumstances disclosed that formed the basis of the application and that Rule 26(2)(d) is not applicable. There are several reasons for this conclusion.

[63]            First, the application to dismiss is brought pursuant to several subsections of s. 27. The Respondents’ disclosure argument only addresses the application pursuant s. 27(1)(c). For example, when the Tribunal considers an application brought pursuant to s. 27(1)(b), it assesses only whether the complaint alleges facts that, if proven, could constitute a contravention of the Code: Bailey v. B.C. (Ministry of Attorney General)(No. 2), 2006 BCHRT 168, para. 12.  The Respondents did not need to await A’s document disclosure in order to bring the application to dismiss pursuant to that section.

[64]           As well, the application to dismiss against the individual respondent was not dependent upon A’s document disclosure, but was premised on D’s employment relationship with the Board and whether D was acting within the scope of D’s employment authority.

[65]           Next, the Respondents have not identified how the few documents related to SID formed the basis of their application to dismiss. The documents are not medical records and are not specifically related to B. While the Respondents say that information about SID, its treatment, and B’s diagnosis was necessary to assess whether, and on what basis, to bring the application to dismiss, they do not rely on such information as the basis of the application pursuant to s. 27(1)(c). Rather, they rely on the lack of such information, as well as documents within their possession and control, to ground the application. This lack of information existed when the Respondents were first notified of the complaint.

[66]           Further, there is no compelling explanation as to why the Respondents needed to delay filing the application to dismiss until after document disclosure. I agree with the Respondents that the complaint is lengthy and, in some parts, not easy to follow and that information about SID and its relationship to B’s behaviours could be useful in assessing whether the complaint has a reasonable prospect of success. However, while it may have been helpful to have had full document disclosure prior to the application to dismiss, it is often the case that such applications are filed prior to one or both parties making document disclosure. It is then incumbent on the parties to provide to the Tribunal all relevant documentation they consider necessary, in the form they consider appropriate, to support or defend against the application.

[67]           Rule 26 does not provide that an application to dismiss may be filed 30 days after document disclosure. If a respondent decides to wait until the complainant has disclosed documents, and to rely on Rule 26(2)(d) for the timing of the application, then that respondent risks the application being untimely and not being considered, unless the document disclosure provides information or circumstances that were not previously known to the respondent and that ground the application.

[68]           Finally, the Tribunal’s former Practice Direction did not stand for the proposition that the Tribunal ­would treat disclosure of the complainant’s documents and witness lists as information that will satisfy Rule 26(2)(d). It stated only that it “may” form the basis of such an application. The information was still required to be “new” and must serve as the basis of the application to dismiss. As noted in Alessa, para. 23:

I am not persuaded by the Ministry’s argument that it can just wait until it receives disclosure from a complainant to file an application to dismiss. The Practice Direction is clear that such disclosure “may” form the basis of an application to dismiss. In my view, the use of this language clearly recognizes that something more is needed than just the fact that disclosure has been received. The Ministry must provide an explanation why it had to wait and, what new information came to its attention in the disclosure that led them to file the application to dismiss.

[69]           While the Practice Direction is no longer in effect, having been superceded by the amendments to the Rules in January 2008, the Tribunal’s approach in this regard remains unchanged.

[70]           Taking all the above into consideration, at least part of application to dismiss is untimely.

Discretion to Waive or Vary Time Limit

[71]            Rule 3(3) provides that a member may waive or vary the Rules, including to shorten or lengthen any time limits in the Rules, as the member considers appropriate in the circumstances. The Tribunal has stated that it will exercise its discretion under Rule 3(3) cautiously: Schofield v. Towers and Affordable Lazorplus Ltd., 2003 BCHRT 77, para 21. Its discretion must also be exercised having regard to the purpose of the Rules, which is to facilitate the just and timely resolution of complaints: Rule 1(1).

[72]           In Paisley and Paisley obo Paisley v. Kerry Park Minor Hockey Association and others (No. 2), 2007 BCHRT 390, para. 13, the Tribunal noted that when assessing whether to extend the time for filing an application to dismiss, it will consider the relevant circumstances, which may include:

i)        The reasons for, and length of, the requested extension;

ii)       The prejudice that the opposing party would suffer if the extension is granted;

iii)     The prejudice that the applicant would suffer if the extension is not granted; and

iv)     The effect of the extension, or denial of the extension, on the full, fair and efficient hearing of the complaint.

[73]           While these are not exhaustive factors, I consider it appropriate to adopt them in assessing whether to extend the time for filing this application to dismiss.

Reasons for and Length of Delay

[74]           The Respondents say that they relied on the Tribunal’s Practice Direction and Rule 27(2)(d) and believed that, in the circumstances, they would have 30 days from the date of document disclosure to file the application to dismiss. They also rely on the complaint’s lengthy procedural history and communication difficulties with A regarding an extension of time to file the Response (which also would have postponed the date to file the application to dismiss). The Respondents say they diligently attempted to contact A to obtain consent, but were unable to do and so filed a “pro forma” Response, assuming the application to dismiss could be filed later if a scheduled settlement meeting did not resolve the complaint. They also note A’s awareness of the Respondents’ intention to file the application. Further, they say the delay is not inordinate in all the circumstances.

[75]           I accept that there were numerous difficulties in communicating with A, but that does not relieve the Respondents of the necessity to secure an extension of time for filing the application to dismiss from the Tribunal. Further, while A may have been aware of the Respondents’ intention to file an application to dismiss after disclosure, A did not expressly consent to any extension of time. While the procedural history mitigates the length of delay given that the parties were engaged in the settlement process for part of this period, on balance, I find this factor weighs against extending the time limit.

Prejudice to Either Party

[76]           A says that if an extension of time is granted, and the application is successful, A will be prejudiced by being denied the opportunity to cross-examine witnesses at a hearing. In my view, the inability to cross-examine witnesses at a hearing does not constitute prejudice arising out of the late-filing of the application to dismiss. A had the opportunity to respond to the affidavit information provided by the Respondents and to file A’s own affidavit material. If A felt that cross-examination was necessary within the context of the application to dismiss, A could have filed an application to cross-examine the deponents. A did not do so.

[77]           A points to no other significant prejudice arising out of the late-filed application. As noted previously, A was aware that the Respondents were going to file the application and was able to, and did, fully respond to it.

[78]           On the other hand, the Respondents say they would be prejudiced if the application was not addressed at this time as they would be denied the opportunity to have the complaint addressed in a timely and efficient manner, without the potential necessity of preparing for and attending a hearing. While this may be so, the delay is attributable to the Respondents.

[79]           However, both parties have gone to considerable time and expenditure of resources to submit extensive materials in regard to the application. In my view, A has had a full and fair opportunity to respond to the application to dismiss, including providing relevant documentation, affidavits, letters of support and argument.

[80]           In the circumstances, I find the potential prejudice to the Respondents weighs in favour of considering the application at this time, but would not be determinative on its own.

Full, Fair and Efficient Hearing

[81]           Finally, and most significantly, the Respondents have raised an issue in their application as to whether s. 8 of the Code is engaged by the complaint. Provided there is sufficient information to do so, the Tribunal prefers to address such issues as early as possible in its proceedings in order to facilitate the just and timely resolution of complaints: Mallenby v. Malaspina University College and others, 2009 BCHRT 208, para. 32; Stephenson v. Sooke Lake Modular Home Co-operative Association, 2007 BCHRT 341.

[82]           In Hughes v. City of New Westminster, 2008 BCHRT 392, para. 4, the Tribunal stated that jurisdictional issues can be raised before the Tribunal at any time:

The Tribunal only has jurisdiction over those matters provided for in the Code. Therefore, jurisdictional issues can be raised before the Tribunal at any time: Brar and others v. B.C. Veterinary Medical Association and Osborne, 2007 BCHRT 363, para. 36. Jurisdiction in the narrow sense of the Tribunal’s authority to deal with a complaint is addressed in s. 27(1)(a). Section 27(1)(b) speaks to the threshold requirement, reflected in s. 21(1), that those complaints which the Tribunal may receive and consider are only those that allege facts that, if proven, could constitute a contravention of the Code. Section 27(1)(g) is also a threshold provision, which has been described as a “close cousin” to the issue of jurisdiction: Imperial Parking v. Bali, 2005 BCSC 643, para. 55. The submissions of the City under these subsections are accepted for filing as all relate to matters which should be dealt with on a preliminary basis as soon as the basis for invoking these sections has arisen.

 

[83]           Given the extensive submissions filed by the parties, it is my view that there is sufficient information before the Tribunal to address the s. 8 issue raised by the Respondents, at least on a preliminary basis, and that the Tribunal should do so now. It is an efficient use of the Tribunal’s resources and consistent with the purpose of the Rules, which is to provide the just and timely resolution of complaints.

[84]           After considering all of the above, I am persuaded to exercise my discretion to extend the time to file the application to dismiss. I will first address the timeliness of the complaint.

Section 27(1)(g) - Timeliness of the Complaint

[85]           Section 27(1)(g) provides that a complaint, or part of it, may be dismissed if the alleged contravention occurred more than 6 months prior to the date of filing and it has not been accepted under s. 22(3) of the Code. Section 22 provides that:

(1) A complaint must be filed within 6 months of the alleged contravention.

(2) If a continuing contravention is alleged in a complaint, the complaint must be filed within 6 months of the last alleged instance of the contravention.

(3) If a complaint is filed after the expiration of the time limit referred to in subsection (1) or (2), a member or panel may accept all or part of the complaint if the member or panel determines that

(a) it is in the public interest to accept the complaint, and

(b) no substantial prejudice will result to any person because of the delay.

[86]           The allegations regarding the refusal to enrol B as a student for the 2007-2008 school year, because he was non-Canadian and because of a disability, are timely, as the complaint was filed within six months of the refusal to enrol. However, unless the remainder of the incidents, all of which occurred during the 2006-2007 school year, constitute a continuing contravention with the timely allegations, either individually or collectively, they are late-filed.

Continuing Contravention

[87]           The Tribunal has consistently held that a continuing contravention requires a succession or repetition of separate acts of discrimination of the same character. There must be present acts of discrimination which could be considered separate contraventions of the Code, and not merely one act of discrimination which may have continuing consequences: Lynch v. B.C. Human Rights Commission, 2000 BCSC 1419, Pavlovic v. UBC and CUPE Local 116, 2006 BCHRT 329.

[88]            In Dove v. GVRD and others (No. 3), 2006 BCHRT 374, para. 20, the Tribunal stated that in determining whether there is a continuing contravention, it must consider all relevant circumstances, having regard to the purposes of the Code:

It must do so in light of the purposes of the Code, including ensuring that individuals who claim to have suffered discrimination are given a means of redress while at the same time ensuring that respondents are treated fairly. … [T]he determination of whether a continuing contravention is alleged, and the length of that contravention is, as stated by our Court of Appeal in O’Hara v. British Columbia (Human Rights Commission), 2003 B.C.J. No. 709, 2003 BCCA 139, a discretionary one. In the end, all one can hope to do is to draw the line in a fair, principled and reasoned way.

[89]           In order to constitute a continuing contravention with a timely allegation, there must first be allegations that could constitute a contravention of the Code: Mallenby v. Malaspina University College and others, 2009 BCHRT 208. Therefore, I will initially consider whether any of the untimely allegations allege a potential contravention of the Code. If there is no potential contravention of the Code alleged, that portion of the complaint will be dismissed. If a potential contravention of the Code is alleged, I will consider whether it constitutes a continuing contravention with the timely allegations.

[90]           The untimely aspects of the complaint fall into three general categories: the imposition of an allegedly discriminatory fee for the 2006-2007 school year based on place of origin; interactions between B, other students, teachers and parents that A says are discriminatory based on disability; and alleged comments by the Principal and staff related to B’s place of origin.

Imposition of a Fee

[91]           A’s first allegation is that the charging of a $600 fee for the 2006-2007 school year was discriminatory based on place of origin. The fee was imposed pursuant to a policy entitled “Non-Canadian Students”. In Martens v. Canadian Cycling Association, 2006 BCHRT 434, para. 29 and Fauerbach v. College of Physicians and Surgeons of B.C. and others (No. 2), 2008 BCHRT 276, para. 33, the Tribunal indicated that citizenship may, in certain circumstances, ground a complaint based on place on origin. In light of this, and for the limited purpose of the time-limit analysis only, I will assume, based on the title and wording of the policy, that the allegation could, if proven, constitute a contravention of the Code.

[92]           I find this allegation does not, however, constitute a continuing contravention with the denial of registration for the 2007-2008 school year. First, the allegation relates to the imposition of a fee as opposed to the denial of registration. Second, there is a significant gap in time between the imposition of the 2006 fee and the denial of registration in late August 2007. The Tribunal has held on numerous occasions that a significant gap in time militates against a finding of a continuing contravention: Dickson v. Vancouver Island Human Rights Coalition, 2005 BCHRT 209, paras. 16 – 17 (gap of nine months), Low v. Registered Nurses’ Association of British Columbia, 2004 BCHRT 70 (gap of 15 months), Taylor v. City of Penticton and others, 2007 BCHRT 393 (gap of 7 and 11 months). In this case, there is a gap in time of almost a year. Given these two factors, I find the imposition of the fee was a separate and discrete act, which does not transform this part of the complaint into a continuing contravention with the timely allegations.

Interactions with Students, Teachers and Parents

[93]           The next allegations relate to B’s interactions with students, teachers and parents. A alleges that B suffered from SID and was treated differently than other students due, at least in part, to this alleged disability. In support of this allegation, A describes incidents that, in some instances, are significantly separated in time, involve different teachers, students and parents, and reflect what could be described as everyday school ground or classroom conflicts. For example, the alleged failure of B’s teacher to deal appropriately with an incident involving a dispute over a cookie, not allowing a “high-five” in the hallway, or students not wanting to play with B, describe incidents involving classroom and school ground interactions between students and the enforcement of school rules that could arise with any student, regardless of their place of origin or whether or not they had a disability.

[94]           The information set out in the complaint does not link these incidents with B’s alleged disability or place of origin. For example, in N and N obo S v. T and a School District, 2006 BCHRT 546, paras. 8 and 20, there was a bullying allegation which involved comments linked to the disability, such as “retard”, “dummy” and “stupid”. While such direct comments are not necessarily required to establish a nexus between a prohibited ground of discrimination and interactions between students, teachers or parents, there must be some information set out in the complaint to link the alleged adverse or differential treatment with a prohibited ground of discrimination.

[95]           In this case, A does not assert that there was any express awareness on the part of any student, teacher, parent or even A, that B was suffering from a disability at the time of the incidents. Further, the information set out in the complaint does not provide a basis for a reasonable inference that the Respondents knew, or ought reasonably to have known, that B had a physical or mental disability and that they failed to accommodate the disability in the 2006-2007 school year. For example, there was insufficient information to suggest that the nature and frequency of B’s inappropriate behaviours were symptomatic of SID, or any other disability, and that a duty to inquire was triggered on the part of the Respondents.

[96]           Whether considered individually or as a whole, these allegations are speculative and do not disclose a potential contravention of the Code. Therefore, this part of the complaint is dismissed.

Place of Origin Comments

[97]           In regard to the comments regarding place of origin, I find that the alleged comments made by the Principal might, if proven, contravene the Code. However, A did not provide any dates or context for the alleged comments. Therefore, I am unable to assess when the comments were made in relation to the timely allegation. The onus is on A to provide this information. As noted earlier, a significant gap in time can be a factor in refusing to find a continuing contravention. Since I am unable to make this assessment due to A’s failure to provide the necessary information, I find there is no continuing contravention of the Code and the allegation is untimely.

[98]           The allegations that unnamed staff made comment(s) at unspecified times, which included reference to B’s place of origin, are simply too vague and unparticularized to sustain a complaint and this part of the complaint is dismissed.

Discretion to Accept Even if Untimely

[99]           The time limit set out in s. 22 of the Code is a substantive provision which is intended to ensure that complainants diligently pursue human rights remedies: Chartier v. School District No. 62, 2003 BCHRT 39. While I have found that the imposition of a fee in 2006-2007 and the alleged comments by the Principal are untimely, I have the discretion under s. 22(3) of the Code to accept these parts of the complaint if A persuades me both that it is in the public interest and that no one would suffer substantial prejudice as a result of the delay in filing: Chartier v. School District No. 62, 2003 BCHRT 39 and O’Hara v. British Columbia (Human Rights Commission), 2002 BCSC 550, para. 20, aff’d. 2003 BCCA 139.

[100]       The reason for the delay in filing and its length are among the factors that may be important considerations in determining whether it is in the public interest to accept a complaint filed outside the six-month time limit: Earnshaw v. Lilydale Cooperative and UFCW, Local 1518, 2005 BCHRT 146; Fontaine v. Ainsworth Lumber, 2005 BCHRT 563.

[101]       A says that it would be in the public interest to accept the untimely allegations because the complaint is “an example of how all people’s not from outside Canada can be treated in this manner with impunity if procedural legalese is allowed to prevail over human rights and dignity” (as written). In my view, this argument is insufficient to sustain a conclusion that it is in the public interest to accept the untimely aspects of the complaint. Many complainants argue that if their complaint is successful, it will have a deterrent effect. This must be weighed against the argument that allowing a late-filed complaint to be accepted, in the absence of a compelling explanation for the delay, could encourage other complainants to ignore the substantive time limits set out in the Code.

[102]       A relied on Chartier as an example of where the Tribunal accepted a late-filed complaint when the complainant was unable to find legal representation. Chartier does not assist A. In Chartier, the complainant, who was unrepresented, had engaged in settlement discussions with the respondents, who had legal representation. The complainant then engaged legal counsel and filed the complaint. The Tribunal found that, in some circumstances, when an unrepresented person engages in negotiations to resolve a complaint, fairness dictates that the person should have the opportunity to file a complaint within a reasonable period of time after the six-month time limit. In this case, A was not engaged in settlement discussions with the Respondents and has not engaged legal representation or asserted that there was an effort to obtain legal representation which was unsuccessful and which contributed to the delay in filing.

[103]       Further, in regard to the imposition of a fee, I note that Board Policy 5100, adopted in June 2007, is now entitled “Out-of-Province Students”. It provides that if the residency requirements set out in s. 82(2) of the School Act for the provision of a free educational program are not met, then the Board may charge a fee to any out-of-province student that it accepts and registers in the School District. Under the Regulations to the Policy, the Superintendent has the sole authority to authorize the acceptance and registration of out-of-province students.

[104]       Under this Policy, whether or not a fee is charged for the provision of an educational program is dependent upon residency. There is no distinction made between Canadians and non-Canadians.

[105]       In Bishop obo Resident Hunters Association of B.C. v. B.C. Ministry of Environment (No. 2), 2009 BCHRT 72, the Tribunal addressed whether the issuance of hunting licenses based on residency could constitute discrimination under the Code:

Thus, the legislative and regulatory scheme distinguishes on the basis of residency, which is defined in such a way to include both Canadian citizens and permanent residents and non-Canadian citizens and permanent residents, providing their primary place of residence is in British Columbia, and they meet certain requirements for being physically present in the province. Conversely, non-residency is defined in such a way as to include both Canadian citizens and permanent residents who are not residents, and other persons whose primary residence is in Canada.

No part of the distinction drawn, on the face of the legislative definition, is based on place of origin. Nor is it even based on Canadian citizenship or permanent resident status. It is based on where a person’s primary residence is and how often and long they are physically in the Province.

Considering this legislative and regulatory scheme, I conclude that any differences in treatment are based, not on place of origin, but on place of residence. All persons resident in British Columbia as defined by the Act are treated the same, regardless of their place of origin. Place of residence is not being used as a proxy for a person’s place of origin. While there may be cases in which differences in treatment based on place of residence could have an adverse effect on the basis of place of origin, that is not this case, where no such adverse effect is alleged.

I therefore conclude that the ground of place of origin is not engaged by the complaint, with the result that the complaint fails to allege acts or omissions which could contravene the Code. I therefore exercise my discretion to dismiss the complaint under s. 27(1)(b). [paras. 25-28]

[106]       As well, in Simon Fraser University International Students v. Simon Fraser University, [1996] B.C.C.H.R.D. No. 13, the former B.C. Council of Human Rights concluded that it was not discriminatory to impose differential fees on international students based on residency and legal status within Canada.

[107]       Under the current Policy, the imposition of a fee for the provision of an educational program is not based on place of origin, ancestry, background or citizenship. Rather, whether or not a fee is charged for the provision of an educational program in British Columbia to a school age child is based on residency: School Act, s. 82. Residency is not a protected ground of the Code. A and B are not resident in the School District and B is not entitled to the provision of a free educational program pursuant to s. 82 of the School Act¸ either currently or during the 2006-2007 school year.

[108]       In Jenkins and Jenkins obo Diabetic Drivers of BC v. BC (Ministry of Public Safety and Solicitor General), 2008 BCHRT 445, the Tribunal concluded that it was not in the public interest to accept a late-filed complaint that was moot. Similarly, in this case, given that the current Policy no longer implies a fee distinction based on whether or not a student is Canadian, but now reflects the residency provisions of s. 82 of the School Act, I find that no public purpose would be served in waiving the time limit to consider whether, under the former policy, the Board contravened the Code when it assessed a $600 fee for the provision of an educational program to a non-resident, non-Canadian student.

[109]       In summary, A has not persuaded me that it is in the public interest to accept the untimely portions of the complaint. A has advanced no explanation for the delay in filing the complaint, let alone a compelling explanation. Having found this, it is not necessary to consider whether there would be substantial prejudice to any person. The untimely portions of the complaint are dismissed.

[110]       As a result, the only allegations that remain before the Tribunal are the allegations related to the refusal to enrol B for the 2007-2008 school year based on place of origin and disability. I will now turn to a consideration of the application to dismiss filed pursuant to s. 27(1)(b) of the Code.

Section 27(1)(b) - Does the Complaint Allege a Contravention of the Code?

[111]       Section 8(1) of the Code provides:

(1) A person must not, without a bona fide and reasonable justification,

(a) deny to a person or class of persons any accommodation, service or facility customarily available to the public, or

(b) discriminate against a person or class of persons regarding any accommodation, service or facility customarily available to the public

Because of the race, colour, ancestry, place of origin, religion, marital status, family status, physical or mental disability, sex, sexual orientation or age of that person or class of persons.

[112]       In order to fall within s. 8 of the Code, A must show that the Board provides a service; the service in question must be one customarily available to the public; and B must be a member of that public. If these criteria are present, then B and the Board are in a service-provider relationship and s. 8 of the Code is engaged. If the Board and B are not in a service relationship, then there is no arguable contravention of the Code.

[113]       The Respondents say that A and B do not live within the catchment area to attend schools located in the School District. They say that, pursuant to s. 2 of the School Act, the Board is not required to enrol students who are not residents of the School District. Further, since A and B are not even residents of the Province, the Respondents say they are not members of the public to whom the Respondents must provide an educational program. They further say that to treat B as a member of the public to whom an educational program of the Board is customarily available removes any discretion and control from the Board regarding registration of students outside the defined district. Therefore, they say the denial of registration for the 2007-2008 school year does not contravene the Code on any grounds.

[114]       Further, the Respondents say that public schools receive funding from the Ministry of Education through taxes taken from British Columbia residents, which funding is used to provide educational programs to its residents. The Respondents say there must be some amount available if extra services are necessary for students who are resident in the defined school district. If a student does not live in the district, and is not a member of the public to whom the Respondents must provide an educational program, then they say the Board has the discretion as to whether or not it will provide the service.

[115]       A says that the Board offers educational programs, for a fee, to students outside the Province. A argues that “if they decide in the future not to do this, that is their prerogative. However, as of the child’s enrolment and time at the school, they were being part of the public with which the school was doing business and during this time they discriminated against the child.” (as written)

[116]       The Board does not dispute that the provision of an educational program is a service for the purpose of s. 8 of the Code. The focus of its argument is on the discretional provision of that service to B.

[117]       In this case, while the Board may not be required to provide an educational program to out-of-province students, it has decided, as a matter of policy, to regularly offer the opportunity of a fee-based educational program to such students. The discretionary nature of the service does not remove it from the Tribunal’s jurisdiction: University of British Columbia v. Berg [1993] 2 S.C.R. 353; Jenkins v. Ministry of Attorney General, 2004 BCHRT 188.

[118]       Further, while B is not ordinarily resident in the Province, B is a member of the public to whom the Board is offering a service in its School District. As noted in Berg, at paras. 55-59, every service has its own public:

… I would reject any definition of the “public” which refuses to recognize that any accommodation, service or facility will only ever be available to a subset of the public. Students admitted to a university or school within the university, or people who enter into contracts of insurance with a public insurer, or people who open accounts with financial institutions, become the “public” for that service. Every service has its own public, and once that “public” has been defined through the use of eligibility criteria, the Act prohibits discrimination within that public.

…

Eligibility criteria, as long as they are non-discriminatory, are a necessary part of most services, in that they ensure that the service reaches only its intended beneficiaries, its “public”, thereby avoiding overuse and unnecessary depletion of scarce resources. The benefits of such screening tools, however, should not come at the cost of excluding the protection of human rights legislation.

…

Instead, in determining which activities of the School are covered by the Act, one must take a principled approach which looks to the relationship created between the service or facility provider and the service or facility user by the particular service or facility.

 

[119]       The Board has established criteria to assess whether, and on what conditions, it will accept and enrol out-of-province students in an educational program in its School District. B applied for enrolment and was accepted one year and denied the next. In these circumstances, B is arguably in a service-provider relationship with the Board, and both the provision of the service and the application of the enrolment criteria to B would fall within s. 8 of the Code: J and J obo R v. B.C. (Ministry of Children and Family Development) and Havens (No. 2), 2009 BCHRT 61, paras. 255-266.

[120]       This conclusion does not, as suggested by the Respondents, remove the Board’s discretion and control regarding the acceptance and registration of out-of-province students, including its ability to deny registration or to charge such students a fee for the provision of an educational program and related services. It does mean, however, that the Board must exercise its discretion and control in a non-discriminatory manner. 

[121]       In this regard, given the conclusions which follow, I have not found it necessary or appropriate to address in this decision the Board’s bona fide reasonable justification argument or whether the criteria established by the Board for the acceptance and registration of out-of-province students are non-discriminatory on their face.

[122]        The application to dismiss pursuant to s. 27(1)(b) is denied. I will now address the application filed pursuant to s. 27(1)(c).

Section 27(1)(c) – Is There No Reasonable Prospect of Success

[123]       The Tribunal’s role under s. 27(1)(c) was described in Wickham and Wickham v. Mesa Contemporary Folk Art and others, 2004 BCHRT 134, as follows:

The role of the Tribunal, on an application, is not to determine whether the complainant has established a prima facie case of discrimination, nor to determine the bona fides of the response. Rather, it is an assessment, based on all of the material before the Tribunal, of whether there is a reasonable prospect the complaint will succeed: Bell v. Dr. Sherk and others, 2003 BCHRT 63.

The assessment is not whether there is a mere chance that the complaint will succeed, which would be the lowest threshold a complainant would have to meet. Nor is it that there is a certainty that the complaint will succeed, which would be at the highest threshold a complainant would have to meet. Rather, the Tribunal is assessing whether there is a reasonable prospect the complaint will succeed based on all the information available to it. (at paras. 11 and 12)

[124]       The Tribunal’s approach was affirmed by the Court of Appeal in Berezoutskaia v. British Columbia (Human Rights Tribunal), 2006 BCCA 95, paras. 9 and 27.  

[125]       The Respondents submitted several affidavits and extensive documentation in support of the application. A also submitted an affidavit and extensive documentation. I have considered all the material filed by the parties in reaching my decision, though I do not refer to it all.

Denial of Registration Based on Physical or Mental Disability

[126]       I will first consider the complaint of discrimination based on disability. In the circumstances of this complaint, A must show that B has a disability, that B received differential treatment regarding a service customarily available to the public and that the differential treatment was based, at least in part, on the disability: Moser v. District of Sechelt, 2004 BCHRT 72, para. 51.

[127]       The Respondents say that A has provided no information to support the assertion that B suffered from a physical or mental disability at any time material to the complaint or that the Principal or D were aware, or ought to have been aware, that B suffered from a disability when making the decision to refuse registration for the 2007-2008 school year.

[128]       In Bell v. Dr. Sherk and others, 2003 BCHRT 63, the Tribunal stated that since it does not conduct investigations, it is the responsibility of the parties to put before the Tribunal all the information they believe is necessary, and in a form they consider appropriate, to enable the Tribunal to make decisions under s. 27. In this case, there is an absolute lack of any medical diagnosis or records to show that B suffered from a disability at any time material to the complaint. Despite having the opportunity to do so both within the context of document disclosure and the context of the application to dismiss, A did not provide any medical records to establish B’s diagnosis, the date of diagnosis and how SID affects B’s behaviours.

[129]       The information A did provide regarding SID was primarily in the form of two letters, dated June 30 and September 15, 2008, from a “PT”, which I assume is a reference to a physiotherapist, though A identifies the writer as being an occupational therapist. The letters state that B was seen in a hospital therapy program from April through July 2008 to work on “sensory integrative therapy, a home exercise program and family education”. The PT recommends that B have movement during recess times and states that “to withhold recess as a consequence of not finishing work etc., is ­­probably not in B’s (or your!) best interest.” The PT notes that movement helps B to focus and concentrate and that the therapy had improved B’s balance and coordination. Finally, the PT states that there should be no extra assistance or personnel needed to meet B’s “sensory integrative needs.”

[130]       While the PT’s letter refers to sensory integrative needs, there is no specific identification of what these needs are, what B’s actual diagnosis is, when the disability or sensory integrative needs arose, and how these needs influence B’s behaviour, apart from an impact on balance and coordination. Given the absence of any medical information, the assertion that B suffered from SID during either 2006 or 2007 is based solely on A’s speculation and belief. Therefore, based on the information before me, I conclude there is no reasonable prospect that A will be able to succeed in establishing that B suffered from a disability at any time material to the complaint.

[131]       There is also no reasonable prospect that A will be able to establish that the Respondents knew, or ought to have known, that B suffered from a disability. A does not assert that the Board, the Principal or any teacher was advised at any time prior to the denial of B’s enrolment for the 2007-2008 school year that B had either a physical or mental disability. As well, there is nothing disclosed in the materials from which it can be reasonably inferred that the Respondents ought reasonably to have known that B suffered from a disability. A admits that B was “very spirited”, “extremely headstrong” and socially immature. In the circumstances of this complaint, it was not unreasonable for the Respondents to conclude that B’s behaviours were a reflection of these admitted personality traits.

[132]       Further, the SIDS literature provided by A states that manifestations of the disorder may include handclapping, restlessness, fidgeting and other nervous type behaviours. However, these were not the primary behaviours that B was disciplined for during school hours. Rather, the behaviours in question included such things as hitting other children, threatening to “whack” a child, threatening another child that B was going to get a friend to “kick her bum”, calling another student “dumb” and spitting. The SIDS literature provided by A did not suggest that such confrontational behaviour with other children is symptomatic of SIDS.

[133]       Indeed, A originally approached B’s conflicts at school as disciplinary issues, though A did not necessarily agree with which student initiated the behaviour and which student was disciplined. It was only after the refusal to register B for the subsequent school year that A asserted that B’s behaviour was influenced by a disability and that the Respondents knew, or ought to have known, about that disability.

[134]       Finally, the materials do not support a reasonable inference that either the Principal or D perceived B as a disabled or special needs student and that such a perception was part of the reason for refusing registration. Rather, they point to a consideration of the time required to attend to interpersonal conflicts involving both A and B, additional supervision during lunch and recess due to inappropriate behaviours such as kicking, threatening and fighting, and learning assistance to bring B up to an age appropriate education level due to B’s previous lack of formal schooling. The information provided by the parties demonstrates that both the Respondents and A were of the view that B was socially immature, lacked appropriate boundaries and needed extra guidance and support to succeed in a school environment.

[135]       After considering the totality of the material, I conclude that A has no reasonable prospect of establishing that B either had, or was perceived to have a disability at any time material to the complaint, that the Respondents knew, or ought to have known, about the alleged or perceived disability and that B’s alleged or perceived disability played any role in the denial of registration for the 2007-2008 school year.

[136]       I dismiss this part of the complaint.

Denial of Registration Based on Place of Origin

[137]       I also conclude that A has no reasonable prospect of success in regard to the allegation that B was denied registration for the 2007-2008 school year based on B’s place of origin.

[138]       First, B was registered for the 2006-2007 school year regardless of B’s place of origin. Next, as noted earlier, the Board’s current Policy does not make any distinction between students based on place of origin. It is focussed on residency.

[139]       There is nothing in the materials to reasonably suggest that B was denied registration because B was non-Canadian. Rather, the materials disclose that B was denied registration because A and B were non-resident in the Province, there was no statutory requirement that the Board provide B with an educational program, and the Respondents considered A and B, for non-discriminatory reasons, to require an inordinate commitment of scarce time and publicly-funded resources.

[140]       I dismiss this part of the complaint, as there is no reasonable prospect of success that A will be able to establish B was denied enrolment because of B’s place of origin.

 

Summary

[141]       The complaint is dismissed in its entirety.
