DECISION

 

Background

 

On May 24, 2013 the Respondent Union, International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers, Local 1432, filed an Application for Certification (hereafter the “May 24, 2013 Application”) with the Prince Edward Island Labour Relations Board (the “Board”) pursuant to s. 54 of the Labour Act, R.S.P.E.I. 1988, Cap. L-1 (the “Act”) and the Regulations made pursuant to the Act. The Respondent named in the May 24, 2013 Application is H-Line Enterprises and the detailed description of the unit for which certification was sought is described in paragraph 9 of Form 1, as:

 

All employees working as journeyman lineman, apprentices, arborist and apprentice arborist/labourer and all other employees involved in the line construction for the Province of PEI, excluding office personnel.

 

 

On May 28, 2013 a panel of the Board granted the Respondent Union’s May 24, 2013 Application and certified the Respondent Union as the bargaining agent for all employees of the Applicant Employer employed as Journeyman, Lineman, Apprentices, Arborist and Apprentice Arborist/Labourer and all other employees involved in the line construction for the Province of PEI, excluding Office Personnel.

 

The Applicant Employer herein filed a Reply to the May 24, 2013 Application on June 7, 2013 pursuant to s. 54(6) of the Act, wherein it requested a review of the Board’s May 28, 2013 Certification Order. 

 

On June 19, 2013 the Applicant Employer filed an Unfair Labour Practice Complaint pursuant to section 10(2) of the Act. The Respondent Union’s Reply to the Unfair Labour Practice Complaint, dated July 4, 2013 was received by the Board on July 5, 2013.

 

On June 20, 2013 a Case Management Meeting was held with the Board Chair, Chief Executive Officer and Legal Counsel, and counsel for the Applicant Employer and Respondent Union, where matters of a practical and logistical nature were discussed. The hearing of the matter was set for September 3, 4, 5 and 6, 2013 by agreement of the parties. The parties further agreed to have another Case Management Meeting on August 26, 2013 to determine whether the parties had been able to resolve any of the outstanding issues which could allow for a lessening of the amount of time required for the hearing.

 

On August 2, 2013 this Board panel, which was differently constituted from the panel which issued the May 28, 2013 Certification Order, met to review the file record. Upon review of the “Application for Membership” Forms submitted by the Respondent Union as part of its May 24, 2013 Application, seven of the Membership Forms were discovered to be irregular, in that six of these Forms did not have the Local Number of IBEW (i.e. Local 1432) identified on either side of the two-sided Form, and a seventh Form had the “Local Number” identified on the “Application for Membership” side of the Form but not on the “Declaration” side of the Form.

 

Immediately upon being notified of these irregularities in the membership evidence, the Board CEO contacted representatives of the Respondent Union on August 2, 2013 to advise of the new Board panel’s discovery of potential irregularities with the Application for Membership Cards. Despite best efforts on the part of the Board CEO, it was August 12, 2013 before he succeeded in making contact with counsel for both parties and advised of the Board’s concerns with respect to the cards. On that same date, the Board received a letter from counsel for the Respondent Union that the Respondent Union was “withdrawing” the May 24, 2013 Application.

 

 

The following day, being August 13, 2003, the Board received a second Application for Certification from the Respondent Union (hereafter the “August 13, 2013 Application”), which, other than bearing the date of August 13, 2013, was identical in form to the May 24 Application. The Board notes that the irregularities apparent in the cards of the May 24, 2013 Application were remedied in the cards attached to the August 13, 2013 Application.

 

On August 26, 2013 a second Case Management Meeting was held, which counsel for the parties attended with the Board Chair, CEO and Legal Counsel. Counsel for the Applicant Employer sought clarification from the Board concerning the written submissions received by the Board on June 12, 2013 from certain employees of the Applicant Employer. The Board Chair in this matter confirmed that the letters did form part of the Board file, and that the Applicant Employer and Respondent Union would have an opportunity at the hearing of the matter to speak to the issue of employees filing submissions with the Board subsequent to the May 24, 2013 Application.

 

Further, the Applicant Employer raised concerns at this second Case Management Meeting that the Respondent Union’s May 24, 2013 Application and August 13, 2013 Application were improperly brought pursuant to s. 54 of the Act, and alleged that the Applicant Employer was not an employer who operated a business in the construction industry. The Applicant Employer indicated at this time an intention to seek leave to file an amended Reply to the May 24, 2013 Application, wherein this issue would be formally raised. The Respondent Union objected to the requested amendment and the granting of leave to amend.

 

The Board Chair confirmed at the August 26, 2013 Case Management Meeting that the matter would proceed to a hearing on September 3, 4, 5 and 6, 2013 as previously scheduled, and given the close proximity of the hearing dates and objections from the Respondent Union, all issues would be heard at the hearing with the exception of the issue of whether the May 24, 2013 Application and August 13, 2013 Application were properly brought pursuant to section 54 of the Act. 

 

On August 28, 2013 the Applicant Employer delivered to the Board an unsigned Amended Application for Review of the Certification Order issued May 28, 2013. On September 13, 2013 the Applicant Employer delivered to the Board a signed Amended Application for Review of Certification Order.  The amendment proposed by the Applicant Employer was worded as follows:

 

The Applicant submits that it does not carry on business in the “construction industry” as the term is defined in clause 52(c) of the Act.  The Respondent trade union, therefore, was ineligible to apply for certification pursuant to Part II of the Act, and the Board was without jurisdiction to consider the Application and issue a Certification Order.  The Certification Order should be rescinded on that basis.

 

As a result of a joint request by the parties, the hearing did not proceed on September 3 and 4, 2013 but rather commenced on September 5, 2013 and concluded on that date.

 

On September 25, 2013 this Board panel convened to review the submissions of the parties at the hearing and the evidence filed in this matter.  It was noted from this review that no evidence had been brought in relation to the Applicant Employer’s Unfair Labour Practice Complaint.  It was further noted that, during the hearing, the Applicant Employer had raised suggestions that certain of its employees who had filed letters with the Board had not been given a full opportunity to be heard.

 

In light of these outstanding issues, the Board prepared and sent a letter to counsel for the parties on October 16, 2013.  The letter stated that the Board would send a letter to the employee of the Applicant Employer who had delivered the letters to the Board from certain employees of the Applicant Employer, advising that if any of those employees wished an opportunity to provide additional information to the Board, that they could do so.  In addition, the Board provided a further opportunity to the parties to call evidence concerning the Unfair Labour Practice Complaint.  Further, the Board invited the Applicant Employer to provide information in support of its request to amend its Application for Review of the Certification Order and to provide argument as to why the May 24, 2013 Application should not have been governed by section 54 of the Act. Finally, if the parties believed that a further hearing date was necessary to address these issues, the Board would grant such a date on an expedited basis.

 

Counsel for the Applicant Employer and the Respondent Union subsequently confirmed in writing that a single day of hearing would be necessary and sufficient to address whether the Applicant Employer was an employer in the construction industry. As such, the issue of whether Part II and section 54 of the Act applied to the Applicant Employer would be argued on consent. In addition, the Applicant Employer confirmed that it was withdrawing the Unfair Labour Practice Complaint with respect to the May 24, 2013 Application, being Board file number 13-016.

 

The Board CEO issued a letter dated October 16, 2013 to the employee of the Applicant Employer who delivered letters to the Board from certain of the Applicant Employer’s employees. In this letter, the Board CEO advised that if any such employees wished to present evidence or make representations to the Board, or to resubmit their letter by way of sworn affidavit or statutory declaration, that they would have ten (10) days to advise the Board. The Board did not receive any reply to this correspondence, nor did any of the employees of the Applicant Employer submit any further material to the Board or request to be heard by the Board. 

 

The hearing reconvened on December 18, 2013 at which time the Board heard evidence and submissions on the issue of whether the Applicant Employer was an employer in the construction industry and therefore subject to the provisions contained within Part II of the Act.

Decision

 

Issue 1 – What is the status of the May 24, 2013 Application for Certification and the resulting Certification Order that issued from the May 24, 2013 Application?

 

As noted above, upon confirmation that this matter would be proceeding to a hearing, and upon the establishment of hearing dates, this panel convened to review the materials on file and to prepare for the hearing of the matter.  At that time, the panel discovered potential irregularities with the membership evidence filed by the Respondent Union in support of its May 24, 2013 Application. Specifically, twelve of the fourteen membership cards submitted were in the identical form, being comprised of a double-sided document, on which the first side indicated “International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers, Local ____” at the top, and the second side indicated, in part, “I, the undersigned, wish the International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers, Local _____ to be certified as Bargaining Agent and to represent me in collective bargaining with my employer.”

 

Of these twelve cards, five had the Local number filled in the blank space on both sides of the card, one had the Local number specified on the front of the card but not on the back of the card, and six did not have the Local number indicated on either side. The remaining two membership cards, although being filed in a different form than the other twelve, had the Local number identified.

 

The issue is thus whether the membership evidence filed by the Respondent Union with its May 24, 2013 Application is sufficient for the Board to find that each employee who signed a membership card was expressing a desire to become a member in a particular Union, specifically Local 1432 of the International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers.  

 

Jurisdiction of the Board

 

Before embarking on the analysis of the sufficiency of membership evidence, the Board must address the Respondent Union’s argument that the irregularities inherent in its membership cards amount to non-compliance with Regulation 3(2), such that the Board is not possessed of the necessary jurisdiction to address the May 24, 2013 Application, in the first place. The Respondent Union asserts that the Board’s lack of jurisdiction prevents it from hearing or otherwise dealing with the Application in any manner whatsoever. Therefore, the Respondent Union claims that the Board should permit the withdrawal of its May 24, 2013 Application, which would allow for the filing of its August 13, 2013 Application.

 

The applicable parts of Regulation 3 provide as follows:

 

3.         (1) An application by a trade union for certification as bargaining agent pursuant to the Act shall be made in Form 1.

(2) Subject to subsection (2.1), concurrently with the filing of an application for certification, the Applicant trade union shall file with the Board the material upon which it relies to establish its right to certification and such material shall include

(a)        a list of persons in the proposed bargaining unit who wish that the Applicant trade union be certified as bargaining agent on their behalf;

(b)        evidence that the persons in the list referred to in clause (a) wish that the Applicant trade union be certified as bargaining agent on their behalf;

(c)        a copy of its constitution, rules and bylaws, or other instruments or documents containing a full and complete statement of its objects and purposes;

(d)        a list of its officers.

 

(2.1)     The Board may waive compliance by an Applicant trade union with clause (2)(c) where the Applicant trade union, by statutory declaration, declares that current copies of the union constitution or other documents required by clause (2)(c) have been filed with the Board.

 

(3)        The material filed by an Applicant trade union pursuant to

(a)        clauses (2)(a) and (b), shall be for the information of the Board only and shall not be available to or open for inspection by any other party to the proceedings;

(b)        clause (2)(c), or material on file with the Board pursuant to subsection (2.1), shall be available to and open for inspection by any other party to the proceedings; or

(c)        clause (2)(d), shall be available to and open for inspection by any other party to the proceedings only with the approval of the Board.

 

 

(4) A person shall be deemed by the Board to agree to the Applicant trade union being certified as bargaining agent on the person’s behalf if at the date of application

(a)        the person was a member in good standing of the Applicant trade union, and, had paid at least two dollars as union dues within three months preceding the date on which the application was filed; or

(b)        the person has signed a document stating that the person wishes the Applicant trade union to be certified as bargaining agent on the person’s behalf and has within three months preceding the date on which the application was filed paid at least two dollars as union dues or fees.

 

The Respondent Union relies on Canadian Union of Public Employees, Local 5003 and Eastern School District, 2012 CanLII13522 (P.E.L.R.B.) where this Board permitted the Applicant Union to withdraw its Application for Certification, which the Union acknowledged as being irregular in a number of areas as pertaining to the membership cards that had been filed in support of its Application for Certification. In that decision, the Board, on the consent of the various Respondent Unions and the interested party, permitted the withdrawal of the Applications for Certification that were deficient, finding the irregularities to be of a technical and inadvertent nature.

 

However, as the Applicant Employer herein has correctly pointed out, Eastern School District, supra, involved an Application for Certification pursuant to Part I of the Act, which permits an employer to file a Reply to an Application for Certification prior to the issuance of an Order for Certification. The evidence is thereby tested before the Board is able to issue an Order. Also in Eastern School District, all parties to the proceedings consented to the Applicant Union’s request to withdraw; therefore, the issue of jurisdiction was not explored or tested, and the Board assumed jurisdiction. Ultimately, the significant distinguishing feature between Eastern School District and the present case is that in the present case, a Certification Order was issued in response to the May 24, 2013 Application, and the Applicant Employer subsequently requested a review of the Certification Order.  In Eastern School District, the Application remained at the stage of being tested by the Respondent.  

 

The Respondent Union further submits that the filing of an Application with membership evidence which may ultimately be held to be incomplete results in a failure to comply with Regulation 3(2). The Respondent Union relies on various decisions from this and other Labour Boards where the Board declined jurisdiction to consider the Application due to the Application being incomplete. Examples from other decisions as presented by the Respondent Union include the Union’s failure to present a copy of its constitution concurrently with its Application, and a failure to reference the geographic area for the description of the Bargaining Unit.

 

 

In certain of these decisions as provided by the Respondent Union, the presiding Board declined to exercise jurisdiction due to the incompleteness of the application before it. However, this Board sees a clear distinction between a deficiency such as an application that fails to contain a required document as stipulated by the Regulations, such as a constitution, and an application where the required documents are filed, albeit with potential deficiencies or irregularities that become apparent upon further inspection of the filed documents. In the present case, the documents outlined in sections 3(1) and 3(2) of the Regulations have been filed and in that regard, the Regulations have been complied with. The Regulations in section 3 are clear with respect to the mandatory filings that must be made, and these filings in the present case have been completed by the Respondent Union.

 

It is this Board’s opinion that once the Board has before it an Application for Certification that contains the documents required by the Regulations, it becomes possessed of the jurisdiction necessary to receive and consider the Application for Certification.

 

This position is consistent with the comments of Justice David H. Jenkins (as he then was) in APM Construction Inc. v. Construction and Labourers Union, Local 1077, [1998] PEIJ45. In that case, a discrepancy existed between the name of the trade union described in the Charter as filed with the Application for Certification and the name of the Trade Union which appeared on the Application for Certification. The Court concluded in that case that the Board was entitled to treat the particular discrepancy as a technical irregularity which did not affect its jurisdiction. Justice Jenkins remarked upon section 3(2) of the Regulations when he stated the following at paragraphs 12-15 of his decision:

 

12. It is a prerequisite to the Board assuming jurisdiction that the Trade Union file the material listed in Regulation 3(2). The statutory filing requirements are mandatory. Fulfilment of these statutory requirements is a jurisdictional question which is reviewable by the Court on the standard of correctness. In this case, the record shows that the Board received the required material, which accompanied the Trade Union’s application.

 

13.  Once the listed material is before the Board which purports to satisfy the statutory requirement, then the Board has some latitude, within the scope of acting reasonably in the circumstances, to adjudicate on the sufficiency of the material and determine whether the Application is made in accordance with the Regulations. I follow the approach taken and opinion expressed by Campbell J. in United Brotherhood of America, Local 1388 [sic] v. Canadian Paperworkers Union, Local 167 (1990), 81 Nfld. and P.E.I.R.40 (P.E.I.S.C.T.D.), at page 53 (affirmed on appeal, supra, based on different reasoning) regarding the distinction to be made on judicial review between the requirement that material be filed which purports to meet the filing requirements, which is a jurisdictional issue such that those requirements must be met, and the sufficiency of the filed materials, which is a matter for consideration within the Board’s jurisdiction, and the consequent difference in standard of review regarding the associated Board determinations. In that regard, the present case is distinguishable from those cases relied upon by the Employer in which a board was held itself or was held by a reviewing court to be without jurisdiction because specified material was not filed as required by the statute.

 

14.  I note in passing that I do not intend this analysis to be interpreted to preclude a board, acting reasonably, from determining in similar circumstances that materials filed are insufficient, such that it would conclude it does not have jurisdiction to entertain an application for certification. Provided a board is acting within its jurisdiction, it has a right to decide rightly or wrongly within the range of reasonableness without being disturbed on judicial review. In the circumstances, s. 4(2) of the Labour Act has full privative effect. See also Adams, Canadian Labour Law (2d ed.), at p. 4-8.

 

15.  In my opinion, the Board was entitled within its jurisdiction to receive and consider all of the mentioned documentation as being from the Trade Union, and to treat the discrepancy as a technical irregularity without consequence which did not affect its jurisdiction.

 

 

It is trite law to confirm that this Board is not bound by previous decisions from this Board or from other Labour Boards. Notwithstanding that, other Boards may have declined jurisdiction in matters before them when faced with a review of Applications for Certification containing technical deficiencies.  It is also clear from the decisions placed before this Board, and the case of APM Construction Inc., supra, that this Board does have discretion and latitude, based on the facts of the matter before it, to “adjudicate on the sufficiency of the material and determine whether the Application is made in accordance with the Regulations” (APM Construction, supra, para. 13).

 

The matter before the Board at present does not involve a failure to comply with the filing of the required documents pursuant to Regulation 3(2). Indeed, all of those materials were filed as required and, in fact, an earlier panel of this Board accepted the materials. For the reasons stated above, this present Board panel has determined that the statutory requirements for filing have been met, and accordingly the Board has the requisite jurisdiction to adjudicate the matter and to determine whether or not the Certification Order should have been granted in relation to the May 24, 2013 Application. 

 

The Irregularities of the Membership Cards

 

As noted, six of the membership cards filed in support of the May 24, 2013 Application failed to reference the Local number on either side of the card, and one of the cards had the Local number filled-in on one side of the form only. The Board must be satisfied that the evidence is sufficient to find that each employee who signed a membership card was expressing a desire to become a member in a particular Local, to wit, Local 1432 of the International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers.

 

This Board is of the opinion that a membership card that does not have the Local identified is insufficient evidence that the employee was expressing a desire to become a member in the particular Local. In support of this conclusion, the Board notes the discretion afforded to it to make determinations on such issues, once the Board has determined it has the necessary jurisdiction.  In this regard, the Board references the powers conferred upon it pursuant to the Act and specifically s. 4 of the Act, which provides as follows:

 

4(1)      The board has exclusive jurisdiction to exercise the powers conferred upon it by or under this Act and to determine all questions of fact or law that arise in any matter before it, and the action or decision of the board thereon is final and conclusive for all purposes, but nevertheless the board may at any time, if it considers it advisable to do so, reconsider any decision, interim order, order, direction, declaration or ruling made by it and vary or revoke any such decision, order, direction, declaration or ruling.

 

 The Board further references the Ontario Labour Board decision of IBEW Local 353 v. P & M Electric (1982) Ltd. [1998] O.L.R.B. Rep. 843 where that board considered a construction industry matter where two of the Union’s membership cards did not reflect the proper name of the Union (referring to “the International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers” instead of “IBEW Local 353”). The Board, after review, rejected the two cards and commented at paragraph 12 as follows:

 

With respect to two cards which did not set out the name of the Applicant, in that they did not identify the number of the Local, the Board rejected those two cards. It was not apparent from the face of the cards that the individual Applicants for membership had been applying for membership in the particular Applicant Union, Local 353. As the cards did not unequivocally indicate that the employees in question were applying for membership in the Applicant Union, Local 353, the Board was not prepared to give any weight to their cards and they were accordingly rejected.

 

This Board is similarly ill-disposed to accept membership cards that do not unequivocally confirm the specific wishes of the employees who purport to seek membership in a specific bargaining unit.  Given the sensitive nature of Applications for Certification, both with respect to time and the workplace (particularly those brought pursuant to section 54 of the Act), the Board expects such Applications to be brought in the proper form through the exercise of due care and attention.  For the reasons stated, the Board therefore rejects the seven (7) cards that do not properly identify Local 1432 of the International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers.  The exclusion of these cards results in the Respondent Union failing to achieve majority support of the employees in its proposed bargaining unit.

 

This Board therefore finds on this issue that it has the necessary jurisdiction to consider the May 24, 2013 Application. Upon considering the May 24, 2013 Application, and reviewing the materials filed in its support and with reference to the aforesaid comments, the Board finds that the Respondent Union has provided insufficient evidence of majority support for its May 24, 2013 Application and the Board therefore dismisses the Respondent Union’s May 24, 2013 Application.

 

The Board’s Options Pursuant to s. 54(7) of the Act

 

As already noted, the present matter is complicated by the fact that a Certification Order has already issued to the original May 24, 2013 Application as filed by the Respondent Union. Further, following the issuance of the Certification Order, the Applicant Employer filed its Application for Review of the Certification Order. Given this present status, with the Certification Order having already issued, and that Certification Order now before us for review, this Board does not find itself to be in a position where it has the authority to permit the Respondent Union to withdraw its May 24, 2013 Application. Put simply, the May 24, 2013 Application has already been adjudicated and has been requested to be reviewed by the Applicant Employer herein.

 

 As correctly noted by the Applicant Employer, at this stage of the proceeding the Board’s options are limited to those stipulated in section 54(7) of the Act, being as follows:

 

54(7)    Upon receipt of an application under subsection (6) the board shall conduct a review and shall either confirm, vary or rescind such order and where the order is rescinded, subsection 20(2) applies.

 

It is clear, therefore, that this Board may either confirm, vary or rescind the Certification Order issued on May 28, 2013, and may only confirm, vary or rescind the Order. At this stage of the proceeding, and in distinction of Eastern School Board, supra, the Certification Order has already issued in accordance with the provisions of Part II of the Act, being the Part under which the May 24, 2013 Application before the Board was brought.

 

Given the foregoing conclusions concerning the Board’s jurisdiction in this matter and the lack of evidence of majority support of members in the proposed Bargaining Unit, and the options available to the Board pursuant to s. 54(7) of the Act, the Board orders that the Certification Order issued on May 28, 2013 is hereby rescinded. In accordance with subsection 20(2) of the Act, this Board revokes the Certification of the Trade Union accordingly.

 

Issue 2 – What is the status of the August 13, 2013 Application for Certification?

 

The Effect of Rescission

 

Flowing from the Board’s finding that the May 28, 2013 Certification Order is rescinded, the Board further finds that the effect of the rescission is such that the Order never existed, that it is rescinded ab initio, and therefore no statutory or other impediment existed on August 13, 2013 that prevented the filing of the August 13, 2013 Application.

 

The Board’s position that the rescission of the May 28, 2013 Certification Order has the effect of the Certification Order never having issued in the first place is supported by various authorities, including the language of section 20(3) of the Act, which reads as follows:

 

20(3)    Where the certification of a bargaining agent is revoked under this section, the employer is not required to bargain collectively with the bargaining agent, and a collective agreement in effect at the time of the revocation of the certification of the bargaining agent is void and of no effect, but this does not prevent the bargaining agent from making an application under section 12.

 

Section 12 is the certification application procedure for Part I of the Act, and accordingly, section 20(3) provides that “the” bargaining agent who was certified under a Certification Order which is subsequently revoked is not prevented from applying for certification by reason of the revoked Certification Order. Additionally, any collective agreement entered into is “void and of no effect.”

 

            This Board is of the opinion that section 20(3) of the Act applies to applications for certification brought pursuant to section 54 of the Act, pursuant to the direction set forth in section 53:

 

53. Except where inconsistent with Part II of this Act the provisions of Part 1 apply to the construction industry and all references therein to “employer” and “trade union” shall be taken to be references to “employers’ organizations” and “council of trade unions” where appropriate.

 

No provision exists in Part II of the Act which is inconsistent with section 20(3).

 

The courts have also addressed the effect of an order being “rescinded”.  In Bickerton v. Bickerton (1975), 9 O.R. (2d) 333 (Ont. S.C.), the Ontario Supreme Court undertook a review of “rescind” in the context of retroactively rescinding an order for support.  The Court commented as follows at paragraphs 19 to 22:

 

19.  The Oxford English Dictionary defines "rescind", in part, as "to abrogate, annul, revoke, repeal, cancel". The same dictionary defines "annul" as "to reduce to nothing, annihilate, put out of existence, extinguish".

 

20.  Black's Law Dictionary, 4th ed., at p. 1471, defines "rescind" as "to abrogate, annul, avoid, or cancel" with respect to a contract. Further, to rescind a contract is defined as "to declare a contract void in its inception and to put an end to it as though it never were" and is further defined as being "not merely to terminate it and release parties from further obligations to each other but to abrogate it from the beginning and restore parties to relative positions which they would have occupied had no contract ever been made".

 

21.  It appears therefore, from the use of the word "rescind" in the Act, that the order made by Hartt J. can be "put an end to as though it never were". As a consequence all of the arrears owing under the order can be "revoked or cancelled".

 

 

22.  It would seem illogical to say that a court may rescind an order, thus cancelling all of the arrears due thereunder, which is a retroactive act, but to forbid that same court from making a retroactive alteration in the amount of the arrears of maintenance owing under the same order. I think to this end the word "vary" in s. 11(2) must be read in connection with the purpose of the section as a whole and with respect to the word "rescind" in particular, and I infer from this that the court must be taken to have the authority not only to avoid an order in total, but also to vary its terms retroactively as it thinks fit and just on the facts in each case.

 

The Board is therefore satisfied that the right granted to it, pursuant to section 54(7) of the Act, to “rescind” the May 28, 2013 Certification Order, results in the Order being “declared void in its inception” and put to an end “as though it never were” (Bickerton, supra).  The parties should therefore be placed in the position that they would have been in had the May 28, 2013 Certification Order not issued. That position, carried through to August 13, 2013, would permit the Respondent Union herein to file its August 13, 2013 Application.

 

Section 12(3) of the Act – “Timeliness” of the August 13, 2013 Application

 

The Applicant Employer submits that the August 13, 2013 Application is not timely. The Applicant Employer relies, in part, on section 12(3) of the Act, which reads as follows:

 

12(3)    Where a trade union is certified as bargaining agent for any of the employees in the unit, but no collective agreement binding on such employees has been entered into, the application may be made at any time after ten months from the date of certification, but not before, without the consent of the board.

 

With reference to other jurisdictions, including Ontario, the Applicant Employer submits that the purpose of this alleged “statutory time bar” as directed by section 12(3) of the Act is to provide a period of repose in the workplace (Greenvilla Homes Universal Workers Union, Laborers International Union of North America Local 183 v. Greenvilla Homes et al. (OLRB) 2679-04-R). However, the Board notes a significant difference in wording between the Prince Edward Island Act and the purported corresponding provision in the Ontario legislation, being section 10(3) of the Ontario Labour Relations Act, the latter of which reads as follows:

 

10(3)    If the Board dismisses an application for certification under this section, the Board shall not consider another application for certification by any trade union as the bargaining agent of any employee that was in the bargaining unit proposed in the original application until one year after the original application is dismissed. (Ontario Labour Relations Act, 2000, c. 38)

 

This Board must proceed cautiously when referred to statutes from other jurisdictions and principles and precedent developed from other provincial statutory provisions that may appear similar at first glance but are in fact quite different.  Obvious differences exist between the two sections of the two statutes referenced by the Applicant Employer, as reproduced above. The Prince Edward Island provision speaks to a certified bargaining unit that does not yet have a collective agreement in place binding upon employees. The Ontario statute speaks to a situation where the Board outright dismisses an Application for Certification; it does not consider the status of a collective agreement. Ultimately, the Board does not consider the comparison between the two statutes to be compelling in the determination of whether the August 13, 2013 Application is timely.  The Board is hesitant to place much reliance on cases from other jurisdictions where the statutory provisions are dissimilar.  Indeed, the Board deems the intent of section 12(3) of the Prince Edward Island Act is to allow the parties a reasonable opportunity to implement a collective agreement in the workplace.

 

Regardless, the Board has determined above that the revocation of the May 28, 2013 Certification Order renders the Order as having never existed.  It is a nullity, “void in its inception”.  Therefore, no collective agreement was or is binding on the employees in the bargaining unit which could invoke the application bar pursuant to section 12(3) of the Act. Further, section 12(3) of the Act ultimately allows the Board the discretion to permit or not permit the circumvention of the so-called time bar as set out in that section.  

 

In the present case, given the circumstances surrounding the Respondent Union’s deficient May 24, 2013 Application and the Board’s findings as set out above, this Board would not exercise its discretion by invoking the “time bar” pursuant to section 12(3) of the Act.  The underlying goal of providing a period of repose in the workplace is not applicable in the present circumstances.  There is nothing in the facts before the Board to suggest that an application bar is a necessary or appropriate remedy in this case.  The Board does not find that the intent of the provision would be achieved by invoking the provision in the present case.  The Board therefore finds that the August 13, 2013 Application is not untimely by virtue of section 12(3) of the Act.

 

Discretionary Time Bar

 

The Applicant Employer further suggests that this is an appropriate case for the Board to issue a “discretionary time bar” pursuant to section 13(7) of the Act, which reads as follows:

 

13(7)    If the board is not satisfied that the Applicant trade union is entitled to be certified under this section, it shall dismiss the application and may designate the length of time that must elapse before the same Applicant may make a new application.

 

In support of its request for the issuance of a discretionary time bar of one year, the Applicant Employer submits that it has experienced prejudice and delay as a result of the procedure followed by the Board in the present case and that the Applicant Employer has been targeted by the Respondent Union in recent years in that a number of certification applications were brought to the Board involving the Applicant Employer or its predecessor.

 

 

At the hearing of this matter, no evidence was brought to confirm that the Applicant Employer had experienced prejudice as a result of either the process followed by the Board or by the previous certification applications. Although the Applicant Employer has had to respond to various applications for certification over the years, it is simply not possible to conclude based on the evidence available to the Board that the consequence of such applications is that a time bar should be imposed in the present case.

 

As the Applicant Employer correctly acknowledged, the issuance of a time bar is an extraordinary remedy. This Board is aware of very few instances where the Board deemed fit to impose a time bar.  The issues surrounding the imposition of a time bar were discussed in Public Service Alliance of Canada v. Canadian Corps of Commissionaires PEI LRB 01-012 (2002). The Board commented at page 5 of its decision as follows:

 

The sole decision of this Board in which the request for this remedy has, to date, been granted in an unfair labour practice complaint is the CAW Canada v. Garden Province Meats (1985) Inc., and UFCW, Local 1252, Intervener decision dated March 1, 1989. That case involved extensive proven instances of intimidation and coercion which were widespread and occurred over an extended period of time and necessitated police authority intervention. The Board in that case granted a six month time bar citing it as an extraordinary remedy.

 

In every case that comes before it the Board must balance interests and rights, and in most of those cases there are four sets of interests and rights to be balanced with the underlying, paramount intent of promoting harmonious labour relations within the Province. Two of those sets of interests and rights are those of organizations, being the Applicant Union and the Respondent Company/ Employer. The other two sets are individuals’ interests and rights, being those of the employees that desire representation and those who do not want representation through collective bargaining. On the basis of these authorities cited and quoted above, this Board is of the opinion that it is quite trite law that the imposition of a time bar is an exercise of a discretionary power that is described and specifically regarded as an “extraordinary remedy”. It is a remedy granted only in exceptional circumstances.

 

Although this Board is aware that certain aspects of the PEI Labour Board’s decision in Public Service Alliance of Canada v. Canadian Corps of Commissionaires were considered and overturned by the PEI Supreme Court following a Judicial Review hearing, the sentiments expressed in the above passage have not been affected or limited by a reviewing court or subsequent Board decision.

 

In the present case, there is no unfair labour practice complaint at issue and the alleged grounds relied upon by the Applicant Employer for the issuance of a discretionary time bar consist of apparent prejudice and delay as a result of the Board procedure and the various historical Applications for Certification brought involving the Applicant Employer. The Applicant Employer further relies upon the two Applications in this matter that were filed with the Board in close proximity to one another.

 

The Board is not persuaded that this is an appropriate case in which to impose a discretionary time bar.  There is nothing in the facts before the Board to suggest that a time bar is a necessary or appropriate remedy in this case, and the Board does not find that the Applicant Employer has presented sufficient grounds to conclude that a time bar is warranted.  The Board is not of the opinion that any breach of fairness alleged to have been experienced by the Applicant Employer is properly or best addressed by prohibiting the Respondent Union from making a new Application for Certification until a certain period of time elapses, pursuant to section 13(7) of the Act.  Further, and put simply, the facts and background of the present case are not sufficient to meet the “exceptional circumstances” that this Board determined necessary for a time bar in Public Service Alliance of Canada, supra.

 

This Board therefore denies the Applicant Employer’s request for the imposition of a discretionary time bar pursuant to section 13(7) of the Act.

Issue 4 – Were the May 24, 2013 and August 13, 2013 Applications in the proper form by being brought pursuant to section 54 of the Act?  This issue requires an examination of whether the Applicant Employer operates a business in the construction industry, as defined by the Act.

 

The issue of whether the Applicant Employer operates a business in the construction industry, and therefore whether the Applications for Certification were properly brought pursuant to section 54 of the Act, was heard by the Board on December 18, 2013. The Respondent Union confirmed at the outset of the hearing, and by letter to the Board dated November 4, 2013 (copied to the Applicant Employer), that it consented to the issue being put before the Board for the purposes of its August 13, 2013 Application only.

 

Statutory Provisions

 

Several statutory provisions in the Act are relevant for the purposes of determining whether the Applicant Employer is engaged in the “construction industry”.  Section 52(c) of the Act defines “construction industry” as follows:

 

52(c)    “construction industry” means the on-site construction, alteration, decoration, repair, or demolition of buildings, structures, roads, sewers, water mains, pipelines, tunnels, bridges, canals, or other works but excludes the manufacture, installation or sale of any prefabricated house or modular home or mobile home;

 

“Employer” is defined at section 52(f) as follows:

 

52(f)     “employer” means any person who employs or in the preceding twelve months has employed more than one employee and who operates a business in the construction industry;

 

Applications for Certification in the construction industry are commonly referred to as “Section 54 Applications” in that section 54 of the Act addresses Applications for Certification within the construction industry. Section 54 reads as follows:

 

 

54(1)    Where a trade union makes application for certification as bargaining agent for a unit of employees of an employer, the board shall forthwith make or cause to be made such examinations of records and other inquiries as it considers necessary and shall determine

(a)        whether the unit applied for is appropriate for collective bargaining; and

(b)        whether a majority of the employees in the unit wish the Applicant trade union to be certified as bargaining agent for such employees.

(2)        If the board is satisfied that the unit applied for is appropriate for collective bargaining and that a majority of the employees in the unit wish the Applicant trade union to be certified as bargaining agent for such employees, the board shall forthwith and without holding a hearing, issue a certification order, that, except as provided in this section, shall have the same effect as an order under section 13.

(3)        An order issued under subsection (2) constitutes, as of the date of issue, a notice to commence collective bargaining and the trade union and the employer, or an employers’ organization representing the employer, shall within ten days after the notice is issued, or such further time as the parties may agree, commence collective bargaining with a view to concluding a collective agreement.

(4)        Where a trade union applies for certification under this section, the board may determine the unit of employees that is appropriate for collective bargaining by reference to a geographic area and it need not confine the unit to a particular site or project.

(5)        Section 22 applies where the board issues an order under subsection (2).

(6)        The employer named in an order issued under subsection (2) may within ten days of the date of issue apply to the board for a review of the order, but the application shall not alter the rights or obligations of the parties arising from the order.

(7)        Upon receipt of an application under subsection (6) the board shall conduct a review and shall either confirm, vary or rescind such order and where the order is rescinded, subsection 20(2) applies.

(8)        Sections 12, 13, 14, 15 and 16 apply to applications under this section but where there is any conflict this section prevails. R.S.P.E.I. 1974, Cap. L-1, s.53.

 

The Evidence of Alan MacDonald

 

At the December 18, 2013 hearing of this matter, counsel for the Applicant Employer presented Alan MacDonald, President of the Applicant Employer, H-Line Enterprises, to give evidence.  Mr. MacDonald testified that H-Line Enterprises was nine years old when he purchased it in 1997 and that the business always performed the same type of work over the years. Mr. MacDonald described his company as a small utility company that, in his words, performs maintenance on power and distribution systems.  The company works year-round and only in Prince Edward Island, maintaining a yearly contract with Maritime Electric Company Limited (MECL) for which it is paid on an hourly rate basis from MECL’s “rebuild” budget.  For MECL work, the Applicant Employer takes direction from MECL as to which lines it is tasked to rebuild.  The Applicant Employer rarely responds to power failures and usually only when MECL is short on crews to meet power restoration needs.

 

 

Mr. MacDonald testified that about 95% of his company’s work is with MECL but that his company would occasionally work with the City of Summerside, various wind energy facilities and Aliant. The company’s work with Aliant would represent less than 1% over the past eight years, comprised rebuild work, and was last performed for Aliant two and half years ago when the Applicant Employer installed some poles to run fiber op cable.

 

Mr. MacDonald testified to the last eight years of the company’s billable hours to determine percentages of work his crews spent on rebuild projects versus new construction.  He described a crew as being one to three employees on a job at a time, and a work week being five days. With respect to new construction over the last eight years, Mr. MacDonald testified that four crews had worked nine weeks for a total of two hundred and thirty six crew weeks over an eight year period. According to Mr. MacDonald and his review of the work historically completed by his company, 94% of the Applicant Employer’s work consisted of rebuild projects and 6% consisted of new construction.

 

Mr. MacDonald indicated that the lifespan of a power distribution system (i.e. poles, conductors and insulators) as being about 40 years, and described the rebuild portion of his company’s work as maintenance of the power distribution system as opposed to responding to a power failure, or in his opinion, new construction. The Applicant Employer’s rebuild work would consist of replacing the power pole; building a temporary line; transferring the line to the new pole once framed and installed; and removing the temporary infrastructure once the transfer to the new pole was completed. The poles and power lines installed would be new, but the same insulators would be re-used. Most often, the same voltage would exist in the rebuilt infrastructure, and if so, no obvious improvement would result from the rebuilt line (other than, this Board notes, the enhanced integrity and durability of the rebuilt line).

 

In contrast to the rebuild work, Mr. MacDonald described the new construction component of his company’s work as being the installation of poles, conductors, and other required power distribution materials in areas that never existed before. New construction work would occur by installing new poles and power lines in new subdivisions (a negligible percentage of the Applicant Employer’s work, according to Mr. MacDonald) and also new poles and lines connecting wind energy facilities to substations or the main power distribution system.

 

With respect to workforce, the Applicant Employer had twenty-eight employees as at the date of the hearing.  Typically, twenty-four or twenty-five full-time employees work year round and are not usually laid off at any point during the year except for perhaps a couple of weeks at Christmas. The company maintains three or four casual employees who consist of retired linemen (typically from MECL or the City of Summerside Electric Utility) who fill-in for holidays or if extra trucks are needed during busy times. These casual employees work from a week to three months annually, depending on requirements, and are told of their work contribution and requirements in advance.

 

The company maintains three classifications of employees, being arborists (described by Mr. MacDonald as being “trimmers of trees”); arborists helpers (those who assist the arborists); and power line technicians (being those who work on the power lines). Generally, the Applicant Employer’s workforce consists of twenty-four power line technicians and four arborists/arborists helpers. The power line technicians consist of foremen, who are red sealed technicians (17 in total) and seven apprentices.  At the date of the hearing, two of the foremen had their arborist ticket while the other two did not.

 

Mr. MacDonald reviewed in detail several weekly timesheets that purported to show the work being performed by his company during the relevant time periods, being the dates on which the two Applications for Certification had been brought by the Respondent Union. These timesheets were introduced as Exhibit “A-1”. The timesheets utilise a series of codes to reflect the nature of the work being performed, where the work is being performed, and for whom the work is being performed.

 

In August 2013, on and around the date of the Respondent Union’s August 13, 2013 Application, Mr. MacDonald testified and Exhibit “A-1” confirms that the Applicant Employer was heavily engaged in work related to the “Hermanville Wind Project”, which was referred to by Mr. MacDonald as a “greenfield” site.  The work involved construction of a new 10 km transmission line from a new substation to the Souris Line Road for connection to the main transmission (or distribution) line.  The Applicant Employer set approximately 100 new poles, which involved digging new holes, framing and installing new poles, installing new insulators and stringing the new conductors.  The Hermanville Wind Project also involved connecting the wind turbines with the substation and installing the overhead collector lines along approximately 10 km of road.

 

Mr. MacDonald stated that everything his company did for that project was “new”, which suggests that the work fell within his estimated 6% of new construction that the company usually completes.  On the date of the August 13, 2013 Application, the Applicant Employer employed seven trucks to the Hermanville Wind Project: three working on the transmission lines and four on the collector lines.  The project lasted four months, with two trucks committed to the project the last two months.  Mr. MacDonald further indicated that the same, or similar, work was performed by his company at an earlier wind turbine project in Elmira.  However, the Applicant Employer also had crews engaged in rebuild work in Bedford on and around the date of the August 13, 2013 Application (replacing approximately 120 poles), and also had a crew engaged in tree trimming.  Therefore, the Applicant Employer was not exclusively engaged in work as defined by Mr. MacDonald as being “new construction” on the August 13, 2013 date of Application.

 

 

Analysis

 

The timesheets comprising Exhibit “A-1” are relevant for this discussion insofar as the determination of the nature of the work performed by the Applicant Employer on the date of the August 13, 2013 Application is concerned.  The Respondent Union encourages the Board to focus the analysis of the Applicant Employer’s business operations on the date of the August 13, 2013 Application.  Given this Board’s findings above, dismissing the May 24, 2013 Application and permitting the August 13, 2013 Application, there is only the August 13, 2013 Application remaining.

 

The Respondent Union claims that if the Applicant Employer was operating in the construction industry on the date of the August 13, 2013 Application, the Board need not look any further into the issue.  The Respondent Union asserts that the evidence in this matter is clear from Exhibit “A-1” that on the date of Application, the Applicant Employer was not engaged in activities which could be described as maintenance or anything other than “construction”, and therefore the August 13, 2013 Application was properly brought pursuant to s. 54 of the Act as a construction industry application.

 

The Applicant Employer asserts that the determination of whether an employer operates a business in the construction industry involves a broader analysis than a simple consideration of the work performed by the employer on the date of an application for certification.  Rather, according to the Applicant Employer, the Board must look beyond the date of the August 13, 2013 Application to derive a clear picture of an employer’s operations, and to examine the context in which the work takes place and the context of the employer’s overall business operations.

 

To begin, based on the foregoing review of the evidence in this matter as it pertains to the work performed by the Applicant Employer on the date of the August 13, 2013 Application, the Board unanimously agrees with the Respondent Union that the Applicant Employer was operating a business in the construction industry pursuant to s. 52(c) of the Act on the date of the August 2013 Application. Indeed, it would be difficult to arrive at any other conclusion.  The Applicant Employer was engaged predominantly in the construction of an entirely new distribution line on the date of the August 13, 2013 Application.  The work consisted of the construction of electric distribution infrastructure in a place that did not have such infrastructure.  There is no issue or question of whether the work being performed constituted maintenance or repair, as there was nothing pre-existing at the Hermanville Wind Project site for the Applicant Employer to either maintain or repair.  Although the Applicant Employer had some employees engaged in tree trimming and rebuilding pre-existing distribution lines, the majority of the Applicant Employer’s work efforts were committed to the new construction at the Hermanville Wind Project.

 

However, without conducting a review of the relevant jurisprudence, this Board is not convinced that the proper approach to interpreting section 52(f), and determining whether an employer operates a business in the construction industry, is to confine the investigation solely to the date of the August 13, 2013 Application.  The Act does not specify that the analysis is to be limited in such a fashion.  Rather, once it is confirmed that the employer has employed more than one employee in the preceding twelve months, section 52(f) of the Act simply requires that the employer be one that “operates a business in the construction industry”.  No further provision exists in the Act or the Regulations to aid in the interpretation, or to suggest that the Board limit its review to the date of the application for certification.  The Board thus believes it necessary to take the time to review the authorities on the proper scope of consideration for determining whether or not an employer operates a business in the construction industry.  In this regard, the Board recognizes that no developed jurisprudence exists from the Prince Edward Island Board on this issue.

 

Although the Board notes and accepts that the established practice for determining employee majority support in section 54 applications is by reference to the employees in a bargaining unit as of the date of application for certification, and the work performed by such employees on that date, it does not automatically follow that the employer’s operations are to be defined on such a limited and narrow basis, to wit, its operations on the singular date of application.

 

The analysis of how and when best to determine the nature of an employer’s operations is intertwined with the discussion of maintenance work versus repair.  Section 52(c) of the Act does not include “maintenance” as a necessary component of “construction industry” work, and various cases have concluded that whereas “repair” work is sufficient to constitute construction, “maintenance” work does not. This issue is touched upon below as part of the discussion on how broad or narrow a view the Board is to take in determining whether an employer operates a business within the construction industry.

 

To assist in determining the proper scope of review of an employer’s operations, the Applicant Employer asks the Board to consider B.B.F. v. Levert & Associates Contracting Inc., [1989] O.L.R.B. Rep. 630.  The Board does not find Levert & Associates to be particularly helpful in resolving the issue, as the case simply reaffirms a tribunal’s requirement to decide cases based on their unique factual situations, and points out the usual difficulty of distinguishing between maintenance work and repair work.  The Ontario Labour Relations Board noted that the difference between the two is very much in the eye of the beholder given the lack of a clear demarcation between construction and maintenance work (paragraph 12).  In the present case, this Board is the beholder who must determine the nature of the employer’s work.

 

 

The Applicant Employer also put before the Board the case of International Union of Operating Engineers, Local 721 v. Nova Scotia Power Corporation (1977) N.S.L.R.B. Decision # 467C where the Nova Scotia Labour Relations Board commented as follows in its Certification Order:

 

[T]he Unit does not include employees engaged in normal maintenance and development because, in the opinion of the Panel, they are not employed in the construction industry.  Specifically, in the case of the Nova Scotia Power Corporation normal maintenance and development includes the construction of transmission lines and sub-stations.  

 

Although this passage is of interest to the Board, given the similarity in industry, the Board must be cautious about relying too heavily on decisions from outside Prince Edward Island where a full factual background is not available, and where conclusions could be reached that are based on faulty assumptions of fact or incomplete knowledge of the background.  This Board does not know the particulars of the work completed by the employees of the Nova Scotia Power Corporation in the 1970’s, and cannot draw conclusions as a result.  Indeed, the passage above as taken from the Order makes pointed reference to the Order’s specific application to the Nova Scotia Power Corporation. 

 

The Applicant Employer has further filed with the board an Agreement between Local Union 1928 of the International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers, AFL-CIO, CFL and Emera Utility Services, dated September 21, 2008, and a more recent “Electrical Agreement” between Nova Scotia Construction Labour Relations Association Limited and the International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers Local Union No. 625, dated February 8, 2013.  For the same reasons and concerns expressed in the immediately preceding paragraph concerning Nova Scotia Power Corporation, this Board is not prepared to attribute significance to agreements from other jurisdictions that rely on facts and circumstances (as well as possibly statutes and regulations) that are not fully known to this Board.

 

The Applicant Employer further draws the Board’s attention to Local 1079A Construction and General Labourers Union v. Fuller Construction Limited (1979) P.E.I.L.R.B. as further support for its argument that the Board needs to take a broad view of the Applicant Employer’s work. Fuller Construction is a one page decision issued by the PEI Labour Relations Board which denied an Application for Certification to the Applicant Union on the basis that the work in question was of a “temporary nature” and did not “adequately reflect the nature and composition of the workforce” over the length of the project. Although the Board denied the Certification Order request, it left the door open for the Union to reapply at a point in the future when its employees were engaged in work that would qualify as construction industry positions, in the particular project at issue.

 

 

However, the denial of the requested Certification Order in Fuller Construction was heavily predicated on the project at hand, being the construction of the Queen Elizabeth Hospital in Charlottetown, and the number of subcontractors at the worksite at the time of the Application. This is exemplified by the Board’s following ruling in Fuller Construction:

 

Consequently, the Board is of the opinion that the work in question does not accurately reflect the nature of the Respondent employer’s work on the project and that the employees affected by the Application are unlikely to be employed by the Respondent employer at such time as the major portion of the project work is being undertaken.

 

In other words, the Board found that the employees captured by the application for certification were not a proper representation of the employer’s workforce.  This Board therefore does not find Fuller Construction to be particularly compelling in the determination of the issue at hand.

 

In support of its position that this Board need only look to the date of an application for certification in determining the nature of an employer’s operations, the Respondent Union directs the Board to numerous decisions where employers were found to operate businesses in the construction industry on the respective dates of application, even when such business activities were beyond the norm for the particular employer.  These decisions include a finding that works performed by a university, an incorporated owner of an apartment complex, and a property management company all placed the respective employers within the construction industry[1].  The essence of these decisions is that, notwithstanding that the employers did not usually perform construction work, the employers did find themselves completing construction industry work on and around the dates of application for certification.

 

In the Page Realty Management decision, supra, the Construction Industry Panel of the Nova Scotia Labour Relations Board commented as follows at page 3:

 

While we accept the evidence of Pam Brown and George Ramia that page Realty’s primary focus is property management and not construction, the evidence as a whole supports the conclusion that a large part of the work done in the months prior to and including July 1998 involved work in the construction industry done for Ramia companies, or for third parties, for a fee.  This is an even stronger case than that disclosed in LRB 1622C United Brotherhood of Carpenters and Joiners of America, Local 83 and Quinpool Properties Ltd., wherein the Panel certified the Union as the bargaining agent under Part II for employees doing “deferred maintenance” on the employer’s own premises. (emphasis added)

 

 

In Canadian Construction Labour and Employment Law, McArthur, Bloch, Salisbury and Brown, 2009, the authors addressed the extent to which an employer must complete construction industry work to be captured within the construction industry, when they commented as follows at paragraph 2.33:

 

To be declared an employer within the construction industry, it is not necessary for the employer to be solely engaged in construction industry work.  One of the leading authorities on this issue was United Brotherhood of Carpenters & Joiners of America, Local 1988 v. Tops Marina Motor Hotel.  In that case, the trade union applied to be certified as the bargaining agent for a unit of employees in the construction industry. The Respondent took the position that it was constructing a new hotel, but its primary business would be in the operation of the hotel once it was constructed.  The Board decided that it was important to focus on what the Respondent’s present activities were, and the Board also looked into its future plans.  The Board determined that regardless of the fact that the business pertaining to the construction industry would be secondary to the operation of the hotel, the employer was still engaged in business in the construction industry as the construction aspect of its operation was an important component.  As a result, the Board determined that it was appropriate to proceed with the application for certification pursuant to the construction industry provisions of the Labour Relations Act.

 

In the present case, this Board agrees that the activities of an employer in and around the date of application (in other words, its “present activities”) are important considerations.

 

The above authors reference Tops Marina Motor Hotel [1964] OLRB Rep. Jan 583 which concluded (and spawned many subsequent decisions that confirmed) that an employer’s business activities need not be primarily related to the construction industry; rather, the construction work engaged in by the employer need only be incidental to its predominant activity, in order to be captured within the construction industry for the purposes of certification applications.

 

In United Brotherhood of Carpenters & Joiners of America v. Kapuskasing Board of Education and Canadian Union of Public Employees (1972), 72 CLLC 16,057 the board commented on the interplay between an employer’s “principal” business, and other business that it may engage in.  The board commented as follows at pages 787 and 788:

 

The Board has, in a series of decisions held that, while the principal business of an employer may not be in the construction industry, such employer may also operate a business in the construction industry.  The fact that an employer does not anticipate it will regularly carry on a business in the construction industry in the future or even carry on one more operation in the construction industry, is no reason not to hold that such employer is carrying on a business in the construction industry.  An employer may, of course, always change its mind in the future.

 

The same sentiment was further expressed in Sheet Metal Workers’ International Association, Local 562 v. Ridsdale Steel Fabricators Inc. [1987] OLRB Rep. April 601 at 604, where the Board commented as follows:

 

Nowhere in the Act is it stipulated that a person must operate a business that is engaged solely or even primarily in the construction industry in order for that person to be an employer in the construction industry.   Nor has the Board required that person’s business be operated solely or primarily in the construction industry in order for that person to be an employer in the construction industry.

 

After a careful review of all of the authorities and submissions presented by counsel for both parties, this Board is of the opinion that the business and activities engaged in by an employer on and around the date of an application for certification are the primary considerations in determining whether the employer operates a business in the construction industry.  This is not to presume that a Section 54 application would be successful against an employer who was found to have operated in the construction industry only on the singular date of application; rather, a board should consider the business activities of the employer on and around the date of application and, as always, on a case-by-case basis.

 

This Board finds that for an employer to be found to operate within the construction industry, it is necessary for that employer to be engaged in the business of the construction industry on the date of an application for certification; however, simply because the employer is engaged in the business of the construction industry on the date of application is not determinative, on its own, that it is an “employer” pursuant to section 52(f) of the Act.  A board needs to take a somewhat broader perspective, taking in the context of the employer’s business operations on and around the date of application for certification, but not so broad a perspective as the Applicant Employer in the present case would suggest, which would entail a consideration of the Applicant Employer’s business activities generally over a period of some years.  Each case must be examined on its own facts; in the present case, with reference to the developed jurisprudence from other jurisdictions, this Board is satisfied that the work completed by the Applicant Employer on and around the date of the August 13, 2013 Application in relation to wind energy projects, and specifically the Hermanville Wind Project, is sufficient for the purposes of declaring the Applicant Employer an employer within the meaning of section 52(f) of the Act.

 

Having reached the conclusion that the Applicant Employer is an employer who operates a business in the construction industry, it is not necessary to further examine whether the Applicant Employer’s “rebuild” work constitutes maintenance or repair, and is thus inside or outside the established parameters of “construction”.  However, should the Board have erred in its foregoing conclusion concerning the importance and nature of the employer’s operations on and around the date of application, the Board finds that the work performed by the Applicant Employer, classified by Mr. MacDonald as “rebuild” work, does not constitute maintenance.  In the Board’s opinion, the act of replacing entire sections of electric power distribution lines with new poles – that the Applicant Employer has caused to be framed – and new conductor, cannot be seen as “maintenance”.

 

As noted, the Applicant Employer encourages the Board to be cognizant of the distinction between “maintenance work” and “repair work”. Although the statutory definition of “construction industry” includes repair work, maintenance work is not captured by the definition. The distinction between the terms has been the subject of considerable discussion in previous cases before many Labour Boards. Perhaps the best discussion on the issue comes from Master Insulators’ Association of Ontario Inc. v. HFIA, Local 95, 1980 CarswellOnt 1078 (Ont. L.R.B.) where the Ontario Labour Relations Board commented as follows at paragraph 29:

 

29.  In our view, it is a question of the context of any given work and the degree of addition or subtraction of such work to an existing system or part of a system.  Where the work assists in preserving the functioning of a system or part of a system, such work is maintenance work.  Where the work is necessary to restore a system or part of a system which has ceased to function or function economically, such work is repair work. "Maintenance" and "repair" are not mutually exclusive concepts, and lack of adequate maintenance will surely produce a situation where repair becomes inevitable. In our view, the performance of adequate and timely maintenance forestalls or reduces the requirement for repair.

 

In this regard, the Board finds that it would indeed be possible for an electric distribution line to cease to function economically if the line was in a sufficient state of disrepair to result in frequent or recurring power outages, necessitating costly and frequent repairs.  Ultimately, each case must be examined on its own facts and through an examination of the nature of the work in which the employer is engaged in (see BBF v. Levert and Associates, supra).

 

The Applicant Employer filed, and both parties referenced, a Policy Statement issued in 2007 by the Construction Industry panel of the Labour Relations Board of Nova Scotia entitled “Work and the Employer in the Construction Industry” (“Policy Statement”).  The Policy Statement, inter alia, attempts to provide clarification on the issue of whether work is maintenance, and thus outside the definition of “construction industry” under the applicable Nova Scotia legislation.  The following excerpt from this policy statement is relevant to the present discussion:

 

A. (2)(b)(i)       Does the work sustain and maintain an operating facility and enable that facility either to operate efficiently or to attain its designed or production capacity? If the answer is “yes”, the work is normally “maintenance”.

(ii)        Does the work assist in preserving the function of a system or part of a system? If the answer is “yes”, the work is normally “maintenance”.

(iii)       Is the work necessary to restore a system or significant part of a system that has ceased to function or to function economically? If the answer is “yes”, the work is normally “maintenance”.

(iv)       Does the work involve an addition to an existing facility? If the answer is “no”, the work is normally “maintenance”.

(v)        Does the work increase the designed or production capacity of an existing facility? If the answer is “no”, the work is normally “maintenance”.

(vi)       The size and complexity of the project in relation to the normal maintenance capacity and activities of an owner of a pre-existing facility may be taken into account in answering the above questions.

 

The Board has considered the Policy Statement and the submissions of counsel for the parties at the hearing of this matter.  Ultimately, the Policy Statement is certainly not binding upon this Board; it contains references to terms that are undefined and seemingly irrelevant to the present case (e.g. “facility”); it can be read and applied to arrive at two different outcomes in the present case; it contradicts the oft-cited case of Master Insulators, supra, without explanation (at paragraph A.(2)(b)(iii)); and was seemingly designed to assist relevant parties in the Nova Scotia construction industry pursuant to Nova Scotia legislation.  The Board, therefore, is hesitant to place much, if any, reliance on such a document.

 

Having regard to all of the evidence in this matter and the authorities and submissions presented by counsel for the parties, and the totality and nature of the work performed by the Applicant Employer, which includes work that is unequivocally new construction, as well as the framing and installation of new power poles and new conductors during rebuild work, this Board finds that the Applicant Employer does operate a business within the construction industry.  In coming to this determination, the Board has considered the activities and operations of the Applicant Employer on the date of the August 13, 2013 Application, the time period surrounding the date of the August 13, 2013 Application, and such activities and operations of the Applicant Employer generally.  Therefore, whether approached from the perspective of the date of the August 13, 2013 Application for Certification, or from a much broader perspective that considers the general nature of the Applicant Employer’s work, the Board finds that the Applicant Employer is an employer who operates a business in the construction industry.

 

With reference to section 52(c) of the Act, the Board takes note of the lack of reference to electricity or power distribution systems in the definition of “construction industry” pursuant to s. 52(c) of the Act. However, that omission is not definitive of whether or not the Applicant Employer’s business is or is not a construction industry business. The drafters of the legislation clearly did not intend to confine the definition of “construction industry” to the stated areas. The use of the words “or other works” within the definition clearly allows for electrical/power lines or other works related to those items listed in the definition to be included within the definition of “construction industry”. Although the inclusion of sewers and water mains, for example, and the omission of electric/power lines and systems does present a curiosity on a strict reading, this Board does find that the infrastructure component of electric lines and systems does fit within the definition, by virtue of the “or others works” provision and by reference to the similarity of other infrastructure works that are listed within the definition.[2]

 

 

The Applicant Employer submits that the distinction within the Act for the construction industry is premised upon the transient nature of the workplace often seen in the construction industry, which sees workers travelling from job to job.  Therefore, according to the Applicant Employer, section 54 would be improperly applied to H-Line Enterprises, given the nature of its workforce which has historically been more stable than that typically seen within the construction industry.

 

In this regard, the Applicant Employer references Gaddess Industrial Inc. and UBCJA, Local 2262, 2013CarswellNB 049 where the New Brunswick Labour and Employment Board recently stated as follows at paragraph 31:

 

There are significant differences between general industry work and that of the construction industry. For example, the construction industry workers exercise, in most cases, a particular craft or skill, the workforce changes frequently and varies from job site to job site which, too, is subject to frequent changes. The work is largely seasonal resulting in a rapid expansion and contraction of the workforce. As a result, separate legislative provisions have been enacted applicable solely to the construction industry. It is necessary, then, in a certification application under the construction industry provisions, for the Board to be satisfied that its process is appropriate to the factual situation out of which the application arises.

 

This Board, as with all other Boards, must examine the relevant factual situation when addressing a construction industry application for certification.

 

The Applicant Employer further references IUOE, Local 793 v. Smiths Construction Co. 1984CarswellOnt 997 which also speaks to the transitory nature of employment within the construction industry.

 

However, there is more to the analysis of the appropriateness of applying pursuant to section 54 of the Act than whether or not an employer named in a certification application has a workforce that changes frequently. Indeed, the transitory nature of the work force is but one consideration that has developed in the jurisprudence and is not solely determinative of the issue.  Further, such a criterion is not found within the Act.  Although recognizing that the workforce within the construction industry does frequently expand and contract, as noted by Gaddess, supra, and that Section 54 applications are designed in part to address this phenomenon, this Board cannot conclude that the August 13, 2013 Application pursuant to section 54 must be struck simply because the Applicant Employer’s workforce may not necessarily fall into the typical transient construction industry workforce.

 

 

The Applicant Employer further asks the Board to consider the previous applications for certification brought over the years against H-Line Enterprises, which were not brought pursuant to section 54 of the Act. The Applicant Employer asserts that the Board must defer to its past practice and rulings, and should not depart from precedent unless there is good reason to do so, following a change in fact or law.  In support of this position, the Applicant Employer references Administrative Law in Canada, 5th Ed. where at pages 139 and 140, the author Sara Blake comments as follows:

 

A departure from a previous ruling should be explained.  The analytical framework of previous decisions should be reviewed to reduce the risk of arbitrariness and the tribunal should be open to argument as to why a previous decision ought not to be followed. Otherwise, the different decision may be regarded as an aberration.

 

Although the Board acknowledges that previous applications for certification involving the Applicant Employer were apparently not brought pursuant to section 54 of Act, this Board does not have any knowledge or evidence to suggest that an analysis was ever undertaken with respect to the appropriateness of a section 54 application for certification for this particular employer.  None of the materials before the board pertaining to previous applications for certification against the Applicant Employer suggest that the issue was ever raised, let alone considered and adjudicated.

 

This Board is not bound by past practice and circumstances within an employer’s industry, and the nature of an employer’s work can change from time to time. To rely on a process or manner of proceeding from years ago, without an in-depth examination of an employer’s activities at the time relative to present, could lead to a faulty analysis and a very erroneous conclusion. This does not mean that the Board is departing from prior practice. As noted, there is no evidence before the Board as to whether an examination was ever undertaken of the appropriateness (or inappropriateness) of section 54 for this particular employer.  To reach the conclusion in the present matter that the Applicant Employer is engaged in the construction industry would not be seen as an “aberration” or as a “departure from a previous ruling” of the Board.  In this regard, the Board further references Administrative Law in Canada, supra, where Ms. Blake comments as follows:

 

When a tribunal is faced with a new case raising legal or policy issues similar to those decided in a previous case between the same parties, the tribunal is not bound by the concept of res judicata.  This flexibility enables a tribunal to apply the public interest in a way that reflects the evolution of policy and effectively regulates dynamic and ongoing relationships between parties. A tribunal may permit re-litigation and may come to a different conclusion without risk of court interference.  However, the importance of stability in an industry requires that a tribunal have good reason for reversing its decisions.

 

            . . . .

 

The principle of stare decisis does not apply to tribunals.  A tribunal is not bound to follow its own previous decisions on similar issues. Its decisions may reflect changing circumstances and evolving policy in the field it governs.

 

This Board is therefore not compelled to adhere to the past practice of the parties, or the Board, in matters of previous applications for certification.  For the reasons already stated above, the Board does not find such historical applications compelling in the present matter.

 

 Therefore, pursuant to the foregoing analysis and for the above-stated reasons and conclusions, the Board finds that the Applicant Employer is an employer that operates a business in the construction industry and that the Respondent Union’s August 2013 Application for Certification was properly brought pursuant to section 54 of the Act.

 

Issue 5 – Has the Applicant Employer been denied procedural fairness or been prejudiced in any manner as a result of the Board’s process in this matter?

 

            The Applicant Employer submits that the process employed by the Board in this case was procedurally unfair to the Applicant Employer, amounting to a breach of the rules of fairness and natural justice and resulting in prejudice to the Applicant Employer.  The Applicant Employer brings several claims in this regard, which the Board summarizes as follows:

 

·         The procedure adopted by the Board and/or the Board CEO in this matter was not clear to the parties or, in the alternative, was not made known to each of the parties, on an equal basis.

 

·         The Board made a substantive decision or decisions at a point prior to both parties in the matter having an equal opportunity to consider the issues and the evidence and make submissions on the issues and the evidence.

 

·         The Board CEO was acting outside of the powers afforded to the position of CEO pursuant to the Act and the Regulations.

 

·         The Board failed to give proper consideration to the expression of wishes of the employees and failed to allow the employees an opportunity to be heard.

 

·         Allowing the August 13, 2013 Application for Certification to be filed sanctions the practice of withdrawing an application and re-filing on a date more favourable to the Union, which creates an abuse of process.

 

To reiterate the background review of this matter set out at the beginning of this decision, on August 2, 2013 this Board panel discovered potential irregularities with the membership evidence filed in support of the Respondent Union’s May 24, 2013 Application.  The Board CEO was instructed to advise the Respondent Union of the irregularities and on the same date the CEO made contact with a representative of the Respondent Union.  On August 12, 2013 the Board CEO was first able to speak with counsel for both parties.  It was evidently during this August 12, 2013 phone conversation that the Applicant Employer was first notified of a potential issue involving the Respondent Union’s membership evidence.  Also, it was on August 12, 2013 that the Respondent Union filed its request to withdraw its May 24, 2013 Application.  The next day, August 13, 2013, the Respondent Union submitted to the Board its August 13, 2013 Application.

 

Ultimately, a period of ten (10) days passed where the Respondent Union was aware of potential membership evidence irregularities, and the Applicant Employer was not. The Applicant Employer became fully appraised of the issue on August 23, 2013, upon receipt of correspondence from counsel for the Respondent Union that summarised the potential membership evidence issue and set forth the Respondent Union’s position in the matter.  Therefore, within three (3) weeks of this Board discovering potential irregularities with the membership evidence, the Applicant Employer was possessed of all information pertaining to the membership evidence issue that was capable of being disclosed, considering the information disclosure limitations set out in Section 3(13) of the Act and Regulation 3(3).

 

From the immediately foregoing review, it is clear that no substantive decisions were made by the Board (or by the Board CEO) at any point during the administration of the matter and prior to the conclusion of the hearing.  In this regard, and with reference to the Applicant Employer’s written and oral submissions in this matter, the Applicant Employer appears to misunderstand both the role of the Board CEO vis-a-vis the Board, and the nature of the decisions that the Board or the Board CEO made during the administration of this matter.  At paragraph 51 of the Applicant Employer’s Post-Hearing Submissions, the Applicant Employer states that “the Board made a decision to allow a withdrawal and re-filing” (of the Respondent Union’s May 24, 2013 and August 13, 2013 Applications for Certification, respectively).  This is incorrect.  The Board did not permit the withdrawal of the May 24, 2013 Application, and the August 13, 2013 Application was held in abeyance pending the Board’s determination of the May 24, 2013 Application, which is only now complete.  Neither the Board nor the Board CEO made any decisions of a substantive nature throughout the administration of this matter.  It is only now, following the completion of the hearing and with this Board Decision, has this Board made any decisions of a substantive nature.

 

It cannot be said that the Board CEO was acting ultra vires the authority of his position pursuant to the Act.  The CEO provides administration while the Board decides the issues.  With reference to the Applicant Employer’s submissions, the Board agrees that the CEO plays an important and integral role in mediating labour disputes; attempting to resolve disputes informally and expeditiously; and by affording the parties an intermediary through which to communicate.  The Board CEO’s role is thus tremendously important and valuable to the smooth operation of the Prince Edward Island Labour Relations Board and to the parties that bring matters before it.

 

This Board cannot find that any of the CEO’s actions in this matter resulted in him exceeding his authority as granted by the Act or usurping the role of the Board as the substantive decision-maker.  There is no evidence before the Board to suggest that the CEO made any decisions of a substantive nature, or decisions that either caused prejudice to the Applicant Employer or impacted the outcome of this matter, or any of its component issues. 

 

            As part of the Applicant Employer’s submissions on the role of the Board CEO in this matter, counsel for the Applicant Employer referenced so-called “Rules of Procedure” of the Board, dated June 19, 1995, and which were included at Tab 29 of the Applicant Employer’s Post-Hearing Submissions.  Upon further investigation into this document, the Board confirms that such “Rules of Procedure” have never been adopted or implemented by the Board.  Therefore, the Board has not considered the “Rules of Procedure” as submitted by the Applicant Employer or the submissions of counsel for either party insofar as they relied upon or referenced this document.

 

            To conclude on the issue of the role played by the Board CEO in this matter, the Board takes issue with the allegations raised against the Board CEO, which were unfounded.  The Board CEO is an officer appointed by the Minister pursuant to statutory authority.  His function is to carry out the duties prescribed by the Minister or otherwise ordered by the Board, and he plays a facilitating role for all parties before the Board.  As the Applicant Employer in this matter was – or should have been – well aware, none of the issues that went to hearing were pre-determined by either the Board or the Board CEO.  The determination of issues was the reason for holding the hearing in the first place.  The Board CEO does not have any authority to make such determinations, and none were made by him.

 

            The Applicant Employer further claims to have been entitled to know and to test the veracity and the extent of the Respondent Union’s evidence of majority support, and to have been entitled to know that the evidence of majority support was insufficient. With reference to the above discussion pursuant to Issue No. 1, this Board has found that the membership evidence of majority support was wanting, resulting in the dismissal of the Respondent Union’s May 24, 2013 Application.  This Board has the right and jurisdiction to make this finding.  Although the previous Board panel that originally reviewed the membership evidence and ultimately issued the Certification Order did not arrive at the same conclusion, it very well could have made the same finding as this Board panel, and the Applicant Employer would not have had any greater standing to know and to test the veracity and extent of the Respondent Union’s evidence of support. In a construction industry application, the employer does not see the membership evidence anyway, but does retain the right to file a Reply to any Certification Order that may issue (as the Applicant Employer has done in the present case).  In either event, the Applicant Employer’s requested outcome is achieved: the May 24, 2013 Application has failed.  This Board therefore cannot find that the Applicant Employer was in any way prejudiced by the Board’s dismissal of the May 24, 2013 Application.  The Board is acting within its right and its jurisdiction in dismissing the May 24, 2013 Application.

 

The Board permitted full opportunity to the Applicant Employer to make submissions – orally and in writing – with respect to the membership evidence and the manner and procedure in which the Applicant Employer believed that particular issue should be addressed.  The Applicant Employer had an opportunity to present its perspective on the matter on August 26, 2013 at a Case Management Meeting involving the Board Chair, CEO and Legal Counsel, and counsel for the Respondent Union.  This Case Management Meeting was held three (3) days after the Applicant Employer’s receipt of the Respondent Union’s August 23, 2013 letter, referenced above.  The Applicant Employer further had full opportunity to make submissions at the September 5, 2013 hearing of this matter, and the Applicant Employer filed extensive post-hearing written submissions, including a Reply to the Respondent Union’s post-hearing submissions.  Therefore, the Board cannot conclude based on the facts and evidence in this matter that the Applicant Employer has been prejudiced or has experienced a denial of procedural fairness.

 

Further, this Board cannot find that the wishes of the Applicant Employer’s employees were ignored in this matter, or that they did not have an opportunity to be heard.  On June 12, 2013 the Board received various statements of desire purported to be signed by certain of the Applicant Employer’s employees.  These statements indicated a desire to not be included in a collective bargaining unit.  During the August 26, 2013 Case Management Meeting with counsel for the parties, the Board Chair confirmed that these statements did form part of the Board file and would be considered by the Board.  The Board again confirms, at this time, that such statements have been included in the Board’s review and consideration of this matter.

 

In addition, the present matter originally provided for four (4) hearing dates in September 2013 and a further hearing date in December 2013.  Counsel for both parties had ample opportunity to call any employees who wanted to give evidence in this matter to the Board.  No employees were called to give evidence.

 

The Board further notes that a letter issued from the Board CEO on October 16, 2013 to the individual who delivered the alleged employee “statements of desire” to the Board. In this letter, the Board CEO advised that if any such individuals wished to present evidence or make representations to the Board, or to resubmit their written statements by way of sworn affidavit or statutory declaration, that they would have ten (10) days to advise the Board. The Board did not receive any such request from an employee, or any reply whatsoever to this correspondence.

 

In the end, given this Board’s finding that the May 24, 2013 Application for Certification is dismissed, the employees who submitted written statements of their desire to not be included in collective bargaining ultimately received their desired outcome as it pertained to the May 24, 2013 Application.

 

The Applicant Employer further claims that it has suffered prejudice as a result of the Respondent Union having the opportunity to file its August 13, 2013 Application, upon having been advised of the potential irregularities with the membership evidence filed in support of its May 24, 2013 Application.  Given the importance of the date of application in construction industry applications for certification, the Respondent Union, as submitted by the Applicant Employer, thus had the opportunity to “date shop” for the most advantageous date for its application, including the date when the most pro-union employees were at work.

 

The right to bring an application for certification on the most advantageous date exists for a Union regardless, as the Union decides when it will file its Application, in its sole discretion.  This right exists either at the outset of a matter – giving rise to a new Board file being created – or following the conclusion of a time bar, for example.  Unless subject to an application bar, a Union is always at liberty to bring an application for certification on a chosen date that may or may not withstand review.  In the present matter, the Respondent Union, upon being advised of the potential irregularities with the membership evidence, had the option to do nothing, and rely on the membership evidence as submitted to see if this Board ultimately accepted the evidence as did the original Board Panel.  Instead, the Respondent Union chose to abandon its May 24, 2013 Application and attempt to withdraw and re-file.  The Board cannot conclude on the evidence that the Respondent Union took advantage of or exploited the process in this matter to its own benefit.  Any Certification Order that may issue from the August 13, 2013 Application for Certification would afford the usual opportunities to the Applicant Employer to test the Application on review and challenge the Respondent Union’s assertions of membership support.

 

The Board therefore finds that allowing the August 13, 2013 Application to be filed in this matter does not create an abuse of process that prejudices the Applicant Employer.  Further, the Board does not accept the submissions of the Applicant Employer that allowing the August 13, 2013 Application to be filed in this matter would open a Pandora’s Box of Applicant Unions that are filing and withdrawing, or “date shopping”, in the effort to achieve a filing on the most advantageous date.  This Board has the discretion and authority to permit or deny any request by an Applicant Union to withdraw and/or re-file and the Board would exercise such discretion and authority on a case-by-case basis, considering the facts and circumstances in each case. 

 

Finally, the Board would question the options available to it even if an abuse of process was determined in the present matter.  Section 13(5) of the Act is imperative in its direction that once the Board determines that a majority of employees in an appropriate bargaining unit support an Application for Certification, the Board shall certify the trade union as the bargaining agent of the employees in that unit.  Although the Board is afforded considerable discretion pursuant to the Act and its Regulations, such discretion would appear not to extend to override the specific imperative mandated by Section 13(5) of the Act. Any recourse that may be available to the Board in the event of a finding of abuse of process would not include the option of denying an Application for Certification that was filed in the proper form and in compliance with the statutory requirements and which showed evidence of majority support.

 

The Board cannot conclude, based on the facts and background of this matter, that it breached its duty of fairness to the Applicant Employer or that the Applicant Employer’s position was prejudiced either during the proceeding or in the final analysis and outcome.  Finally, the Board further notes that there is no outstanding unfair labour practice complaint before it as alleged by the Applicant Employer against the Respondent Union.

 

Issue 6 – What is the outcome of the August 13, 2013 Application?

 

Given the foregoing findings and conclusions of the Board, the Board now has before it the August 13, 2013 Application which requires determination.  In reviewing any application for certification, the Board must consider the following criteria:

 

·         The status of the parties

·         The appropriateness of the bargaining unit

·         The composition of the unit

·         Whether there is a  majority of employees in that appropriate unit supporting the certification of the Applicant Employer as their agent for collective bargaining purposes

·         Whether there are grounds for granting the relief sought

 

Upon a thorough review of the August 13, 2013 Application, the Board is satisfied that the appropriate documentation was filed to engender the Board with jurisdiction to hear the matter pursuant to Regulation 3.

 

The Board has carefully reviewed the membership evidence accompanying the August 13, 2013 Application and concludes that there is a clear majority of the Applicant Employer’s employees who wish to be represented by the Respondent Union for collective bargaining purposes.

 

Accordingly, the Board is satisfied that the unit is appropriate for collective bargaining and that a majority of employees of the Applicant Employer wish the Respondent Union to be certified to represent them for collective bargaining purposes.  The Board finds that all requisite grounds exist to empower the Board to permit the August 13, 2013 Application and to grant the Certification Order requested. 

 

The Board therefore certifies the International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers, Local 1432 as the exclusive certified bargaining agent for all employees of H-Line Enterprises employed in Prince Edward Island as journeyman lineman, apprentices, arborist and apprentice arborist/labourer and all other employees involved in the line construction for the Province of PEI, excluding office personnel.  A Certification Order shall issue to this effect and the Board so rules.

 

Given the manner and the background in which the August 13, 2013 Application has come before the Board and in which the Order for Certification will issue, the Board wishes to make clear for the parties that any concerns the Applicant Employer may have with respect to the August 13, 2013 Application and the Order for Certification that will issue pursuant to this decision may be addressed by way of Reply pursuant to Section 54(6) of the Act.

 

Conclusion

 

In summary, the Board responds to the issues in this matter in the following manner:

 

1.      What is the status of the May 24, 2013 Application for Certification and the resulting Certification Order that issued from the Application?

 

The May 24, 2013 Application is dismissed due to lack of evidence of majority support.  The Certification Order dated May 28, 2013 that issued from the May 24, 2013 Application is revoked and rescinded, ab initio.

 

2.      What is the status of the August 13, 2013 Application for Certification?

 

Given the dismissal of the May 24, 2013 Application and the revocation of the May 28, 2013 Certification Order, and the Board’s determination that neither a statutory nor discretionary time bar are appropriate or necessary remedies in the matter, the Board finds no reason that the August 13, 2013 Application cannot now proceed, which has, until now, been held in abeyance.

 

3.      Should the Applicant Employer be permitted to amend its Application for review of the Certification Order to plead that the Applications for Certification were incorrectly brought pursuant to section 54 of the Act?

 

Yes.

 

4.      If the Board grants the Applicant Employer’s request to amend its Application for review of the Certification Order as per number 3 above, were the Applications for Certification in the proper form by being brought pursuant to section 54 of the Act?

 

Yes.  The Board finds that the Applicant Employer is an employer who operates a business in the construction industry.

 

 

 

5.      Has the Applicant Employer been denied procedural fairness or been prejudiced in any manner as a result of the Board’s process in this matter?

 

No.

 

6.      What is the outcome of the August 13, 2013 Application?

 

The Board grants the August 13, 2013 Application and certifies the International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers, Local 1432 as the exclusive certified bargaining agent for all employees of H-Line Enterprises employed in Prince Edward Island as journeyman lineman, apprentices, arborist and apprentice arborist/labourer and all other employees involved in the line construction for the Province of PEI, excluding office personnel.  A Certification Order shall so issue.
